On the crest of the St Francis Dam, forty-five miles north of Los Angeles, a clockwork drum turned slowly under a pen. The pen was moved by a float in a twelve-inch pipe fixed to the upstream face, and it drew the height of the water on a sheet of squared paper that was changed once a week. From Monday 5 March 1928 the line it drew was straight. The reservoir stood at 1,834.75 feet above the sea, a quarter of a foot below the lip of the spillway, three inches from full, and it stayed there, through the wind of the weekend that threw water over the lip, through Monday morning when the chief engineer came to look at a leak, through Monday evening when the last cars going home to the power house up the canyon came up the road past the east end and saw nothing wrong. At about half past eleven on the night of Monday 12 March, by the clock on the drum, the line began to fall. It fell slowly at first, then faster, and when it had fallen about three-tenths of a foot the pen went down the paper almost vertically and did not come back.

The chart was found afterwards, because the piece of the dam it stood on was the one piece left standing. The commission that read it put the first fall at "about 11.30 p.m. (corrected time)" and the end at midnight, and warned that the clock could not be trusted to the minute. What the clock meant was argued over for a year, and is still. Some read the fall as water: a leak under the dam, growing for half an hour before the end. Some read it later as the dam itself, lifting at the heel so that the float in its pipe went down against a reservoir that had not moved. Either way the pen had been writing for five days, and what it wrote was that the St Francis reservoir, for the first time since it was built, was full.

## Who ran it

William Mulholland was seventy-two in March 1928 and had been in charge of the water of Los Angeles for forty-two years. He was born in Belfast in 1855, went to sea at fifteen, came to California in 1877 and to the city the year after, and was hired as a *zanjero*, a ditch tender, by the private Los Angeles City Water Company. He read engineering at night. In 1886 he was its superintendent, and when the city bought the company in 1902 he came with it as chief engineer of what became the Bureau of Water Works and Supply. In 1904 he began the surveys for the aqueduct from the Owens River, 233 miles away across the desert, and in 1913 he opened it. He had built, he told the coroner's jury, nineteen dams. All but two were of earth or rock fill. The two of concrete were the dam at Weid Canyon above Hollywood, finished in 1924 and renamed Mulholland Dam at its dedication in March 1925, and the one in San Francisquito Canyon.

Above him sat the Board of Water and Power Commissioners of the city, businessmen and lawyers. Its president, Reginaldo F. del Valle, an attorney, had sat on it for twenty years. Asked at the inquest what the board had done about the St Francis site and plans, he said that after the permit from the government "this matter has been left entirely in the hands of Mr. Mulholland", and that no member of the board, since the project began, had been an engineer: "None at all." Asked whether the board had ever told its chief engineer to bring in geologists or engineers from outside, he said that the board "has had the utmost confidence, and has now, in his ability as an engineer", and left the detail to him. Below Mulholland were the men of the bureau: his assistant Harvey Van Norman, who had come back from building the city's outfall sewer in August 1926 to find the dam thirty feet from its top; the office engineers W. W. Hurlbut and Edgar Bayley, who had the drawings and the computations; Stanley Dunham, the superintendent who built it; William Lindsey, the general foreman who dug the trenches; and Tony Harnischfeger, the keeper, who lived in a cottage a quarter of a mile below the dam with his six-year-old son and a woman named Leona Johnson, and who had worked for Van Norman, Van Norman said, for nearly fifteen years.

The state had nothing to do with it. California's law of 1917, passed the year after a dam went out above San Diego, required the plans of dams to be approved by the state engineer, and exempted municipal corporations with an engineering department. ("[Four Billion Gallons](/experiments/2026/10/19/four-billion-gallons.html)" tells the story of that earlier dam.) Mulholland asked the state engineer, Wilbur McClure, to look at the site anyway. "Didn't spend but a half day," he said, "and he saw all there was to see in a half day, because there wasn't much to see."

Below the dam, San Francisquito Creek ran nine miles south to the Santa Clara River, and the river ran forty-three and a half miles west past Castaic, Piru, Fillmore, Santa Paula, Saticoy and Montalvo to the sea. A mile and a half below the dam was Power House No. 2 of the city's Bureau of Power and Light, with the families of its operators in cottages beside it. Farther down were ranches, among them Chester Smith's; a construction camp of the Southern California Edison Company at Kemp, with about a hundred and forty men in tents; the lemon and orange orchards of the valley; and the towns. The ranchers of the river had been in dispute with Los Angeles since 1926 over the creek's water, which the city held back, and had hired a San Francisco engineer, Carl Grunsky, to say so.

## What they were trying to find out

The St Francis Dam was built to hold water, not to test anything. But a dam is a hypothesis about rock, and this one had been written down in an unusual way, so that it is possible to say exactly what was assumed and what was not.

The first assumption was that a section of concrete that had been calculated for one canyon would serve in another. The coroner put it to Mulholland in his second appearance before the jury: the jury had learned that morning from Bayley and Hurlbut that the design "was an adaptation of the dam originally designed for Hollywood, and, with some changes, the general scheme was applied for the St. Francis Dam". "Yes sir," Mulholland said, "very little difference." Hurlbut, pressed by the deputy district attorney to say who had designed the St Francis Dam, answered that it "was designed under the instructions of the Chief Engineer and based on studies which were made for a gravity type dam, which was the Hollywood Dam". The computations, he said, were the Hollywood computations, and "there were no changes in the design to fit the different conditions". The rock at Hollywood was sandstone. The rock in San Francisquito Canyon was a mica schist on the east side and the stream bed, and a red conglomerate on the west, meeting along an old fault that ran up the canyon under the dam.

The second assumption was about that rock. The schist, Mulholland told the jury, was "a hard igneous mica schist"; the word, he said, "means solid". The conglomerate on the west was "a solid indurated conglomerate", altered by heat and pressure, and he had tested it himself. "I ordered the holes in, was very much interested in their behavior, put a big hole at the top, filled it full of water, and it was there about two weeks, and the conglomerate was no softer than it was afterwards, had to bail the hole out, the conglomerate was very tight." He had tunnelled into the ridge west of the dam "to see if I could make a dam out of it", and concluded that "if I could make a dam as good as that, I would be through making a dam, that is permanent". Dunham, asked what tests were made of the foundation before the dam was begun, answered: "None made at all." Ten holes were drilled in the stream bed, but they were for drainage, not exploration, and no log of them could be found.

The third assumption was added while the dam was going up. The design of July 1923 was for a dam 175 feet above the creek, holding 30,000 acre-feet. In July 1924 ten feet were added to the height, for 32,000 acre-feet, and a dike was run westward along the ridge to hold the extra water. In July 1925, after eleven months of pouring, another ten feet were added, for 38,168 acre-feet. The base was not widened. A gravity dam is held in place by its own weight, and twenty feet of water on a base designed for a lower dam is a question put to the base; the engineer J. David Rogers, who went back to the bureau's drawings sixty years later, found no record that the question was ever worked. Mulholland's own answer, when the coroner asked what factor of safety the dam had, was "About three or four."

Underneath all three assumptions was a rule for reading leaks, and Mulholland stated it on the first day of the inquest. Muddy water from under a dam "is taken as a very bad characteristic of a leak through the earth anywhere. It means the earth is cutting." Clear water meant the earth was not cutting. Every dam leaks, he said; the bureau measured the leakage of this one daily over a V-notch weir, and "of all the dams I have built and of all the dams I have ever seen, it was the driest dam of its size I ever saw in my life."

So the experiment, as it stood on 5 March 1928, was this. A section proved on sandstone had been set on schist and conglomerate across a fault, raised twenty feet, and filled by degrees: a third full in 1926; to within three feet of the lip in May 1927, and down again; and now to within three inches, and held. The instruments were the weir, the register and the keeper's eye. The reading that would mean danger was mud.

## What they built

<figure>
  <img src="/static/img/three-inches/completed-dam-1926.jpg" alt="An oblique view from a hillside downstream of a stepped concrete dam curving across a canyon, with the spillway openings along its crest, a long dike running along the ridge to the right, and the full reservoir and bare hills behind">
  <figcaption>The completed dam and reservoir from the hills below, with the wing dike running off along the ridge to the right. Plate 11 of the Governor's commission's report, 1928.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Governor's commission described it from the bureau's own drawings. It was "of the solid gravity type, curved on a radius of 500 feet to the upstream face at the crest": a curved wall of concrete whose stability was reckoned from its weight alone, the arch being counted as a margin and not as a support. The crest was 16 feet thick; the greatest section was 205 feet high and 175 feet thick at the base, by the drawing the bureau supplied; the main dam was 700 feet long along the curve, and from its west end a wing wall ran a further 500 feet along the ridge. The downstream face rose in steps five feet high. The spillway was eleven openings in the parapet, each twenty feet wide and a foot and a half high, with the lip at 1,835 feet; five outlet pipes of thirty inches ran through the base, controlled by gates on the upstream face. The reservoir held 38,000 acre-feet, about twelve billion gallons.

Construction began in April 1924 and the dam was finished on 4 May 1926; the concrete was poured in sixteen months by the bureau's own men, since, as Mulholland said, "we don't let contracts". The aggregate was the gravel of the creek bed a quarter to half a mile below, "neither washed nor graded". In the stream bed a trench was taken to about thirty feet below the gravel and a concrete wall built across it as a cofferdam, later swallowed into the heel of the dam. On the east the concrete was notched into the schist and carried up the slope of the canyon wall "without steps, and with no cut-off wall". On the west a trench about three feet wide and three feet deep was cut along part of the abutment with picks and gads. Lindsey, who dug it, said the men went in four feet where the rock was hard, because to go deeper they would have had to blast, and "Didn't want to shoot it, shake up the rocks." Ten holes were bored in the stream bed under the middle of the dam, fifteen to thirty feet deep, and piped to a common outlet on the downstream face, to let out water that might press upward under the concrete; the water that came from them was piped down to the keeper's cottage, "because it was sweet and clear". There was no drainage under the two sloping abutments, no cut-off wall, no inspection gallery, no grouting of the rock, and no contraction joints, because the bureau's practice was to let the concrete crack where it would and caulk the cracks afterwards. "No inspection gallery was carried through the dam," the commission wrote, "nor was any pressure grouting attempted under any part of the structure."

It cracked. Four cracks ran through the body of the dam from the crest, two of them, about fifty-eight feet east and west of the outlet gates, marking out what would become the standing section. Mulholland and Van Norman judged them the ordinary shrinkage of a mass of concrete, and in the winter of 1927 to 1928 the bureau's men stuffed them with oakum and hemp rope on the downstream face and pumped cement grout in behind, to stop the water that was being lost through them.

Storage began on 1 March 1926, two months before the dam was finished. The water reached 1,779 feet, 13,200 acre-feet, in June 1926, and was held through the summer and lowered in the autumn. From January 1927 it was raised to 1,832 feet by 10 May, three feet below the lip, held for seventeen days, and let down to 1,813 by November. From 1 January 1928 it rose steadily. By 5 March it stood at 1,834.75 feet, three inches below the lip, and no more aqueduct water was turned in. As it rose, the leaks grew. Seepage had appeared on both sides of the main dam and along the slope below the long dike as soon as storage began; the dike's leak became so troublesome that a line of drain tile was buried along its downstream face. In the first week of March a new leak came out at the foot of the dike about 150 feet west of the main dam, and an eight-inch concrete pipe was laid to carry it along the base of the dike and let it out beside the west abutment. On the Saturday before the end, Chester Smith, driving up the canyon to mend a fence, stopped his car and looked across at "considerable water coming out on the west bank", and said to Jack Ely, the keeper's assistant, as a joke, "what are you sons of guns going to do here, going to flood us out down below?" Ely answered in the same way: "We expect this dam to go out any minute." Smith told him, "I believe you had better turn some of that water out, it don't look good to me."

The rock itself had been described by the people who read rock. The commission's two geologists found "a fairly uniform mica schist" under the stream bed and the east slope, "strongly sheared" along planes that lay nearly parallel with the slope, so that "under such stresses the schist would slip like a pack of cards thrown upon a table". On the west, above the fault, was the conglomerate: small pebbles in a matrix of sandy, micaceous, flaky detritus, cut by seams of clay and veins of gypsum, "so minutely crushed as to have lost most of the strength to be expected in a rock possessing its general appearance". It was hard when dry. "When, however, a piece of the rock is placed in water, a startling change takes place. Absorption proceeds rapidly, air bubbles are given off, flakes and particles begin to fall from the sides of the immersed piece, the water becomes turbid with suspended clay and, usually in from fifteen minutes to an hour, a piece the size of an orange has disintegrated into a deposit of loose sand and small fragments, covered by muddy water." The commissioners did not need a laboratory to find this out. They found it out in March 1928, after the dam was gone, with a glass of water.

## Clear as glass

On Monday 12 March 1928 the reservoir had stood three inches below the lip for a week. The weekend's north wind had thrown water over the spillway and down the stepped face, so that men looking at the dam from below, Smith among them, saw water running where it had not run before. Down the canyon, water that the bureau had let into the creek for the first time in two years ran past Power House No. 2 and over the road at Harry Berry's house, and the men there cleared the culverts. At Power House No. 2 David Matthews had noticed on Saturday "an extra amount of water coming down the spillway", and had gone up with Berry and stood on the east end of the dam for ten or fifteen minutes "talking about the condition of the leak there", and had thought, he said afterwards, that "that hill was all soaked".

That morning Harnischfeger telephoned the bureau. There was a new leak, on the west side, and the water was muddy. Van Norman was in the room next to Mulholland's and heard him say he was going out to San Francisquito: "It is full, and the wind has been blowing some, water coming over the spillway. I am going to look at it." Van Norman said, "I will go along with you." They drove up in the bureau's car and were at the dam by half past ten. Harnischfeger walked round with them.

"The keeper telephoned us that the water was dirty," Mulholland told the coroner nine days later, "and we went up there and the water was not dirty and had not been dirty, but it was cutting." The new leak came out of the west abutment, above the contact between the conglomerate and the schist, and ran down the hillside across the cut of an old construction road that the bureau was in the course of replacing, and it was the loose dirt of the road that it picked up. Van Norman climbed to within ten feet of the place where it came out; Mulholland, who said he "was disinclined" to climb in where Van Norman was, stayed below. "The water came out of the leak and it was as clear as glass." He put its flow at about a second-foot, a cubic foot a second, which was more than all the seepage of the dam together had been a fortnight before: on 29 February the weir had measured 7.6 miner's inches, a sixth of a second-foot, and now the whole of the seepage was something near two second-feet, "or twice the total observed at any previous time", as *Engineering News-Record* reckoned it afterwards. It was not increasing while they watched it. It was clear. "No, sir," Mulholland said, "it never occurred to me that it was in danger. I just wanted to see the new leak, naturally."

Van Norman's account of the same two hours is almost the same. "Everything was, as far as I could see, as secure as it ever had been. There was some seepage coming from both ends of the dam, some coming from under the very base of the dam. The water in every case was crystal clear, no indication of any erosion, and there was nothing that would alarm, or, at least, did not alarm me to any extent at all. I left there with perfect confidence that the dam was perfectly all right." He added that Harnischfeger "was a man that I had the utmost confidence in, and he did in me, and if there had been anything at all there that he was apprehensive about, I am sure he would have told me". They left at about half past twelve, drove the hour and a half back to the city, found the cafeteria in their building closed, and had lunch in a restaurant on Second Street. A photograph taken that noon shows the three of them walking along the crest, with the wind-blown water on the spillway and, in the picture's left middle, the new leak coming down the west side.

Could anything have been done? The coroner asked. The five outlet pipes, all opened, Mulholland said, "could not have lowered that water more than an inch or two a day in the reservoir". If he had thought the dam was going, "I would have sent a Paul Revere alarm up and down the Valley." But he had not thought so, and neither had Van Norman, who said he would have slept in the canyon that night without "the remotest idea" of danger. The reading was clear water, and clear water was the dam being a dam.

Chester Smith, who was not an engineer, read it differently. He drove cattle up past the dam that Monday and back in the afternoon, and still saw "a big leak on the west side". "I was a little suspicious, to tell you the truth," he said, "and left my door open." He slept in the barn that night with the door open, three or four miles below the dam, and the people who worked for him slept in the house.

## What it looked like from inside

Nobody who saw the dam go lived. "It is reported that one of the caretakers was seen on top of the dam at 11 p.m.," the commission wrote, "only an hour before the failure, and apparently up to this time there had been no alarming developments. The caretakers were lost in the flood, and so far as is known there is no living witness of the dam's collapse." The last people who passed it were employees of Power House No. 1, five miles up the canyon, driving home along the road that climbed the east wall and skirted the east end of the dam about ten feet above its crest. Dean Keagy, a warehouseman, passed at about half past eleven and saw lights in the canyon bottom below the dam, which he took for a camp. Helmer Steen and Katherine Spann passed a few minutes later and told the jury they had seen nothing unusual, only the dam blacked out, as it always was. Ace Hopewell, a carpenter, came up on a motorcycle at about ten minutes to twelve. About a mile beyond the dam he stopped, because he heard a rumbling behind him, and he sat with the engine running and smoked a cigarette and listened, and then went on to Power House No. 1, where he found out what it had been.

At Power House No. 1 the operator, Ray Silvey, had spoken on the telephone with the operator at Power House No. 2 at 11.47 pm, and everything there was normal. At 11.57 and a half, by the records of the Southern California Edison Company and of the city's own receiving stations, the Borel transmission line, which ran down the canyon on poles about ninety feet above the east end of the dam, went dead; the commission rounded it to 11.58, and took it as the moment of failure. At 12.03 am the power from Power House No. 2, a mile and a half below the dam, went off. In the five minutes between, the water had come down the canyon at eighteen miles an hour, more than a hundred feet deep.

At Power House No. 2 the operators' families were asleep in their cottages. Ray Rising, a general utility man, woke to a roar and a wet mist in the air and got out; his wife Julia, who was twenty-nine, and their three daughters did not. Lillian Curtis's husband woke to the same mist and put her and their three-year-old son up the slope behind the house before the water took him and the other two children. Of the hundred and twenty or so people living at the power house, Rising, Lillian Curtis and her son were almost the only ones to live. The power house itself, the commission found, was "completely carried away" down to the floor the generators stood on. Harnischfeger, his son and Leona Johnson were among the first dead; her body was found between two blocks of the dam, dressed, as if she had been up and out of the house when it came.

Three or four miles down, Chester Smith's dog barked. "I ran when I heard that noise, and the dog awakened me; I could hear it coming, hear the trees breaking, and could hear a big pole snapping, could hear the wires on the electric poles going. I knew what was coming, because I had been in a flood once before." He ran a hundred and fifty feet to the house where Hugh Nichols and his wife were sleeping and shouted "The dam is broke", and the three of them ran a hundred and fifty feet to the hill with Mrs Nichols between them, "and as we ran up the hill, the water was right behind us". Afterwards people asked him why he had not run to warn the others. "It was impossible." He put the water at his place at a hundred and twenty-five feet high. The family that had moved onto his ranch to work for him died in it.

The flood came out of the canyon into the Santa Clara valley at about twelve miles an hour. At 12.38 am it took out the Edison substation near Saugus, and at 1.20 am it reached the Edison construction camp at Kemp, sixteen and a half miles below the dam, where "more than 80 out of about 140 perished". It reached the bridge at Fillmore at 2.25, Santa Paula at 3.10, Saticoy at 4.15 and the Montalvo bridge, four miles from the sea, at five. By then it was two miles wide and moving at about five miles an hour. The commission reckoned the peak below the dam at more than half a million cubic feet a second. "The magnitude and violence of the wave released on the failure of this dam," it wrote, "are hard to visualize even by engineers familiar with floods and flood conditions." It had occurred in darkness, and "very few of the persons in the constricted valley below the dam escaped with their lives, though they were immediately adjacent to the safety of the steep slopes of the bordering hills".

Mulholland and Van Norman were woken and driven up to the canyon in the night. "The first time was in the dead of night," Mulholland said later of his two visits to the wreck. "I was up there the night of the tragedy, Mr. Van Norman and myself, had no chance to observe the details."

## What happened

<figure>
  <img src="/static/img/three-inches/standing-section-1928.jpg" alt="A view down into a scoured canyon with a single tall stepped block of concrete standing on the right, bare eroded hillsides on both sides, a broken wing wall on the left skyline and a stream running through the wreckage">
  <figcaption>The dam site from downstream after the failure: the standing section, the stripped abutments, the end of the wing wall on the left. Plate 22 of the Governor's commission's report, 1928.</figcaption>
</figure>

Daylight showed what was left. Of the 700 feet of the main dam, one piece about 100 feet long stood in its place in the middle of the canyon, the piece that carried the outlet gates and the register, with its two ends sheared off. On each side of it was a gap about 300 feet wide. The gap on the west, where the conglomerate had been, held no concrete at all; the blocks from that side, some of them weighing ten thousand tons, lay half a mile and more down the canyon. The gap on the east was choked with blocks that had fallen almost where they stood, and the schist wall above it was sliding in a continuous rain of fragments that was still coming down when the commission visited ten days later. Both abutments had been scoured away to depths of fifteen to sixty feet below the surfaces on which the concrete had been poured. Water still ran out of the seams of the conglomerate and along the top of the clay gouge on the fault. A triangulation from the old survey stations found that the standing section had moved seven-tenths of a foot, which the commission read as some five and a half inches downstream and six inches towards the east.

The dead were counted for weeks. The Associated Press had 243 bodies recovered on the morning of 14 March, with "from 300 to 600" unaccounted for. The commission, writing on 24 March, had "known dead at this time is 236 and 200 are still missing". *Engineering News-Record* put the dead and missing at 450 on 25 March and at 426 in May. The county's official figure, in August, was 385. Later counts, which go on being revised as remains are found and names matched, put it at 431 or more, and the state's marker at the site says over 450. Nobody knows. The commission's phrase for the valley was that the damage "will certainly be many millions of dollars"; the city's engineers put it at ten million, and the lemon groves of the Santa Clara under a strip of silt and gravel one to three miles wide.

Mulholland said on 13 March that seepage had caused part of the hill on the west side to crumble, and that there had been no earthquake; the seismographs at Pasadena and at Georgetown agreed that there had not. Half a dozen inquiries were sitting within the week. Governor C. C. Young's commission, four engineers and two geologists under A. J. Wiley of Boise, met in Los Angeles on 19 March, went to the canyon, and signed its report on 24 March. The district attorney's board of three engineers and two geologists under Edward Mayberry reported on 4 April. The city council's board under Elwood Mead, the federal Commissioner of Reclamation, reported a few days later. The water board's three geologists reported. The coroner's jury, nine men of whom six were engineers or contractors, sat from 21 March to 10 April and gave its verdict on 12 April. And in June a report for the valley's water district by the Grunskys and the Stanford geologist Bailey Willis said something the others had not. The companion piece, "[Six reports](/deeper/three-inches/six-reports/)", goes through them.

They agreed on the rock. "The failure of St. Francis Dam was due to defective foundations," the Governor's commission concluded, in its first sentence; the conglomerate, "when wet became so soft that most of it lost almost all rock characteristics", and "with such a formation, the ultimate failure of this dam was inevitable, unless water could have been kept from reaching the foundation". The commission thought the west end had gone first, at or near the fault, by a blowout or a settling in the softened conglomerate, and that the rush of water had then cut away the schist under the east end. The district attorney's board said that "it was not economically feasible to build a safe dam at the site chosen". Mead's board put it in numbers: pieces of the conglomerate that broke at 600 to 1,900 pounds to the square inch when dry broke at 210 and 845 after ten minutes in water, and samples soaked twenty-four hours "disintegrated into fine particles and sand grains and therefore could not be tested"; it found that the seepage had gone from 7.6 miner's inches on 29 February to about 50 on 12 March, and that "Mr. Mulholland was positive that all seepage streams were clear where they emerged below the dam". The coroner's jury listed ten defects, among them that the dam "was built upon two formations meeting at a fault contact", "was not carried far enough into the bedrock and had no cutoff walls", and had drains only in the centre; and it found that the leakage "was remarkably small, considering the critical condition existing under the dam, and less than is commonly present in dams that are considered safe and have stood for years". "The only reasonable explanation," it wrote, "is that those in charge were completely deceived as to the true conditions and acted through ignorance and not from intent to omit any precaution from any motive."

Then it said where the fault lay. "The responsibility for the error in engineering judgment rests upon the Bureau of Water Works and Supply and the Chief Engineer thereof." The deeper error, it went on, was one of public policy, and belonged to the commissioners, the legislatures of city and state and the public, who had let "excessive responsibility to be delegated to or assumed by any one individual". "A sound policy of public safety and business and engineering judgment demands that the construction and operation of a great dam should never be left to the sole judgment of one man, no matter how eminent, without check by independent expert authority, for no one is free from error, and checking by independent experts will eliminate the effect of human error and insure safety." It found no criminal act or intent, and recommended no prosecution.

Mulholland had said much the same on the stand, where he had wept. "I have a deep appreciation of the job before you, gentlemen. Don't imagine for a minute that I would throw you off the scent. I am willing to take my medicine like a man." On the first day, asked whether he would build the dam again, he had said "I will answer that frankly and say, I would not", and then, pressed, "Not in the same place", and then, "Well, it fell this time and there is a hoodoo on it, that would be enough." He had a suspicion of what had caused it, he said, and "I don't want to divulge it"; the dynamiting of the aqueduct by Owens Valley men in 1924 and 1927 was in everyone's mind, and every board that considered it set it aside. On his second day he closed with the sentence that has been quoted ever since. "Whether it is good or bad, don't blame anybody else, you just fasten it on me. If there is an error of human judgment, I was the human. I won't try to fasten it on anybody else." The companion piece, "[On the stand](/deeper/three-inches/on-the-stand/)", follows the two days.

One finding went further than the rock, and it concerned the clear water. On 10 May *Engineering News-Record* printed an analysis that S. B. Morris of the Pasadena water department had made of a sample of the seepage coming out of the west bank just after the break, beside a sample of aqueduct water from the surge tank at Power House No. 2. The seepage carried ten times the chlorides and five times the dissolved solids of the water that had gone into the reservoir, and was "heavily charged with calcium and magnesium sulphates": it had been dissolving gypsum out of the veins in the conglomerate. The water board's geologists had found that seepage through the conglomerate had averaged not less than two miner's inches since storage began. At that rate, the journal worked out, water that looked clean could have carried away 97 tons, or 1,343 cubic feet, of the rock in the fifteen months the reservoir had been in service. "This experience is a warning that clear seepage is not necessarily harmless; many minerals are soluble, and only a chemical test will show what clear water really contains." The test that would have shown danger, the editorial said, was not the one the engineers had used. "There has been a widespread tendency to look for muddy seepage as the danger sign, and in the absence of turbidity, or of large quantity of flow, to feel no anxiety about leakage through or under dams."

Willis, in June, moved the question from the west side to the east. The east abutment, he wrote, "was located on a landslide. It was an old slide which had become inactive and was modified by erosion and vegetation, but it is recognizable in the topographic forms of the hillside. When it had become soaked by the water in the reservoir standing against its lower portion, it became active and moved." The slide, he and the Grunskys argued, had lifted the east wing, tilted the whole dam, and let the water under the west; the proof was a ladder from the upstream face found crushed in a crack at the base of the standing section, which could only have been swallowed if the block had lifted and settled back. The Governor's commission had looked at the slides on the east and concluded that "all of the recent landslides in the vicinity of the dam site took place after and as a result of the failure of the dam and were in no way responsible for such failure". The two readings have never been reconciled by anyone who was there.

## After

The city did not argue. Van Norman, speaking for it to the relief committees, said: "The city built this dam to provide storage which it was obliged to have. No question of the legal status of claims should ever be raised; the moral obligation to repay damage in the valley is sufficient." The council voted a million dollars on 20 March, and within forty-eight hours of an agreement with the contractors' association 1,300 men and a million and a half dollars' worth of machinery were in the valley clearing silt off the orchards, every tract signed for against the owner's claim. The aqueduct, burst in three places below Power House No. 2 by the head of the flood, was running again by 24 March.

On 14 November 1928 Mulholland handed the commissioners his resignation, to take effect on 1 December. He was seventy-three, and, as the journal reported it, "he desires to rest and to leave duties of his position to younger men of the organization". Van Norman succeeded him. The commissioners kept him as a consultant. He died at home on 22 July 1935, after a stroke, at seventy-nine. Van Norman told the papers: "Bill Mulholland was rough. He was tough. But we loved him."

The state did what the jury and the commission had asked. On the proposal of the state engineer, Edward Hyatt, and the president of the Railroad Commission, the legislature of 1929 passed Chapter 766 of its statutes, which took effect on 14 August 1929 and put every dam in California that was not federal under the state engineer: existing dams to be examined and approved or repaired, new dams to have their plans approved and their construction supervised, all dams to have their maintenance watched. The exemption for cities was gone. Before the act, the state's own account says, its supervision "was limited in scope and extended to less than half of the dams in the state". The same session set up a board to license civil engineers. The first dam the new law stopped was the county's San Gabriel dam at the Forks, in October 1929, when a state board that included George Louderback, who had sat on the St Francis commission, found that its west abutment was an old landslide.

Hollywood came next. A resident of the district sued in 1928 to have the reservoir behind Mulholland Dam drained, on the ground that it, "like the St. Francis dam, has been built without a cutoff wall at the heel, without inspection galleries, contraction joints or sufficient drainage pipes". Mulholland had already had the reservoir lowered. State and city boards examined the dam in 1930 and 1931 and found that, with no relief of the water pressure under it, the line of its thrust fell outside the middle third of its base; from 1932 to 1934 the city buried its downstream face under 300,000 cubic yards of earth, and the reservoir has been kept low since.

The standing section stood for a year. Engineers came from everywhere to see it, and then the curious came, and in May 1929 the city bored two tunnels fifty feet below its base, put in five tons of high explosive, dug a pit in the stream bed in front of it, and toppled it in. It was 160 feet high, 90 feet wide and 100 feet thick at the base, about 45,000 tons of concrete, and burying it cost $15,000. "The structure itself is now gone," *Engineering News-Record* wrote under the heading "Requiescat". "The lessons to be learned from it will stand forever."

The reading of the wreck went on without the wreck. Charles Outland, a Santa Paula historian who had grown up with the flood, published in 1963 the first account to notice that the lowest steps in the section the bureau had given the commission did not appear in the construction photographs, and that the dam had been raised twenty feet on the same base. From 1992 Rogers, a geological engineer, took the question up with the inquest transcript, the bureau's archive and the geology. He found that the east abutment had been built against an ancient landslide in the schist, as Willis had said; that the blocks found farthest downstream had come from the east side, not the west; that the register's pipe on the standing section had been bent towards the east while the reservoir was still nearly full; that the dam's real base width was about 148 feet, not 175; and that "no site-specific rational design methodology was actually performed", only the transfer of the Hollywood section. His account of the last night is that the east abutment began to slide, and the dam, losing its support on that side and overloaded by twenty feet of extra water on a base that had never been recalculated, lifted at its heel and tilted downstream; the three and a half inches the register recorded in its last forty minutes was not water leaving the lake but the float's pipe going down with the dam. "The dam was LIFTING," he wrote, "as opposed to the reservoir dropping just prior to the failure." The historians Norris Hundley and Donald Jackson, who went through the same archive, accepted much of the geology and argued, against Rogers's reading, that the dam had fallen short of the engineering practice of its own day. The argument is open. What is not in dispute is the sentence Rogers put at the head of it: "The dam collapsed 10 days after the reservoir's initial filling, to within three inches of the spillway sill."

In 2019 Congress made the site a national monument. The reservoir is a canyon again, with a stream in it.

## What it taught

The St Francis Dam was not run as an experiment, and that is the first thing it teaches, because it was one. A hypothesis about rock had been written in concrete, raised twice without being re-examined, and filled by stages over two years while its instruments reported; and the men who read them had decided, before the test began, what a bad result would look like. The lesson is in the reading.

**An instrument answers the question it was built to answer.** The register on the crest measured the height of a float in a pipe, and the commission read its last half-hour as water leaving the reservoir, as it was built to be read. Sixty years later the same trace was read as the dam lifting under the float. The V-weir measured the volume of the seepage, and the volume was small; the keeper's eye measured its colour, and the colour was clear. None of the three measured what was being carried out in solution, which was the quantity that mattered, and the one instrument that could have, a chemist's flask, was never put to the water until the dam was gone. "Only a chemical test will show what clear water really contains." The engineers at Vajont, thirty-five years later, had a model that answered the question of the wave and could not answer the question of the slide ("[Seven Hundred Metres](/experiments/2026/10/21/seven-hundred-metres.html)"); the engineers at San Francisquito had a weir that answered the question of volume and could not answer the question of what the volume was dissolving.

**A result carried from one trial to another is not a result.** The section had been computed for sandstone at Hollywood, with a coefficient of friction taken from a book, and it was set on schist and conglomerate "with minor modifications, by the Chief Engineer", and nobody recomputed it when the dam was raised ten feet, or when it was raised ten more. The jury's finding that the chief engineer and his assistants "had had limited experience in the building of large masonry or concrete dams" is exact: seventeen of his nineteen dams were earth, which sits on yielding ground, and a gravity dam cannot. What had been proved at Hollywood, if anything had, was proved for Hollywood.

**The dangerous reading is the one that agrees with you.** Mulholland's rule, that mud means cutting and clear water does not, was true as far as it went, and it was the only rule he took to the dam on 12 March. Clear water confirmed the dam; a leak that doubled the seepage in a fortnight, that came out above the fault, that surged, and that the keeper had thought muddy enough to telephone about, was explained by the road cut and the wind. Chester Smith, who had no rule, left his door open. The difference between them was not knowledge. It was that one of them had a hypothesis to protect.

**One judgement is one judgement.** The jury's sentence about "the sole judgment of one man, no matter how eminent" has been quoted in the writing on dam safety ever since, and it is right, but it should be read with the other half of its finding, that the fault was "a logical result of a set of conditions that the citizenship has allowed to develop and continue". Del Valle's board had no engineer on it and did not think it needed one; the state's law exempted the city; the state engineer gave the site half a day. Mulholland had, by his own account, asked for that half-day because "there ought to be state inspection of such things, whether it is a municipality or not". He was not checked because everyone, including the law, had decided he did not need to be, and he had built nineteen dams that stood.

**Check the story before you learn from it.** The height of the dam is 185 feet in the newspapers, 195 above the creek by the drawings, 205 and 208 to the bottom of the trench; the base is 175 feet to the commission and 148 to Rogers; the dead are 236 and 200 missing, 385, 426, 431, 450; the west end went first to five boards in 1928 and the east end first to Willis in June and to the geologists since. What survives every version is the shape: a design borrowed, a base not widened, a rock that turned to mud in a glass of water, a reservoir held three inches from full for the first time, a leak read as clear, and a line on a chart that ran level for five days and then went down. The lesson is not that Mulholland was careless. He went to the dam himself the morning it was reported, and he took the blame alone. It is that a man who has built nineteen dams and is told the water is clear will believe the water, and that an experiment must be designed so that someone else is reading the instrument.
