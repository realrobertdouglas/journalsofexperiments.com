At a quarter to six on the evening of the first day, three of the five had their tanks on in the wet room and were ready to go out, and there was a problem with the pingers. Each diver had to carry a small sonic beacon in case he got lost, and only two of the five were working. The one the record calls No. 4 looked disgusted. He spoke to the watch director over the intercom and got leave to carry a beacon with a broken switch in his hand, so that three could go out as planned, and at 5:55 he was the first in the water. On a hillside above Great Lameshur Bay, on the south coast of St John in the United States Virgin Islands, in the summer of 1970, a psychology student watching a television screen in a van wrote "No. 4 is the first in the water," and the time, and went back to his cards.

He had a card to punch every six minutes. It recorded, for each of the five people in the steel cylinders fifty feet below the van, where they were, what they were doing, whether they were talking and to whom, how they were standing or sitting, and how much they were moving. He punched it round the clock, in pairs, in four-hour shifts, for 182 days. By the time the habitat came up in November the cards had recorded between four and six thousand observations on each of several dozen measures for each of forty-eight people. The programme that produced them had been announced to the public as a feat of living on the sea floor, and the public read it that way. The people who had paid for it were after the cards.

## Who ran it

The idea came out of a corridor. In November 1966 the National Aeronautics and Space Administration held a symposium on isolation and confinement, and in a side conversation two sets of psychologists, one from NASA and one from the Office of Naval Research, agreed that a crew living for weeks in a habitat on the sea floor looked very much like a crew on a long flight in space, and that the sea was the cheaper place to watch one. The Navy's men had just done it. In August and September 1965, in Sealab II off La Jolla, twenty-eight men had lived at 205 feet in three teams, and two psychologists, Roland Radloff of the Naval Medical Research Institute and Robert Helmreich, had watched them through closed-circuit television and written a book about it, *Groups Under Stress*, which came out in 1968. The Navy's next habitat, Sealab III, was going to 600 feet, and the Navy's interest in it was not science.

NASA paid for two study contracts in June 1967 to ask whether undersea crews could stand in for space crews, and the answer that came back was that they could, provided the crew had real work to do. The Department of the Interior, whose Bureau of Commercial Fisheries and Geological Survey employed marine scientists who wanted to live on the bottom for their own reasons, joined in November 1967. In December the General Electric Company's Missile and Space Division at Valley Forge, Pennsylvania, sent the Office of Naval Research an unsolicited proposal for a sixty-day mission, and offered to build the habitat largely with its own research money. The Navy would be lead agency. The name came from the small glassy stones that fall from space and come to rest on the sea floor.

The four who went down were all from the Interior Department. Richard Waller, the crew chief, was an oceanographer of the Bureau of Commercial Fisheries and the only professionally qualified diver among them. Conrad Mahnken was an oceanographer of the same bureau, John VanDerwalker a fishery biologist, and Edward Clifton a geologist of the Geological Survey. They were 35, 34, 32 and 31, all married, three of them with children, all raised in the Depression, all first-born sons, all once Boy Scouts; the behavioural programme's interviewers wrote all this down before they went in, and added that "these men are easy to know, to like, and to respect." The behavioural programme itself was Radloff's, run from Bethesda with Richard Mach and Nicholas Zill of Bellcomm, the company that did NASA's systems analysis, and with six Navy seamen apprentices as observers. The medical programme was the Navy's and the University of Pennsylvania's. The Office of Naval Research, whose Denzil Pauli had edited the Sealab II report and would edit this one, held the overall management.

Tektite II, the next year, was the Interior Department's, with James W. Miller as programme manager, Waller as operations director and VanDerwalker coordinating the science; the Smithsonian chose the scientists from their research proposals; and the behaviour programme went to Helmreich, now at the University of Texas at Austin, with Radloff beside him and NASA's Stanley Deutsch chairing the committee over both. The final volume lists eleven government agencies, more than thirty universities and institutes, and seven companies among the participants. "TEKTITE II was not a line item in any budget plan," the final report says, which is why the generators were borrowed.

## What they were trying to find out

The summary report lists three missions and says that "since the three missions are interdependent, equal emphasis had to be placed on each." The first was "to study the behavior and effectiveness of a small group of highly trained men to real work under stressed, isolated conditions." The second was medical: whether men could live for two months saturated with nitrogen at a little over two atmospheres, breathing a mixture of 92 percent nitrogen and 8 percent oxygen that no habitat had used before, instead of the helium the Navy used deep. The third was that the men being studied under the first two should do real marine science while it was being done to them.

Under the first mission was a hypothesis, and the summary report states it: "that behavioral, habitability, and crew effectiveness data obtained in observations of undersea teams could be used to predict and understand similar problems involving space teams." It is two claims. One is that a crew can be observed closely enough, for long enough, that the observations will show what makes one crew work better than another, and that the thing shown can be measured before the crew is chosen. The other is that what is true of four marine biologists under fifty feet of warm water will be true of astronauts. Radloff thought the second claim was true only if nobody tried to make the habitat look like a spacecraft, and he said so at length afterwards.

The Interior Department's question was plainer. Marine scientists had wanted for years to stay on the bottom instead of making short dives from the surface; a habitat where the men's tissues were saturated with gas at the pressure of the water would let them go out as often as their tanks allowed and pay for it with one long decompression at the end. Was that a research tool, or a stunt?

## What they built

The habitat was two white steel cylinders, each 12.5 feet across and 18.1 feet high, standing on a rectangular base and joined by a tunnel four and a half feet wide. Each cylinder had two rooms. On one side the bridge, with the controls and the dry laboratory, stood over the crew quarters, with four bunks, a galley, a radio and a television set; on the other the engine room, with the air-conditioning, the frozen food and the toilet, stood over the wet room, where a hatch in the floor opened on to the sea, held out by the pressure inside. A cupola on the engine room and six hemispherical ports looked out on the reef. Ballasted, the whole thing weighed 310,000 pounds. General Electric built it at Valley Forge; it was assembled for the first time at the Philadelphia Naval Shipyard on a Navy pontoon barge, loaded into the well of the landing ship *Hermitage* on 8 January 1969, floated out at Lameshur Bay, and winched to the bottom at 49 feet, where the hatch, six feet above the sand, set the saturation depth at 43. Compressed air came down an umbilical from a barge jacked up on pilings at the nearest point of shore, and nitrogen was bled in until the oxygen stood at 8 percent. The carbon dioxide the men breathed out was taken up by Baralyme, the granular chemical of a submarine's scrubber.

Beside the shore, Navy Seabees built a camp of thirteen wooden huts and an aluminium building for about a hundred people, thirty-five of them scientists, with two 10,000-gallon water tanks, and the government of the Virgin Islands sent a water barge once a week. On the support barge stood an air-conditioned van divided by a folding partition. One side was the watch director's station, where a watch officer and a medical officer sat at a control panel with two television monitors, the intercom and the habitat alarms. The other side was the behavioural station, and it was the reason the whole thing existed.

Each of the four rooms in the habitat had a closed-circuit television camera and an open microphone. On the behavioural side of the partition six monitors showed the four rooms and two cameras outside, with audio and video tape recorders and, at the back, a rack of equipment that recorded on paper tape whenever anyone pressed the "in" or "out" button on the dive panel in the wet room, switched on the stove, the radio or the television, or plugged a headphone into the socket at his bunk. In front of the monitors sat two of the six observers, seamen apprentices straight from training at Orlando, Florida, chosen for a classification test score in the top tenth of the population, "a clerical interest, and strong motivation toward and interest in the project." They had arrived two and a half days before the mission started and learned the job from a forty-four-page manual. Each observer, the manual told him, "was asked to consider himself the scorer of a ball game."

What he scored went on to a punched card. The behavioural station had been given a small plastic device called an information recorder, in which a template printed with the categories of behaviour was laid over a partly perforated IBM card, so that the observer could punch a record of where each man was and what he was doing without writing a word. Every fifteen minutes, at a moment chosen at random so that the men could not learn the rhythm, one observer recorded the location, activity and communication of all four aquanauts; the other kept the records of meals, transits between rooms, conversations and talk with topside, and started the tape recorder when the men at the lunch table began to talk. The cards were flown to Washington once a week, run through programmes already written, and the summaries flown back. Observation ran from a quarter past six in the morning to a quarter to midnight, seventeen and a half hours, which covered about 97 percent of the men's waking time; the observers had asked for a boat to take them off the barge after midnight, when the men were still up and generating data, and had been refused. "For the ubiquitous and convenient reason of 'safety' this request was unacceptable to the command," Radloff wrote in the final report. "Such a lack of appreciation for what doing science involves interfered with the on-going scientific endeavor."

Two of the four men also slept with electrodes on their scalps, sending six channels of brain, eye and heart signals up a thousand feet of cable, and a third wore an electrode cap NASA had made for astronauts. All four filled in a mood checklist every evening, and on Wednesdays they gave blood and breathed into the doctors' instruments, which Clifton and his colleagues later reckoned took between a tenth and a fifth of their waking hours.

A year later, for Tektite II, the van was moved on to the hillside above Beehive Cove and the behavioural side was staffed by three teams of eight, ten and eight students from the University of Texas, all of them qualified divers who also served as the aquanauts' support divers, "which served to build rapport between the psychological mission and the Aquanauts." They had six 18-inch monitors, one to each room, one for the water outside and one spare, three video recorders, three loudspeakers and two headphone jacks. Observation now ran twenty-four hours a day, in pairs, in four-hour watches, and the status record was punched for each aquanaut every six minutes. A second team of observers, from a NASA contractor, watched the evenings of one mission and wrote down everything that happened in plain words.

## Sixty days

The four went into the habitat at eleven in the morning on 15 February 1969. Thirty-six hours later the carbon dioxide stood at 10.2 torr, five times the design figure, because the scrubber did not work as it had in tests; the men took the carbon dioxide fire extinguishers out of the habitat, rigged a makeshift scrubber and for two weeks changed the Baralyme every four hours, round the clock, standing three-hour watches at night as submariners do. In the first third of the mission the men spent two and a half hours a day each on maintenance, and the behavioural record shows the whole crew resting and sleeping more than an American on an average day. All four got ear infections from a bacterium called pseudomonas, and the man the cards call aquanaut 1 was kept out of the water for twelve days by the doctors; the graph of time in the water dips in the middle third and rises again, and the graph of time in the crew quarters does the opposite. After recovery, the appendix says, "the crew entered the water with renewed vigor."

On 17 March the Vice-President sent a message to mark the halfway point, and about then the four passed Scott Carpenter's thirty days in Sealab II, which was the record. Towards the end individual men were spending five hours a day in the water, ranging more than 1,800 feet from the habitat on twin tanks, tracking lobsters fitted with acoustic tags by night and mapping the bottom by day. They logged 432 man-hours outside, 7.2 a day among the four. At four in the morning on 15 April they left the habitat for the transfer capsule, and at half past four decompression began, and it ended on 16 April, about twenty hours later, within three minutes, the summary report says, "of a time scheduled many months previously." The post-dive examinations found nothing wrong except a small cloudiness in the lens of Clifton's right eye, off the axis of vision, which the ophthalmologists could barely find again a few months later, and the sleep records showed the men sleeping longer and deeper as the weeks went on. The President's telegram, dated 16 April, said: "Your record breaking venture into inner space is another milestone in human achievements. The aquanauts join the astronauts as space pioneers. Congratulations!"

The Office of Naval Research's own list of what Tektite I had contributed put at its head "the most sophisticated program for studying human behavior ever attempted in any operational environment." The programme had made, by the summary's count, "over 400,000 individual observations"; the appendix says half a million. In Washington, Radloff and his colleagues began to find out what they said.

What they said first was that the habitat had been a committee's work. "The camel has been described as a horse designed by a committee," Radloff wrote in the appendix. "In some respects Project Tektite I was such a creature." At one stage the proposal had called for a crew of one marine scientist, one scientist's assistant who was also an astronaut trainee, a habitat engineer and a physician, to resemble a space crew; Radloff had fought it, on the ground that an undersea habitat is an analogue of space and not a simulation of it. There had been suggestions that the habitat should go without resupply from the surface to increase the feeling of isolation, until someone worked out how many dry towels and how much Baralyme that would mean. "The proper posture for a behavioral program in a natural environment," he wrote, "is piggyback not monkey-on-the-back." The men themselves had taken the cameras well. There were hand-lettered signs held up to the lens in the first days, and good-natured references to the "shrinks" thereafter; the television was switched off on six occasions, for privacy, and the microphones fifty-four times, and the appendix is careful about why. "Crew members wanted to discuss in critical vein their reactions to topside events without being overheard by the persons who were subject of the discussion."

The second thing the cards said was where the men had been. The pair that dived together most talked together most, 53 minutes and 49 minutes on an average day; the pair that dived together least, 5 minutes, talked least, 16. The pairs did not settle until the third week, and then held to the end. One man took the best working space on the bridge by leaving the dinner table before the others, night after night, until in the middle weeks he spent as much time there as the other three combined, and nobody objected. The men worked, by the cards, about as much as an American man at an office, but they did their own housekeeping as well, and they did not take weekends off.

## Mission 6-50

Tektite II ran the same habitat for ten scientific missions between 4 April and 6 November 1970, with a crew of four scientists and one engineer each time; an eleventh crew of photographers and General Electric engineers spent the last six days in it. Fifty-three people went down. Six of the missions were twenty days and four were fourteen, and the two sets of three twenty-day missions were run as sixty-day experiments in command: an engineer stayed thirty days while two crews of scientists passed through under him, was replaced by a second engineer, and the command of the habitat moved from engineer to scientist and back by a plan written before anyone dived. The scientists were chosen by a Smithsonian board on the merit of their research proposals and grouped so that their programmes fitted, with no psychological screening beyond a check for gross disorder. "No use was made of available predictive measures," Helmreich wrote, because the programme wanted the widest possible range of people to predict.

One mission was all women, and it was the one the newspapers came for. Mission 6-50 went down on 6 July 1970 and came up on 20 July. Its leader was Sylvia Earle of the Los Angeles County Museum of Natural History, a botanist; with her were Renate True of Tulane, Ann Hartline of the University of California and Alina Szmant of the Scripps Institution of Oceanography, and the engineer, Margaret Ann Lucas, from the University of Delaware. Earle's project was the plants, and her report in the final volume opens with a sentence that could stand for the whole programme: "Perhaps the most striking aspect of plant life on a coral reef is the general lack of it." In fourteen days she counted 154 species of marine plants in the bay, twenty-six of them never before reported from the Virgin Islands, and thirty-five species of plant-eating fishes in fourteen families, and used wire cages set along two 180-metre lines to show that where the grazing fishes were abundant the plants grew only inside the cages.

The cards counted something else. Compared with the forty-three men, the five women worked significantly more of their mission time, spent more of it on marine science and less on leisure, and did not differ in sleep, in maintenance of the habitat or in care of themselves. Helmreich set the finding down and then set about it. "The fact that the females measured significantly higher than males on the Work and Marine Science criteria could be an indication of the natural superiority of women or of differential motivation. Consideration of social psychological factors suggests the latter interpretation although causal forces cannot be extracted from the data." The one women's mission among ten had been screened harder than the men's; it had been talked of, by programme staff and the scientific community alike, as the test of whether women could take part in undersea and space exploration at all; the support divers had been keener to help it; and the press coverage "far exceeded that accorded any other mission." On every background measure the women looked like the men, except that they were younger, 27 on average against 33, had had higher grades at school and fewer fights, and had been less likely to work in the summers. "None of the factors cited above reflect any discredit on the performance of the female mission," he wrote, "which was excellent on an absolute basis; they are cited to illustrate the importance of motivational factors." The NASA contractor's habitability team put it more shortly: the women "showed positive adaptation to the habitat in all respects, but there were not enough of them to analyze these results statistically." Forty years later VanDerwalker, who had coordinated the science, told a gathering on St John that in work accomplished the women's team had been the most successful of the lot.

## What it looked like from inside

The plain-words record is the NASA contractor's volume of transcribed evenings, published in 1972 with the crew reduced to numbers and "some off-color words" replaced by milder ones. It was restricted to professional researchers, and it is the nearest thing the programme left to a diary.

On the first evening No. 1 put on a Beatles cassette, then a Mozart violin concerto, then Vivaldi, and arranged his gear on his bunk while the others dived; later he spent four minutes looking for jam. No. 3 handed baskets of specimens up through the well. A diver's strobe went off by accident and topside asked for the pinger receiver to be turned up until nobody could hear anything else. At 7:55 No. 5, who had been watching the tarpon that came past the cupola every night, said: "Those things go by like blimps." At 8:18 the carbon dioxide was rising and No. 4 was told from the surface to change the Baralyme; he found there were no threads on the bolt, and the change took him, with help, until twenty to ten, while supper went cold and No. 3 played the ukulele on the bridge. They ate at a quarter to ten, quietly. At eleven No. 4 handed out the habitability forms, and they filled them in. At five to midnight No. 1 turned off the lights in the crew quarters, took the tape recorder to the wet room and showered in the dark, and No. 4 worked on in the wet room by the light from the engine room until the observers stopped at 12:35.

On the fourth evening No. 1 argued against "the motel concept for isolated research habitats," which the observer called "a good hard discussion about what the habitat concept is all about." One evening No. 4 asked what was for supper and got a beer, and the observer noted that alcohol was not allowed on board "but a very few cans of beer have found their way somehow to the habitat." Another evening No. 1 offered rum and Coke or a martini, of which they had neither, and No. 3 said, "Topside can't believe we're not on alcohol," and No. 1 took the camera off its mount and turned it on each of the others in turn and proposed skits: "I'm serious, we'll call ourselves the Tektite II players." On the eleventh day No. 1 said, "I'm going to go up and party, No. 4. I can't stand it any more," hugged No. 2 hard, and then said what the observers wrote down: "I don't know how those guys could have stood it for 60 days on Tektite I. I would have gone bananas, and they didn't even dive that much, either. NASA is running out of cassettes."

What they said about the surface was sharper. One afternoon the mission director came down with press photographers and No. 1 was angry because he could not play music while they were there; that evening he heard a rumour was going round the base camp that the crew were drinking. Late one night he told No. 4 that the project had no single line of leadership: "One group puts in some money, then wants to call some shots, and those guys up there let them get away with it." Deutsch, summarising for NASA, found this where he expected it: "intra-crew hostilities were generally surpressed or directed against the topside personnel," and the aquanauts "felt a strong need to escape into the water, but they always felt that the habitat was home and security."

The surface had its own view. The base camp's social centre was a shed of packing crates called Hurricane Hole, where the drink list offered everything and the drink served was always rum. The decompression barge had been towed over from St Thomas behind a leased tug whose captain was sure it was going to Tortola, and a leak in its seal was fixed at three in the morning before the first mission by shimming the capsule's track with half-dollar coins. In seven months there were two emergencies. At the end of the first mission a small motor burned out in a power surge as the crew were leaving. The other was a hurricane alert, on which one crew was brought up and decompressed early; within an hour or two of their reaching the surface the storm had veered west, and they were sent back down "with hardly a chance for a cigarette or whatever else they craved the most." One of the topside crowd, writing the chapter on base camp life, quoted the letters that came afterwards: "A necktie is very like a noose, isn't it?"

## What happened

Helmreich's report, finished in March 1971 before the final volume, is a preliminary cut at the cards, and it says so. The observers' reliability had been checked by having an independent judge recode the videotapes; across all variables the agreement ran between .89 and .97, and "Don't Know" was punched in less than half of one percent of records. The average aquanaut worked 8.04 hours a day and the scientists among them spent 7.15 hours a day on marine science, "far more undersea work than could have been achieved through operations from the surface." Leisure came mostly as talk at and after meals; the games went unused.

The first finding was the size of the differences. "Scientists on the highest team spent 39.4% of their time in Marine Science work (average 9.4 hours per day) while those on the lowest averaged 21.1% (average 5.1 hours per day)." That is four hours and eighteen minutes a day, team against team, in the same habitat on the same reef. Mission length did not explain it; the two sixty-day sequences did not differ; storms and breakdowns had not been adjusted for and could only have blurred it. What explained it, as far as the cards could tell, was that each crew set itself a level of work in its first days and kept to it. "In TEKTITE, no transmission of expectancy from program personnel was planned or undertaken," because the point was to see what norms crews set when nobody set them, and the answer was that they set very different ones. The three teams that worked most were led by scientists and the three that worked least by engineers, whose job was the habitat and not the reef; where an engineer joined in the science, his team did better. The teams in which engineers helped with science and scientists helped with maintenance did more marine science than the teams in which they did not, by an hour and a half a day. Those who talked most with their crewmates worked most: the correlation between gregariousness and marine science was .59. Those who talked most with topside also worked most, which was the opposite of Sealab II's finding and, Helmreich thought, a matter of what had been counted. Replacing the engineer in mid-mission did not change the work; it dropped the crew's talk from 33.8 percent of the time to 28.5, where crews that kept their engineer rose from 28.3 to 30.1. The frozen meals NASA supplied saved the long missions the time of cooking, and the time saved went into leisure, not work.

Two things the cards found about measurement itself. The debriefings showed that "self-reports of performance elicited after an experience are not valid indices of the actual behavior exhibited. Frequently, those Aquanauts expressing most pride in their achievements rated among the lower performers, while those expressing personal dissatisfaction with personal performance were high on objective indices of performance." And the habitability questionnaires showed that the aquanauts liked the carpets, the air-conditioning and the shower, rated access to news "lower than poor," and rated the habitat worst of all as a place to do the work they had come for. "The habitat is better designed for goals of comfort than for goals of research," the contractor concluded; of forty complaints about food, thirty-nine came from the seven missions given NASA's frozen menus rather than the three allowed to order from the supermarket on St Thomas.

The second finding was Radloff's questionnaire. He had argued since Sealab that the best predictor of future behaviour is past behaviour, and for Tektite II he wrote a Life History Questionnaire of thirty-two questions, each answered once for every year of the first nineteen of a life. Against the criterion of marine science time, with forty scientists, nine of its variables together gave a multiple correlation of .79, which is to say they accounted for 62 percent of the variance; the ten best performers had been in worse health between the ages of six and twelve than the ten worst, which the authors christened "the Teddy Roosevelt Effect." "An extremely effective predictive test has been developed," Helmreich concluded, "which needs only refinement and validation." What it asked, what it found and what became of it are in [*The questionnaire*](/deeper/every-six-minutes/the-questionnaire/).

The Minitat, a smaller habitat meant to run seven missions at 100 feet on 95 percent nitrogen, never carried a crew in the sea; the tables for it were proved in a fourteen-day chamber dive at the University of Pennsylvania in June and July. The last crew came up on 6 November 1970, the landing ship took the habitat back to Philadelphia on 21 November, and the programme office closed in May 1971. Of 915 man-days in the habitat, the medical director reported, thirteen man-days of diving had been lost to illness.

## After

The habitat went into storage at General Electric in Philadelphia. It was sold for a dollar to a group who trucked it across the country to San Francisco, where it stood at Fort Mason as a museum that schoolchildren walked through, was certified for the water again and renamed Tektite III, and never went in; it was moved to the Oakland estuary in 1984 and cut up by welding students in 1991.

Helmreich's cards went to Austin. His report listed sixteen topics for later analysis, from territoriality to the content of the videotapes, and ended on them: "A large archive of highly reliable data on real behavior has been amassed in which researchers can test important theoretical hypotheses for some time." A few of the analyses came: a paper with Roger Bakeman in 1975 on cohesiveness and performance, whose subtitle, "Covariation and causality in an undersea environment," says what it was asking. Then the money for habitats went where the money for Sealab III had gone. "This accident and budgetary constraints of the post-Vietnam years brought underwater exploration to an almost complete standstill," Helmreich wrote in 1986, when NASA, planning a space station, asked him to say what the sea had taught. "After years of neglect, interest in psychological research in undersea habitats as representative, confined environments has recently reawakened." The lesson he drew from Sealab and Tektite together was that the laboratory had got isolation wrong. Pairs of men shut in a room with food and games had broken down and quit; men in a cold habitat at 205 feet, and men and women in a warm one at 43, had not, and the difference was that the habitat had rewards as well as costs, and real work. Of the predictors, he said this: "Few significant and replicated findings regarding personality influences in contained environments have emerged." By then Helmreich had turned to the crews of airliners; the paper's references are to jet transport pilots and the training of cockpit crews, and the finding that a crew's output is set by the norms it brings aboard went with him.

Earle, the best known of the fifty-three today, has spent her life since in the water. Clifton's and VanDerwalker's reef studies, and the work of the fifty who followed them, are in the 634 pages of *Scientists-in-the-Sea*. The habitability transcript, with its five numbered people, is on a NASA server, still marked "No reproduction or reprinting of this material is allowed."

## What it taught

Start with what it was not. It was not a failure of the apparatus or of the men; the habitat ran about 250 days in two years without an injury, and the 1,150-odd man-days inside it produced the first body of reef science done from the bottom. It was not a case of watching for nothing; the cards did find things, and the things they found were not the things the programme had expected.

**An instrument that can see everything sees what it was built to count.** The cards could record where a scientist was every six minutes and whether she was in the water. They could not record what she found there. Deutsch admitted it in a sentence: "Scientific performance is always difficult to quantify. In this study immediate performance criteria were substituted for ultimate criteria." Hours in the water stood in for discoveries, and the whole apparatus of prediction was then aimed at hours. Earle's 154 species were counted by Earle. A programme that had wanted to know what makes a scientific crew productive measured instead what makes a crew busy, and the two may be the same, but nobody on the hillside could tell.

**Two experiments in one vessel will each take the other for noise.** The scientists wrote in *Science* that the isolation imposed for the behavioural programme had cost them contact with the surface team that would have helped the science, and that the psychologists' tests took a tenth to a fifth of their waking hours. The psychologists wrote that the project's other purposes had threatened to turn their natural group into a simulation, "a Heisenberg effect in spades!" Both were right. The habitat's great advantage as a laboratory, that the subjects had real work and real reasons to be there, was the same fact that made them resist being subjects, and the committee that owned the habitat had no rule for whose experiment gave way.

**Subjects find the instrument's blind spot and use it.** The men adjusted to the cameras in days. They switched off the microphones fifty-four times, almost always to talk about the people on the surface, and they kept the beer out of the frame. The observers knew, and wrote it down, and counted it as evidence that the microphone is more intrusive than the lens. It is also evidence that what a crew most needs to say in confinement is the one thing the record was least able to hear, and that the record of crew-and-topside relations, which Helmreich later called the most important area for further research, was the part the crew edited.

**The number everyone read was not the finding.** Sixty days was the headline, the telegram and the record, and it was the least informative number in the programme; the medical result was that nothing happened. The finding was four hours and eighteen minutes a day between the best crew and the worst, set in the first days by a norm nobody had asked anyone to set, and the recommendation that followed was the plainest in the report: tell a crew what is expected of it. Sixty was a number the public could hold. The difference between crews was a number the sponsors had paid for and did not use, because the next programme was cancelled.

**With forty subjects and fifty predictors you will find predictors.** The Life History Questionnaire accounted for 62 percent of the variance in forty scientists' work with nine variables chosen from a pool for the purpose, and its authors, who knew the arithmetic, called it preliminary and shrank the figure to .71 themselves. Fifteen years on the same investigator reported that few findings about personality in confined groups had ever been replicated. The honest sequel is the part worth keeping: that a predictor found in one crew of forty is a hypothesis, and that the habitat which could have tested it was cut up for scrap.

**Check the story before you learn from it.** The four men were in the habitat for 58 days and 17 hours, from 11 a.m. on 15 February to 4 a.m. on 15 April, and the programme called it sixty, which counted the decompression; some accounts say fifty-eight, some fifty-nine. The habitat sat at 49 feet and the men were saturated at 43, which the summary report gives once as 42, and the Navy's magazine and most retellings say fifty. Tektite II had eleven crews and ten scientific missions, fifty-three aquanauts by the final volume, forty-eight in the behaviour programme and fifty-four in one commemorative account. The observations on Tektite I were four hundred thousand in the summary and five hundred thousand in the appendix. The women's team was "the most successful of the bunch" in one man's memory forty years on, and significantly busier by the cards, and its leader's report does not mention either. What the record supports without argument is a card every six minutes for 182 days, the width of the gap between crews, a questionnaire, and a programme that ended before anyone could use what it had found.
