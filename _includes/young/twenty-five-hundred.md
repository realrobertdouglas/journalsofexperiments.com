## The count

On the morning of Saturday July 24th 1915, a big white excursion steamer called the Eastland was tied to a wharf on the Chicago River, in the middle of the city, taking on passengers for a picnic. Three government men stood at the gangplank. Two of them held counting machines, small hand registers that clicked once for every person who walked past. Their job was to make sure that no more than 2,500 passengers got on, because 2,500 was the number written on the ship's certificate.

When the count reached 2,450 the men moved up the plank so that the crowd could only come through one at a time. Then they counted the last fifty. The very last were two women and a small girl. The man in charge, Robert McCreary, was worried that the girl had been hidden behind one of the women, so he asked, "Mr. Lobdell, is this little girl counted?" The inspector said, "Yes, Mr. McCreary; she is counted." That made the 2,500. The plank was pulled in.

Even a baby counted as one, McCreary explained later. The count was exact. About twenty minutes later the Eastland rolled over onto her side, still tied to the wharf, in twenty feet of water, and more than eight hundred of the people who had been counted were dead.

## The ship

The Eastland had been built in 1903 at Port Huron, Michigan, for a company that carried fruit and passengers between the town of South Haven and Chicago. The owners wanted a very fast ship, twenty miles an hour, and one that did not sit deep in the water, because the river at South Haven was shallow. She was 265 feet long, 38 feet wide, with three steel decks, a hundred cabins and a dining room the whole width of the ship.

A ship that is tall and narrow and does not sit deep in the water is easy to tip, the way a tall glass is easier to knock over than a short one. The Eastland's builders knew this. Their answer was water. Under her floor she had tanks, five on each side, and when they were filled with water from the lake the weight low down held her steady. Her chief engineer in 1904 explained how it was done: fill the big tank under the boilers, number 3, right to the top, every morning before any passengers came aboard. Number 3 held about 250 tons of water and took half an hour to fill. A tank only half full, he said, was as bad as an empty one, because the water would slosh to the low side and hold the ship over.

The trouble was that the water took time to put in, and the ship was in a hurry. All the water came in through one valve, on one side, low down in the engine room, and there was no gauge to show how much was in any tank.

The Eastland leaned over many times in her life. On a hot Sunday evening in July 1904, leaving South Haven with most of the water pumped out so she could get alongside the shallow dock, and with about 1,200 people standing on the top deck for the breeze, she leaned over about twenty degrees. The government inspector who was aboard said afterwards, "I was a little bit scared or afraid myself." A newspaper reported that the crowd would not come down from the top deck "until the fire hose was turned on them." The ship was straightened by filling her tanks. After that she went to Cleveland, on Lake Erie, and leaned there too. People said she was "cranky." In 1910 her owners put an advertisement in a Cleveland newspaper offering $5,000 to anyone who could prove she was not safe. Nobody claimed it. In 1913 a naval architect, John Devereux York, rode on her and wrote to the harbour master that unless her faults were fixed there would be a "serious accident."

## The number

In America in 1915 a passenger ship was inspected once a year by two government men, one for the hull and one for the boilers. They decided how many passengers she could carry. They measured the decks. They counted the life preservers, the lifeboats and the life rafts. They watched the ship with people on her. But they did not measure the one thing that decides whether a ship will tip over, which is called her stability, because the law did not ask them to and they did not know how. One inspector was asked if he could work it out for the ships in his district. "It takes too long a time," he said.

The ship's builder said in court the next year that there had never been a stability test of the Eastland at all. A professor who had been asked about her shape before she was built said he had designed it for 1,000 passengers, with a small margin of safety, about eighteen inches. By 1915 the certificate said 2,500.

Here is how it got to 2,500. In 1914 the ship had been allowed 2,045 passengers. In May 1915 the two inspectors, Robert Reid and Charles Eckliff, wrote to her captain, Harry Pedersen, that she could have 2,500 if she carried enough life preservers, lifeboats and rafts for that number. The rule was: boats and rafts with room for thirty per cent of everyone aboard. On June 7th the captain swore a statement that he had ordered four new rafts, each for twenty-five people, and asked for the full number "as soon as these rafts are installed." On July 2nd he telephoned to say the rafts were on board, and wrote a letter listing their numbers. The same day the inspectors issued a new certificate: boats and rafts for 776 people, life preservers for 2,570, and so "2,570 persons, viz, twenty-five hundred passengers and crew of seventy."

Nobody went to look at the rafts first. "Did you examine the boat to find out whether or not that equipment was on before you granted the increase?" Reid was asked afterwards. "We did not." He went to look two days later and found them there. Four rafts, a hundred seats, 250 more passengers. The chief engineer of the Eastland, Joseph Erickson, was Reid's son-in-law. Had they ever talked about the limit? "No, sir."

A naval architect called William Wood put the problem in one sentence at the inquest: "You can't increase the carrying capacity of any steamboat by adding to the life-saving appliance." Lifeboats are for after a ship fails. They do nothing to stop her failing. And every boat and raft went up on the top decks of a ship that was already too tall for her width.

This was the thinking of the whole country just then. Three years earlier the Titanic had sunk with lifeboats for half her people, and in March 1915 Congress had passed a law, the Seamen's Act, saying that a ship must have boat and raft room for everyone aboard. The idea was good. But it taught everyone to think that the way to make a ship safe was to count her boats.

## The morning

The Western Electric Company's workers from the big Hawthorne works outside Chicago had bought about 7,000 tickets for their picnic across the lake at Michigan City, Indiana. Five steamers were hired. The Eastland was to load first and leave by a quarter past eight, so that she could get back for her own afternoon trip.

She had come to the wharf at four in the morning. At half past six the passengers began to come up the plank, about fifty a minute. In the engine room, at about the same time, a nineteen-year-old oiler named Peter Erickson was told to open the valves and pump out tanks 2 and 3 on both sides. They always did that in the mornings in Chicago, he said. So the ship was loading with her tanks empty.

At about a quarter past seven, with about 1,700 people aboard, she leaned over towards the wharf so far that the plank rose and the crowd had to be held back. The engineers let water into the tanks on the river side to pull her straight. Then she leaned the other way, towards the river. The harbour master, Adam Weckler, came down to the wharf at ten past seven and saw her leaning five or six degrees. He shouted up to the captain to fill his tanks and straighten her. "He said he was trimming all the time," Weckler remembered. The harbour master told him, "I won't give you the bridge until you right her," and would not let the ropes be cast off.

Down below, the chief engineer sent the oiler to open the valves to the tanks on the wharf side, about seventeen minutes before the end. The ship leaned a little more, then stopped. The engineer said, "Well, I guess we've got her." Another engineer remembered the words as "I think you've caught her now, all right," and added, "but we hadn't." All the water for the tanks on the high side had to come in through the single valve on the low side, and up through the pipes, and it was slow.

On the top deck seven hundred to a thousand people were standing. It had started to rain. A band was playing. At about twenty-five past seven the mate, Adelbert Fisher, saw her lean further than ever and then straighten up a little, and thought she would come back the way she always had. He tripped over a raincoat on the deck. When he looked up the captain was shouting to the harbour master, and then she went. Water poured in through the open doors in her side and the round windows of the crew's quarters, which were only about two feet above the river. The one rope still holding her pulled its post clean out of the wharf. She lay down on her side in the water, with her upper side sticking out like the back of a whale.

The people on the top deck were mostly thrown into the river or climbed onto the hull. The people inside, in the cabins and the dining room and the bar, where the first newspaper report thought five hundred had gone to get out of the rain, had no chance at all. Fisher grabbed a girl who was holding his arm and slid down the side of the ship with her. The inspector Lobdell jumped into the water with his counter still in his pocket. "How much time did they have to save themselves?" the harbour master was asked. "None at all."

## What happened next

Tugs and men from the wharves pulled people out of the river. Firemen cut holes in the steel with torches to reach the people trapped inside. The dead were taken to an armoury and laid in rows, and all night families walked past them looking for their own. The first reports said a thousand; the count settled over the following weeks at 844, including twenty-two whole families. It was the worst disaster in the history of the Great Lakes, and it happened in a river in the middle of a city, with the ship tied up.

Everybody looked for someone to blame. The county coroner's jury met the same day and sat for a week. It said that the captain, the chief engineer, the owner's manager, the man who hired the ship and the two inspectors should be charged with manslaughter. But it also said something more important: that the government had never had any way of testing whether a passenger ship was stable, and that "the present method of determining the passenger-carrying capacity of vessels is not founded on any proper basis."

The Secretary of Commerce, William Redfield, whose department the inspectors belonged to, came from Washington and held his own inquiry for a week. He could not find out who was to blame, because a federal judge had taken the main witnesses for his own grand jury. But he recommended new laws: that naval architects should check the plans of every passenger ship before she was built, and that any ship whose stability was in doubt should be given an "inclining test," a real measurement with weights.

Two grand juries charged the owners, the captain, the engineer and the inspectors with crimes. In January 1916 a federal judge in Grand Rapids, Michigan, heard the evidence for a month. The ship's builder was made to testify and admitted there had never been a stability test. On February 18th 1916 the judge said the government had not made out a case, and refused to send the men to Chicago for trial. Nobody was ever tried.

The families of the dead sued the owners. The owners used an old law from 1851 that says a shipowner only has to pay up to the value of the ship, if the loss happened without his knowledge. The wreck was raised and sold for $46,000, and most of that went to the company that raised her. The case dragged on for twenty years. In 1935 a court of appeals agreed with the judge below that the Eastland had been "seaworthy in every respect" and that the fault was the engineer's, for not handling the tanks properly. By then, the court said, the money was "exhausted."

The Navy bought the hull, renamed her Wilmette, and used her as a training ship on the lakes until 1945. She was sold for scrap in 1946. The wharf is still there.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**You can measure something exactly and still measure the wrong thing.** The count at the gangway was perfect. Three men, two machines, a child checked twice, not one person over 2,500. But the number that mattered was never measured at all: how far the ship could lean before she would not come back, with all those people on her upper decks and no water in her tanks. Nobody had ever tested it. The only test the Eastland ever had was 2,500 people walking aboard.

**Lifeboats do not make a ship safe. They only help after she has failed.** The owners added four rafts and got permission for 250 more passengers. The rafts, the boats and the life preservers all went on the top decks, which made the ship a little more top-heavy, not less. Everyone was counting seats in boats, because of the Titanic, and nobody was thinking about weight.

**When something keeps leaning, that is a reading.** The Eastland leaned in 1904, in Cleveland, in 1913, in a frozen canal in 1914, and on the morning of July 24th 1915. Every time, the people in charge decided that the man at the valve had been too slow, and told him to be quicker next time. They never asked whether a ship that depended on a man being quick with a valve was a safe ship.

**If the safety of something depends on one person doing one thing every morning, write it down.** The engineer who knew the ship in 1904 kept her steady by filling tank number 3 right to the top before anyone came aboard. He left. Nobody wrote the rule on the wall. Eleven years later a new engineer, a new assistant who had been aboard two days, and a nineteen-year-old oiler pumped her empty in the harbour, because that was what they did.

**Inquiries find the people the law lets them see.** The law said accidents were caused by the "incompetency or misconduct" of the ship's officers, so the inquiries looked for an officer, and the courts found the engineer. He had made mistakes. But finding him left the ship's design, the rafts and the way the number was set exactly where they were.

So the lesson is simple. Before you trust a number, ask what it measures. The Eastland's 2,500 measured life preservers and rafts, and the people who read it thought it measured safety.
