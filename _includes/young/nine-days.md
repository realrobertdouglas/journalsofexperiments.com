## The river, December 8th 1903

Late in the afternoon of December 8th 1903, on the Potomac River just below Washington, a tug was holding a big houseboat steady in a gusty wind. It was nearly dark and there was ice floating in the river. On the roof of the houseboat, on a track, stood a flying machine with four cloth wings and a petrol engine, its two propellers spinning. In a little canvas car between the front wings stood a young engineer called Charles Manly. He was twenty-seven. The machine had cost the United States government fifty thousand dollars and the Smithsonian Institution about twenty-three thousand more. It had never flown. Manly had never flown either. Nobody had.

Manly gave the signal. The car ran along the track. At the end of it something at the back of the machine broke, the rear wings folded, and the machine reared up until it was standing on its tail in the air. Then it fell over backwards into the river, on top of Manly.

He got out. Nine days later, on a beach in North Carolina, two brothers who made bicycles flew a machine of their own for twelve seconds, and then for fifty-nine.

## The professor

Samuel Pierpont Langley was born in 1834 near Boston. He never went to college. He taught himself engineering, became an astronomer, and ran an observatory in Pittsburgh for twenty years. In 1887 he became the Secretary of the Smithsonian Institution in Washington, which means he was in charge of it. He was one of the most respected scientists in America.

That same year he started building model flying machines. He called them aerodromes, from a Greek word meaning "air runner." The first ones were driven by rubber bands. Later ones were made of steel and driven by tiny steam engines, and they were launched from the roof of a boat on the Potomac River. For nine years they failed, one after another, and Langley wrote down every failure.

Then, on May 6th 1896, one flew. Aerodrome No. 5 weighed about twenty-four pounds, and nobody was on board. It climbed slowly round in big circles, and when its steam ran out it came gently down onto the water, a minute and a half later, without a scratch. Alexander Graham Bell, the inventor of the telephone, was there and photographed it. He wrote that nobody who had seen it could doubt "that the possibility of mechanical flight had been demonstrated."

Langley said that was the end of his part of the job. He had proved that a machine heavier than air could fly. Building one big enough to carry a person, he wrote, was something "the world may look to others" for.

## The fifty thousand dollars

The others turned out to be the army. In 1898 President McKinley wanted to know whether a flying machine could be used in war. A board of officers looked at Langley's models and said yes, and the War Department's Board of Ordnance and Fortification, which paid for new weapons, gave Langley $25,000 in November 1898 and another $25,000 a year later, to build a machine big enough to carry a man. Langley promised to keep the work private until it succeeded.

So the experiment changed. The question was no longer "can a machine fly?" It was two new questions. First: if you build the model four times bigger in every direction, will it still fly? Second: can a machine that balances itself, launched off a catapult, carry a person who has never been in the air before?

Langley hired a young engineer from Cornell University, Charles Manly, to run the work. Manly later wrote that there were two ways to build a flying machine for people. One was to start with gliders, let the pilot learn to fly by practising, and add an engine later. The other was to start with models that flew by themselves, make them bigger, and build a machine so steady that "no especial skill on the part of the operator is required." Langley chose the second way. In Dayton, Ohio, two brothers called Wilbur and Orville Wright were choosing the first.

## What they built

The hardest part was the engine. The man Langley hired to build it could not do it, and the engine makers of Europe said it was impossible. So Manly built it himself, in the Smithsonian's workshops. His engine had five cylinders arranged like the spokes of a wheel, gave fifty-two horsepower, ran for ten hours without stopping, and weighed only about two pounds for each horsepower. Nothing like it existed anywhere in the world.

The machine itself was called the Great Aerodrome. It had a frame of steel tubes and four wings, two in front and two behind, covered in cotton cloth. With Manly on board it weighed 830 pounds. There were two hand wheels for the pilot. One tilted a big tail at the back, which was supposed to keep the machine level by itself, the way the model's tail did. The other turned a small rudder for steering. There was no way to tilt the wings. If the machine leaned over, the pilot could do nothing except wait for it to right itself.

Then there was the houseboat. Langley had launched his models from the roof of a boat, over water, so that they would not be smashed when they came down, and he did the same with the big machine, although Manly advised against it. On the roof of the new houseboat was a turntable weighing fifteen tons carrying an eighty-foot launching track. A car ran along the track, pulled by springs, holding the machine. At the end the car stopped and the machine was supposed to fly off into the air.

One thing was never built: any way for Manly to practise. Langley had planned to launch the big machine first with a dummy on board, in case it crashed. But by 1903 so much time had been lost that Manly offered to go himself, and Langley agreed, the record says, "with great reluctance." There was no glider, no test flight, no hour in the air. The first time Manly left the ground would be in the real machine.

## The summer on the river

In July 1903 the houseboat was towed forty miles down the Potomac and anchored in the middle of the river. Fog hung over it for weeks. The engine's electrical parts rusted, the batteries went flat, and the damp got into the wooden ribs of the wings, so that the glue holding them together came apart. Newspaper reporters came down in boats and waited. Langley would not let them aboard.

On August 8th a quarter-size model with a small petrol engine was launched. It flew about a thousand feet in twenty-seven seconds. Langley said it was the first time in history that a flying machine with an engine had flown in public. It was not flown again. There was no time.

## October 7th 1903

On October 7th the wind dropped. At twenty past twelve Manly started the engine and gave the order to release the car. The car ran sixty feet in three seconds. "Just as the machine reached the end of the track," Manly wrote afterwards, "the writer felt a sudden shock, immediately followed by an indescribable sensation of being free in the air." Then he felt the machine plunging downward. He turned the tail wheel as far as it would go. Nothing happened. The front wings hit the water and smashed, and Manly went under the river with the machine on top of him. He pulled himself out through the wires and came up. The first thing he saw was a newspaper reporter in a rowing boat, racing to reach him first.

The reporters wanted a statement, so Manly gave them one. "The experiment was unsuccessful," he said. Then the foreman looked at the launching car and found that a small pin on the front of the machine had caught in it as the track dropped away. The catapult had pulled the front of the machine down into the water. Langley told the press the next day that the accident was "due wholly to the launching mechanism."

That did not help. The *New York Times* called it "a ridiculous fiasco," and said that a flying machine which really flew might take "from one million to ten million years." At Kitty Hawk, where he and his brother were putting their own machine together, Wilbur Wright wrote to a friend: "I see that Langley has had his fling, and failed. It seems to be our turn to throw now, and I wonder what our luck will be."

## December 8th 1903

Manly's advice was to change nothing except the little pin. By then the government's money had all been spent, and Langley would not spend any more of the Smithsonian's. If they waited for spring, there was no money to wait with. So on December 8th, when the wind dropped at noon, they went. By the time a tug had been found and the houseboat towed into position it was almost dark, the wind had come up again, and the river was full of ice. Manly wrote that it was "a case of 'now or never.'" Hundreds of people watched from the wharves.

At a quarter to five he gave the signal. He felt the machine sway, then "a tremendous jerk which caused the machine to quiver all over," and then the nose was going up and up. He turned the tail wheel the other way. Nothing happened. The machine stood on its tail in the air, hung there for a moment, and fell backwards into the river.

Under the water, Manly's cork jacket caught on the frame. He could not go down, and the floor of the car was pressing on his head so that he could not go up. He tore the jacket in half, dived under the machine, swam clear, and came up under a block of ice. In the dark the tug's crew tried to pull the wreck in by its tail and broke the frame in two.

Nobody ever found out exactly what went wrong. The foreman saw the tail drop and drag on the track just before the end. The army officer watching for the Board heard "a crashing, rending sound" and saw the rear wings collapse, "just how, it was impossible for me to see." Langley's people said the catapult was at fault again. Others said the wings were too weak and braced in the wrong place. The only test that could have settled it was another launch, and there was no money for one.

## Nine days later

On December 17th 1903, at Kill Devil Hills in North Carolina, Wilbur and Orville Wright flew their machine four times in a cold wind, with five men watching. The first flight lasted twelve seconds and the last fifty-nine. Their machine ran forty feet along a wooden rail, lifted off, and flew into the wind under its own power.

The Wrights had done something Langley never did. For three years, before they ever put an engine on anything, they had gone to the sand dunes of North Carolina and flown gliders, hundreds of times. Wilbur had explained why in a speech in Chicago in 1901. The wings and the engine, he said, were already more or less solved. What nobody had solved was how to balance and steer a machine in the air, and the reason was that nobody had ever practised. The great German glider pilot Otto Lilienthal, he worked out, had spent only about five hours in the air in five years. "It would not be considered at all safe for a bicycle rider to attempt to ride through a crowded city street after only five hours' practice," Wilbur said. Practice, he wrote, "is the key to the secret of flying."

In Washington nobody wanted to hear about it. In January 1904 a congressman asked the House of Representatives: "if it is going to cost us $73,000 to construct a mud duck that will not fly 50 feet, how much is it going to cost to construct a real flying machine?" In March the Board told Langley there would be no more money. Years later it admitted that it had believed the launching, not the machine, was to blame, but had stopped the work because of "the adverse opinions expressed in Congress and elsewhere."

Langley wrote one public account. Its first sentence said that the experiments had been stopped for lack of money "without the machine ever having been in the air at all." He died in 1906, aged seventy-one.

## The machine that "flew"

In 1914 the Smithsonian let an aeroplane builder called Glenn Curtiss take the old Aerodrome to his factory in New York State to try to fly it. Curtiss had just lost a court case to the Wrights for copying their invention, and if Langley's machine could be shown to have been able to fly in 1903, before the Wrights, that would help him. At his factory the machine was rebuilt, with new wings of a different shape, much stronger bracing in a different place, floats to take off from a lake, and controls of the kind the Wrights had invented. On May 28th 1914, with Curtiss flying it, it lifted off the water for about 150 feet. The longest it ever stayed up with Langley's engine was about five seconds.

The Smithsonian then announced that Langley had built "the first aeroplane capable of sustained free flight with a man," and hung the machine in the National Museum with a label saying so. Orville Wright protested. In 1921 an English friend of the Wrights, Griffith Brewer, read out in London a list of eighteen things that had been changed before the machine "flew." The Smithsonian did not change the label. So in 1928 Orville sent the Wrights' own 1903 machine, the most famous aeroplane in the world, to a museum in London, because of what he called the Smithsonian's "hostile and unfair attitude."

It took until 1942. That year the Smithsonian's Secretary printed Orville's own list of thirty-five differences between the machine of 1903 and the machine of 1914, and wrote that the 1914 flights "did not warrant" the claim that Langley's machine could have flown in 1903. He said he was sorry. Orville Wright died in January 1948. In December that year the Wright machine came home from London and was hung in the Smithsonian, forty-five years to the day after it first flew.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Test the thing you are actually trying to find out.** Langley's team tested the engine for ten hours. They jumped up and down on the frame. They soaked the wing ribs in water. They flew the models over and over. The one thing they never tested, before the day of the real flight, was a person flying a machine. That was the whole experiment, and it was the only part that had never been tried.

**A model is not the real thing.** The little aerodromes flew beautifully, with no one on board, balancing themselves. That proved that a machine heavier than air could fly. It did not prove that a man could fly one, or that a machine four times bigger would behave the same way. Langley believed the models had answered the question. They had only answered half of it.

**Practice counts as data.** The Wrights spent three years gliding down sand dunes before they added an engine. That looked like play, not science. But every glide taught them something about balancing in the air that no model and no calculation could.

**Do not let money set the date.** On December 8th the wind was wrong, the light was going, and the river was full of ice. They launched anyway, because the money had run out and it was "now or never." A test run on the wrong day because of the calendar is not a fair test of the machine.

**Wanting a result is not the same as getting one.** In 1914 the Smithsonian rebuilt the machine with everything that had been learned in ten years, flew it for five seconds, and said that proved it could have flown in 1903. It took twenty-eight years, and the loss of the Wright machine to London, before the Smithsonian admitted that it had not.

So the lesson is simple. Before you say an experiment has worked, make sure you have tested the thing you really wanted to know, and not just everything around it.
