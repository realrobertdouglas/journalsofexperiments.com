## The rocket

The Space Shuttle was launched by two big white rockets strapped to its sides, called solid rocket boosters. Each one was a steel tube packed with fuel that burned from the inside out, like a giant firework. The rockets were so big that they were built in pieces in Utah, sent to Florida by train, and stacked up on top of each other at the launch pad. Where two pieces met there was a joint, and the joint had to keep the burning gas inside, because the gas was hotter than a blowtorch.

The joint was sealed by two rubber rings, called O-rings. Each ring was about as thick as a pencil and ran all the way round the rocket. When the rocket lit, the pressure inside was supposed to push the first ring into the crack and seal it, in less than a second. If the first ring did not seal, the second ring was supposed to do the job instead. That was the plan.

The company that built the rockets was called Morton Thiokol. The rockets belonged to NASA, the American space agency, and were looked after by NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center in Alabama. Thiokol's engineers knew the joints best. Among them were Roger Boisjoly, who inspected the rockets after they were fished out of the sea, Arnie Thompson, who was in charge of the design of the steel cases, and Bob Ebeling, who ran a team trying to fix the seals. Allan McDonald was Thiokol's senior man at the launch site in Florida.

## What went wrong with the joint

In 1977, long before the Shuttle first flew, Thiokol tested a rocket case by filling it with water at high pressure. They found something nobody expected. When the pressure came on, the joint opened a little instead of closing. That meant the crack the rings had to seal got wider at exactly the moment the rings had to seal it. Engineers at NASA wrote memos saying the design was "completely unacceptable". Nobody answered them, and the joint was not redesigned.

The Shuttle started flying in 1981. The rings were never supposed to be touched by the hot gas at all. But on the second flight, one ring came back partly burned. On later flights more rings came back burned, or with black soot behind them, which meant hot gas had blown past them before they sealed. Each time, the engineers worked out that the ring had not burned all the way through, so there was still a margin, and the Shuttle was allowed to fly again. NASA's paperwork said that if a joint leaked, the result would be "loss of mission, vehicle and crew". The Shuttle flew anyway, twenty-four times.

In January 1985 the Shuttle was launched after the coldest weather Florida had seen in years. Thiokol's engineers worked out that the rings had been at about 53 degrees Fahrenheit, which is cold for Florida. When Boisjoly took that rocket's joints apart, he found the worst damage yet: grease between the rings "blackened just like coal", all the way round a third of one joint. He began to think the cold was the reason. Rubber gets stiff when it is cold, like a garden hose in winter, and a stiff ring might not move fast enough to seal.

That spring Thompson tested it. He squeezed a piece of O-ring between two metal plates, then pulled the plates apart at the speed the joint opened. At 100 degrees the rubber kept up with the metal. At 75 degrees it lost touch for nearly two and a half seconds. At 50 degrees it had still not touched the metal again after ten minutes. In July 1985 Boisjoly wrote a letter to his boss warning that if a ring failed in a field joint, the result "would be a catastrophe of the highest order - loss of human life." In October, Ebeling wrote a report that began with the word "HELP!" because his team was not being given the time or the help to fix the problem.

## The night before

The Shuttle Challenger was due to launch on the morning of January 28th 1986, with seven people aboard, including Christa McAuliffe, a schoolteacher who was going to teach lessons from space. The launch had already been put off four times. On the afternoon of January 27th the weather forecast came in: a cold front, with temperatures in the low twenties overnight, far below freezing. Nobody had ever launched a Shuttle in weather like that.

When Thiokol's engineers in Utah heard the forecast, they got worried. Ebeling called a meeting, and then called McDonald in Florida, who found out the real forecast: about 26 degrees at launch time. McDonald told the engineers to prepare charts and to recommend a safe temperature. A telephone conference was set up between Utah, NASA in Alabama and NASA in Florida.

At quarter to nine that evening, Florida time, the engineers started presenting their charts over the phone. There were thirteen of them, most written out by hand because there had been no time to type them. They showed the black grease from the cold flight, the test of the rubber at different temperatures, and how rubber gets harder as it gets colder. Their conclusion was that the Shuttle should not fly unless the rings were at least 53 degrees, the coldest they had ever flown. The forecast said 29 degrees.

The NASA managers did not like the answer. One of them, Lawrence Mulloy, said that no rule had ever mentioned the temperature of the rings, that the Shuttle had flown twenty-four times, and that if Thiokol invented a new rule now, "we may not be able to launch until next April." Everyone remembered it in a shorter form: "My God, Thiokol, when do you want me to launch, next April?" Another NASA manager, George Hardy, said he was "appalled" by Thiokol's recommendation. But he also said he would not launch if Thiokol said no.

Then Thiokol's managers asked for five minutes off the line to talk among themselves. They put the phone on mute. The five minutes became thirty. In the room in Utah, the senior manager said that a "management decision" was needed. Boisjoly and Thompson argued again. Thompson walked up the table, put a pad of paper down in front of the managers and started to sketch the joint. When he saw that nobody was listening, he stopped. Boisjoly picked up the photographs of the black grease and tried once more, and then he stopped too. The senior manager turned to the head of engineering, Bob Lund, and told him to "take off his engineering hat and put on his management hat". Then the four managers decided among themselves, and nobody asked the engineers what they thought.

At about eleven o'clock Thiokol came back on the phone and said it now recommended launching. NASA asked for that in writing. The fax arrived in Florida at quarter to midnight. It said the temperature data were "not conclusive". It was signed by a Thiokol vice president. McDonald, who was in the room in Florida, had refused to sign it himself, and had told the NASA managers that if something went wrong he would not want to be the person explaining it to a board of inquiry. He was told it was not his concern.

The NASA managers in Florida then phoned their boss about some other problems, the weather at sea and ice on the launch pad. They did not mention the O-rings. The people at the top of NASA, the launch director, and the seven people who were going to fly never heard that anyone had argued against launching. Ebeling drove home and told his wife, "It's going to blow up."

## The morning

It was so cold that night that the water pipes on the launch tower had been left trickling so they would not burst. The wind blew the water into sheets of ice and icicles a foot long. Teams went out to the pad twice in the dark to look at it. The launch was put off for two hours while some of the ice melted.

At 11:38 in the morning, with the air at 36 degrees, Challenger lifted off. Less than one second after the boosters lit, a camera caught a puff of grey smoke coming from a joint on the right-hand booster. The ring had not sealed. Eight more puffs followed in the next two seconds. Then the smoke stopped, probably because burned bits of rubber and putty had plugged the gap for a while. At 58 seconds a small flame appeared at the same joint. It grew, and burned through to the big fuel tank the boosters were strapped to. At 73 seconds, nearly nine miles up, the fuel tank broke apart and the Shuttle came apart with it. All seven people aboard were killed.

## What the investigation found

The President set up a commission to find out what had happened. It included Neil Armstrong, the first man on the Moon, Sally Ride, the first American woman in space, and Richard Feynman, a famous physicist. At a public hearing, Feynman squeezed a piece of the rubber ring, dropped it into a glass of ice water, and showed that when he let go it did not spring back. "I believe that has some significance for our problem," he said.

The commission found that the joint had failed because the cold rings were too stiff to seal the crack as it opened. It also found that the decision to launch was "flawed", because the people at the top had never been told that Thiokol's engineers were against it.

Then it looked at the charts from the night before. The engineers had compared two flights: the cold one, with the terrible black grease, and a warm one that had a little soot. From two flights you cannot tell much, and the NASA managers had pointed out that the warm flight had had soot too. But the commission did something the engineers had not. It made a chart of every flight, including the seventeen flights that had come back with their joints perfectly clean. Every single one of the clean flights had flown at 66 degrees or warmer. Every flight colder than that had come back damaged. Drawn that way, the pattern was plain. The commission wrote that a careful look at all the flights "would have revealed the correlation of O-ring damage and low temperature."

There is a complication. The engineers did not have the ring temperatures for all the flights. Those were worked out later by the commission. Boisjoly's team had asked for the numbers months before and never got them. Also, a committee of Congress pointed out that the Shuttle before Challenger had been delayed several times in cold weather, below 53 degrees, and nobody at Thiokol had said a word then. So the engineers were right, but they had only started to say so the night before.

## After

Discovery flew in September 1988, two years and eight months later, with new joints. The new joint had a metal latch to stop it opening, a third ring, and electric heaters to keep the seals warm whatever the weather. McDonald was put in charge of the redesign, after a congressman threatened the company for having pushed him aside for telling the truth. Boisjoly left the company and spent the rest of his life telling engineering students about that night. He died in 2012. Ebeling blamed himself for thirty years, until hundreds of strangers wrote to him in 2016 to say he had done his job; he died a few weeks later, at 89.

The commission's chairman asked McDonald, in public, whether he had "in effect been punished for being right". McDonald said: "I feel I was."

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**You cannot see a pattern if you only look at the failures.** The engineers compared the flights that had gone wrong. The flights that had gone right were the other half of the evidence, and nobody put them on the table. When the commission added them, the answer was obvious: cold flights got damaged, warm flights did not. Whenever you are trying to find out what causes a problem, write down the times it did not happen too.

**Being asked to prove something is dangerous is not the same as being asked whether it is safe.** Every other time, Thiokol had to prove the Shuttle was ready to fly. That night, the engineers found they were being asked to prove it was not. Lund said afterwards: "We had to prove to them that we weren't ready." Nobody could prove the ring would fail, because it had not failed yet. If you ever hear "prove to me it will go wrong", be careful.

**The people who decided were not the people who knew.** The engineers who had seen the black grease and run the tests were in the room, but the decision was made by four managers on a muted phone, and nobody asked the engineers for a vote. Then the people at the top of NASA, and the crew, were never told there had been an argument at all. One commissioner, a general, said that if an airline had argued for two hours about whether a wing might fall off, he would at least mention it to the pilot.

**A warning that nobody acts on is not a warning.** The joint problem had been written up in memos since 1977. A letter in July 1985 said "loss of human life". A report in October said "HELP!" Each one was filed. Writing a danger down is only the first step; someone has to stop and fix it.

So the lesson is simple. Count the times nothing went wrong as carefully as the times something did, and when someone says "prove it is unsafe", remember that the question was supposed to be the other way round.
