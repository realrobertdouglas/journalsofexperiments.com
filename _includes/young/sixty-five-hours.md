## The start

Spitsbergen is a group of Arctic islands between Norway and the North Pole. At a quarter to two in the afternoon on July 11th 1897, three sailors inside a tall wooden shed on Danes Island, at its north-west corner, cut three ropes at the same instant. A balloon rose out of the shed with three men standing on the roof of its basket and went out over the harbour, cheered by the crew of a Swedish gunboat. Over the middle of the harbour it began to sink. The basket touched the water, or nearly. Eight sacks of sand went over the side, and the balloon bounced back up. Then a sailor shouted. The drag ropes were lying on the shore.

Three heavy ropes lay along the stones below the shed. Each was the lower two-thirds of a guide rope that should now have been trailing in the sea behind the balloon. Together they weighed about 530 kilograms. The men who ran to them thought the ropes had snapped. They had not. They had unscrewed.

The balloon was out of sight within the hour. It stayed in the air for sixty-five hours and thirty-three minutes. What happened in that time, and in the eighty-seven days after it, nobody knew for thirty-three years. Then it became known hour by hour, because the three men in the basket had written it all down.

## The three men

Salomon August Andrée was born in 1854 in the small town of Gränna, in Sweden. He trained as an engineer, spent a winter at a scientific station in Spitsbergen, and then worked at the Swedish Patent Office. Among his private notes are two lines, one above the other: "The thing is so difficult that it is not worth while attempting it. The thing is so difficult that I cannot help attempting it." In 1893 he bought a balloon in Paris and made nine flights in it, alone. He fitted it with a sail and with ropes that dragged along the ground, and found that he could make it travel at an angle to the wind instead of straight downwind.

Nils Strindberg was born in Stockholm in 1872, a relative of the famous playwright. He was an assistant in physics at the university and had been a photographer since he was sixteen. In 1896 he got engaged to Anna Charlier, a postmaster's daughter. Knut Frænkel was born in 1870, a railway engineer's son and a gymnast, and had just qualified as an engineer himself. When the balloon left, Frænkel was 27, Strindberg was 24 and Andrée was 42. The money came quickly: Alfred Nobel, the inventor of dynamite, promised half the cost, King Oscar II and a rich merchant gave most of the rest, and within a month the whole budget was covered.

## The plan

Andrée presented his plan to scientists in Stockholm and to an international congress of geographers in London in 1895. Sledges had failed to reach the North Pole, he said. A balloon could carry three men there and home again. Reaching the Pole "is not a purely scientific, but a technical problem," and he broke it into four requirements. The balloon must lift three men, their instruments, four months of food and ballast, about 3,000 kilograms in all. It must be tight enough to stay up for thirty days. It must be filled with gas in the Arctic. And it must be steerable, at least a little.

The first three he answered with other people's numbers. For the fourth he had only his own flights, on which he had steered about 27 degrees away from the wind. So the experiment had two parts. One was about material: would a silk bag hold enough hydrogen for a month? The other was about control: would ropes and a sail turn a balloon from a thing blown by the wind into a thing under the control of the pilot?

Under both lay a guess about the Arctic summer: constant sun, little snow, few storms, and smooth ice for the ropes to drag over. Fog was not on the list. When someone asked about it, Andrée said that fog over so large an area "is a mere supposition for which there is no ground." The experts in London argued back. An American general who had led a polar expedition told him that a balloon loses at least one percent of its gas every day, and that it might reach the Pole but could never come back. Andrée answered with arithmetic, and told them all: "I do not ask for money. I have got the money, and the attempt will be made."

## The ropes

The balloon was made in Paris by Henri Lachambre: a ball of varnished silk with a net over it and a wicker basket hung underneath.

The ropes were the invention. Three guide ropes, about a thousand metres of rope altogether, were to trail their lower ends on the ice or the sea. If the gas cooled and the balloon sank, more rope rested on the ground and the balloon grew lighter. If the sun warmed the gas, the balloon rose, lifted more rope and grew heavier. That kept it at a steady height without throwing out sand. For steering there were three sails, and a tackle that swung the whole balloon on its ropes so that the sails met the wind at an angle.

Critics worried that a dragging rope would catch under the ice and never come free. Andrée's answer was a screw joint partway down each rope, and a winch in the basket that could twist the rope until the joint unscrewed and the lower part dropped off. He wrote down what he thought of it: an invention "which, perhaps, is not without ingenuity, but which, in my own opinion, is altogether unnecessary." He built it, he said, because the critics would not stop.

For sending news there were thirty-six carrier pigeons and thirteen cork buoys with brass tubes for letters. For the journey Andrée hoped never to make there were three sledges, a canvas boat, a tent, rifles and food for six months.

## The first summer

A wooden balloon house five storeys tall went up on the beach at Danes Island in the summer of 1896, and the balloon was filled with hydrogen made on the spot. Then they waited for a south wind. For three weeks the wind came from the north, or not at all, and in the middle of August Andrée let the gas go. The waiting had been an experiment of its own, and the expedition's third man, a meteorologist named Nils Ekholm, read the result: the gas was leaking out through the seams, about thirty kilograms of lift a day. Over thirty days that was 900 kilograms out of about 3,000, before any sand was thrown out for clouds, fog or landing. Ekholm resigned. Strindberg was urged to follow him and would not, because he had given his word. Over the winter the balloon was enlarged and varnished again, and in June 1897 it was filled once more at Danes Island. Frænkel had taken Ekholm's place.

## The flight

On the morning of July 11th 1897 the wind came from the south, fresh and gusty. On the roof of the balloon house Andrée asked the other two. Frænkel hesitated, then said they should go. Strindberg said: "I think we ought to try it." Andrée told the ship's captain that his companions insisted on starting and that he had no good reason to refuse, "although with some reluctance." The balloon was named *Örnen*, the Eagle, as it rose.

Within two minutes the basket had dipped, the sand had gone, and the ropes were on the beach. Suddenly lighter by more than 700 kilograms, the *Eagle* shot up to 600 metres, four times the height the ropes were meant to hold it at, and went into a cloud. Up there the sun warmed the hydrogen and some of it blew off through the valve. Strindberg's note in his diary was two words: "Guide-rope lost." They tied a spare line to one of the rope stumps and flew on. That evening they dropped a buoy with a message: "Weather magnificent. In best of humours." It washed up in northern Norway three years later.

Soon after midnight the balloon sank into the shadow of a cloud bank, and the ropes came down onto the ice. This was the plan at last. It did not work. The spliced rope was too short to swing the balloon, so it lay the wrong way to the wind and the sails pushed it down. Then the wind died, and when it came back it blew west, slowly, in fog.

Fog was the thing nobody had counted. It settled on the silk and the net as water, and the water weighed more than anything they could throw out. On the afternoon of July 12th the basket began hitting the ice. For an hour they threw out whatever could be spared, including the great buoy that was meant to be dropped at the Pole, empty. The basket went on bumping, every five minutes, then every other minute. At ten that night the *Eagle* stopped altogether, dripping, and would not move.

Andrée sent the other two to bed and sat up alone. Just before eleven he wrote the only passage in all the diaries that says what he thought he was doing: "It is not a little strange to be floating here above the Polar Sea. To be the first that have floated here in a balloon. How soon, I wonder, shall we have successors? Shall we be thought mad or will our example be followed?"

The balloon stayed stuck for thirteen hours, held by a rope caught under a block of ice. When it came free they released four pigeons. One carried the only message that reached home while anyone could still have acted on it: their position, and "All well on board." That afternoon the fog came back, and this time it froze on the ropes. At eight in the evening they made the decision a crew makes once. Over the side went buoys, the winch, 75 kilograms of sand and 200 kilograms of food. The *Eagle* lifted and the sails filled, and for a little while, for the only time in the flight, it sailed the way it had been designed to. "Altogether, it is quite stately!" Andrée wrote. By midnight the basket was bumping again and the spliced rope had broken. At 7.19 on the morning of July 14th, in Andrée's handwriting, the last words written in the air: "We jumped out of the balloon."

## On the ice

They had come down on drifting sea ice about 480 kilometres from where they started and about 290 kilometres from the nearest land. For a week they stayed by the balloon, unpacking the basket and putting the sledges together. They decided to walk to Franz Josef Land, 340 kilometres to the south-east, where a store of food had been left for them.

They set out on July 22nd. Each sledge weighed between 140 and 200 kilograms, so they put all three men on one sledge at a time and went back for the others. Strindberg measured their position by the sun, and his figures said what nobody wanted to know: the ice was drifting, and carrying them backwards almost as fast as they walked. He also wrote letters to Anna, in shorthand, in the back of his logbook. On July 25th, her birthday, the three men gave four cheers for her.

After three weeks of the hardest work any of them had done, they had gained about 53 kilometres, and in the wrong direction. On August 4th Andrée gave up on Franz Josef Land and turned towards a group of islands north of Spitsbergen instead. Then he wrote down the thickness of the ice and took a sample of algae from underneath it. August was sickness, polar bears, open water, and the slow discovery that those islands were out of reach too. Frænkel's foot gave out and he could not pull. The ice carried them south-east, nearly as far as they had hoped to walk south-west.

By the middle of September they knew they would have to spend the winter on the ice. On September 17th they saw land for the first time since the flight: an island east of Spitsbergen that was one great dome of ice with cliffs of glacier for a shore. It was called White Island. They shot seals and a bear and reckoned they had food to last until the end of February. At the end of September they moved into a hut of ice and snow that Strindberg had built, and named it Home. On the night of October 1st the floe split under its wall, and water poured in. The big diary ends: "No one had lost courage; with such comrades, one should be able to manage under, I may say, any circumstances." After that there is only Strindberg's little almanac, a word or two a day. October 5th: "Moved to land." They had reached White Island. The almanac has one last entry, for Sunday October 17th: "home 7.05 a.m."

## The finding

The pigeon came home first. On July 15th 1897 the crew of a Norwegian sealing ship saw a strange bird on the rigging, and the captain shot it. Only later that day, when another skipper asked whether it might have been one of Andrée's pigeons, did he turn back and find it floating. Then nothing. The buoys came ashore in 1899 and 1900. In 1898 a Swedish expedition landed on White Island, looked round, and left, a few miles from a camp that was then less than a year old.

For a generation the story ended there. In the summer of 1930 the snow on White Island melted as it had not in many years. A Norwegian sealing ship carrying a small party of scientists anchored off the island, and on August 6th two young sailors walked up the beach looking for drinking water. They picked up an aluminium lid. Then they saw something dark in a snowdrift: a canvas boat, half buried, full of gear, much of it stamped with the expedition's name. Near it lay a body with a large letter A sewn inside the jacket and a diary in the inside pocket. Nearby, between two rocks, was a grave. It was Strindberg's. The leader of the party wrote that if the snow had been a couple of feet deeper, "everything would have been concealed." A month later a journalist reached the island, when more snow had gone, and found Frænkel's body, with Strindberg's almanac in the pocket and inside it a fountain pen that still had ink in it.

The diaries were dried and read. The photographic film went to an expert in Stockholm, who had almost no hope of it. From 192 exposures that had lain in the ice for thirty-three years he recovered about twenty pictures that could be printed. They are the pictures in this entry.

On October 5th 1930 the *Svensksund*, the same gunboat that had taken them north, came up Stockholm harbour in the rain with three coffins on her deck. The King met them on the quay. In the cathedral the archbishop began with the words "Welcome home!" and read aloud what Andrée had written in the basket on the night of July 12th.

## What killed them, and what killed the balloon

What killed the three men has never been settled. The camp was well stocked with meat, ammunition, matches and fuel. Strindberg died first and was buried by the other two; Andrée and Frænkel seem to have died in the tent at about the same time. It has often been said that they died of a disease called trichinosis, from a parasite in the bear meat they ate, but a careful review in 2022 says that has not been proven. Cold, fumes from the stove in a closed tent, and a bear have all been suggested.

What brought the balloon down was settled, by the people who had built it. A committee went through every sack of sand thrown and every height recorded. Between the start and the landing the *Eagle* lost about 1,580 kilograms of lift. Leaking gas accounted for perhaps 600 of that, most of it in the first hour, when the lost ropes sent the balloon too high. The rest was water and ice from the fog, which had weighed more than the leak. "Strangely enough," the committee wrote, "the fog, and the ice deposited by fog, were not taken into calculation." The steering was never tested at all. With the lower ropes gone, the balloon could not be swung on them.

At the end of his logbook, between a recipe for pancakes and the menu for a banquet, Strindberg had written a heading: "Proposals for alterations in the next polar balloon expedition." Under it are four lines. The first is: "The drag-lines to be sheathed in metal." On a drifting ice floe, he had already found both of the failures the committee would confirm thirty-three years later.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Andrée turned a dream into a list, and that was clever.** Instead of saying "we will fly to the Pole", he wrote down four things the balloon had to do: lift the weight, hold its gas for thirty days, be filled in the Arctic, and be steered. Three of the four really were met. The balloon lifted the load, it was filled on an Arctic beach, and the gas did not run out. Making a list lets you check each thing. That part he did well.

**But he had tested the most important thing only once.** Steering was the one thing that made this an expedition rather than a flight that went wherever the wind blew. He had tried it on one afternoon in 1894, with a smaller sail on a smaller balloon. The ropes were the only way to steer, and they were lying on the beach two minutes after the start. The thing that mattered most was the thing tested least.

**A safety device added to please other people can be the thing that breaks.** The critics worried that the ropes would catch under the ice. So Andrée added screw joints that would come apart if a rope snagged. He wrote that he did not believe in them and had only added them because the critics kept on. Those joints are what unscrewed on the beach. If you add something to a design, you have to believe in it and test it, or it belongs to someone else's fear and not to your plan.

**The thing nobody put on the list is the thing that gets you.** Fog was not on Andrée's list. He had seen little of it in the Arctic and said it was not a real risk. It came on the first night and never left. Water from the fog settled on the silk and weighed more than all the sand they threw out. The balloon was pulled down by something nobody had counted.

**Writing things down is how an experiment outlives the people who did it.** The three men died. But Strindberg kept his notes neatly, Andrée kept writing in a tent that was killing him, and they wrapped their film in brass. Thirty-three years later people could read exactly what had happened, sack of sand by sack of sand, and work out why the balloon came down. Strindberg had even written a list of four changes for the next balloon. That is why we know this story at all.

So the lesson is simple. Test the most important part the most, do not rely on a device you do not believe in, think hard about what is missing from your list, and write everything down.
