## The idea

In the 1950s a Navy doctor called George Bond had an idea about diving. When a diver goes down, the pressure of the water pushes gas into his body. The deeper he goes and the longer he stays, the more gas gets in, and the slower he has to come up, or the gas comes out of his blood as bubbles and hurts him. That slow coming up is called decompression.

Bond noticed something. After about a day down, the body is full and cannot take any more gas. After that, it does not matter whether a diver stays for one day or thirty: the decompression at the end is the same. Bond called this saturation diving. If it worked, divers could live on the sea floor for weeks, work there every day, and pay the price of coming up only once.

In 1957 Bond began to test the idea on the side, with two friends. Walter Mazzone had been a submarine officer in the Second World War and was now a Navy pharmacist. Robert Workman was the Navy's expert on decompression. They called the work Genesis. They put guinea pigs, goats and dogs into a pressure chamber, breathing helium and oxygen, and sometimes paid for the animals and the food out of their own pockets. When the animals did well, Bond asked to try it on men. The Navy said no. It changed its mind when the space programme wanted to know whether astronauts could breathe helium and oxygen. They tried it on men: six days at ordinary pressure, then 100 feet, then 200 feet. It worked.

The Navy was interested now. In July 1964 four men lived for eleven days in a steel tank called Sealab I, 193 feet down off Bermuda. In 1965 Sealab II, a bigger tank 57 feet long, was put down at 205 feet off San Diego, California. Twenty-eight men lived in it in turns, fifteen days each. One of them was an astronaut, Scott Carpenter, who stayed for thirty days. Another was Berry Cannon.

## Berry Cannon

Berry Cannon was born in 1935 and raised by his grandmother in Williston, Florida. He spent four years in the Navy, went to university, and became an electronics engineer at a Navy laboratory in Panama City, Florida. In Sealab II he spent fifteen days at 205 feet. He was married to Mary Lou and had three sons, Patrick, Kevin and Neal.

He kept a diving logbook in capital letters. His first entry, for a dive to 200 feet in a Florida spring on July 8th 1967, ends: "No top at all, got cold."

## The big step

After Sealab II the project was taken over by a new part of the Navy, the Deep Submergence Systems Project, which had a lot more money. Its chief scientist, John Craven, wrote many years later that Sealab III had a secret purpose: if it worked, the Navy could send divers out of submarines to work on the sea floor where no one could see them.

The plan for Sealab III was much bigger. Instead of 205 feet, the habitat would go to 600 feet: three times as deep, with nearly three times the pressure. Five teams of nine men would live in it for twelve days each, two months in all, testing tools and studying the sea.

Bond and Mazzone had always gone one step at a time. This was a leap. Mazzone said afterwards: "We went to 600 feet. We were not being assured that the technology was there. We had no hot water suits that really worked. We had no way to keep the diver warm." As Mazzone put it, "the control shifted from the medical people that were doing it to the operational people."

## What they built

The habitat was the old Sealab II tank made over, with two extra rooms welded underneath. The men would get into it through a hatch in the floor of one of those rooms. The hatch was four feet square and weighed 150 pounds. The air inside the habitat was kept a little higher in pressure than the sea outside, to keep the water out.

Above the habitat was a ship, the *Elk River*, with a hole cut through its middle. On the ship were two pressure chambers, and two round steel capsules that could be joined to them. Divers would be put into a chamber on deck and pressurised to the pressure of the sea floor. Then they would step into a capsule at the same pressure, be lowered on a cable to the bottom, and swim across to the habitat. Weeks later they would come back up the same way and sit in the chamber on deck for about a week while the pressure was slowly let off.

The divers would breathe from a rig on their backs. The rig had a bag that the diver breathed in and out of, and a canister of a chemical called Baralyme. Every breath out carries carbon dioxide, which is poison if it builds up. The Baralyme soaked it up. If the canister was empty, the diver would breathe his own carbon dioxide back in, and it could kill him.

The cold was the other danger. The water at the bottom was 47 degrees Fahrenheit, and helium carries heat out of a body six times faster than air. The project had ordered electrically heated suits and suits warmed by hot water pumped down from the ship.

Everything was late. There was a steel strike, some of the steel was brittle, and after the Apollo fire the oxygen system had to be redesigned. When the habitat was tested in a dry dock in San Francisco, it flooded because somebody had left a valve open, and it was tested with air, not helium, to save money. One of the two capsules could not be used. The other had flooded on a test dive and been patched up, but its heater did not work and its fan blew cold helium straight at the men inside. The heated suits were taken out of service a few days before the dives because of electrical faults. The office in Washington set the date anyway: the habitat would go down on February 15th 1969.

## The leak

On February 15th 1969 the habitat was lowered to the sea floor, 610 feet down, off San Clemente Island in California. Even before it went down, gas had been seen leaking out of it. The commander on the ship decided the leaks were small.

They were not small. Helium is the smallest of all gases, and it gets through almost anything. On the bottom the habitat lost helium at about 3,000 cubic feet an hour. A small submarine went down to look and saw seven streams of gas pouring out of it. To keep the sea out, the people on the ship pumped more gas in, so the pressure inside went up. Alarms went off that meant electricity was leaking from the main power cable into the water. Nobody could fix any of this from the ship. Somebody had to go down and get inside.

## The first dive

Four men were pressurised in a chamber on deck: Bob Barth, the team leader, who had been in every Sealab; Berry Cannon; Richard Blackburn; and John Reaves. On February 16th they went down in the one working capsule. The trip down took two hours instead of thirty minutes. "The bell had absolutely no heat in it whatsoever," Blackburn remembered.

Cannon swam out, flooded a ballast tank to settle the habitat, and came back after thirteen minutes with his teeth chattering, breathing hard. He said, "Cold. Very Cold." Barth swam under the habitat and pulled on the hatch. It would not move. The extra pressure inside, meant to keep the water out, was holding it shut with a force of about four and a half tons.

On the ship a television camera showed the pressure gauge. A diver called Keith Moore, who had his hand on the valve that fed air to the habitat, understood what was happening. He asked the commander three times for permission to turn the air down so the leaks would let the pressure drop and Barth could open the hatch. Three times he was told to hold pressure. There was no way to talk to Barth. Divers in the water had no radio.

After less than fifteen minutes Barth came back to the capsule, dizzy and about to pass out. The four were hauled up and went back into the chamber to get warm. The rigs they had breathed from went to the diving locker, where Chief Paul Wells and Keith Moore refilled the canisters. Everyone who worked with Wells said he was the most careful man on the project.

## The second dive

About four hours later, with an hour's sleep, they were asked to go again. The habitat was losing gas fast and would soon be lost. Scott Carpenter argued that the men needed more rest. He was overruled. Barth said that he and Cannon were ready: they were the two who had trained to open the hatch. The divers have said since that they were given pills to keep them awake.

Cannon wore a hot-water suit with nothing under it, no gloves and no hood. When they reached the bottom at about four in the morning on February 17th, the hot-water system did not work. The two men went out anyway. Barth went to the hatch, which was now held shut with nearly seven tons of force. Cannon went to look at the leaking cables on the side of the habitat, in the dark, in a cloud of bubbles.

At about five o'clock, on the television screen on the ship, Cannon began to convulse. Bond was watching. "I saw his body jackknifing, making a rapid motion," he said. "Any time you see rapid motion in a diver, you know he's in trouble." In the capsule, Blackburn and Reaves heard a scream through the water. Cannon's mouthpiece came out. He sank to the bottom a few feet from Barth. Barth tried to push a spare mouthpiece between his teeth, but his jaws were clamped shut. Barth tried to bring him back to the capsule, but he was worn out. Blackburn came out and brought Cannon in.

In the rush, three of the four breathing rigs were left outside the capsule, hanging on a line. The capsule weighed 25,000 pounds and took more than half an hour to reach the ship. Berry Cannon was dead before it got there.

That night, by Craven's account, the men on the ship rigged a winch and pulled the habitat off the bottom before it could flood. It never went down again.

## What the Navy found

At first the Navy's doctors said Cannon had probably had a heart attack. A week later the Navy announced that one of the four breathing rigs had come back with its Baralyme canister empty. The coroner said Cannon had died of carbon dioxide poisoning. The Navy stopped all Sealab diving.

On February 26th a board of three officers began a hearing in San Diego. It sat for seventeen days. It heard that the empty rig also had a hole in its breathing hose. The officer in charge of the videotape said the pictures could not show which of the four men had worn the empty rig. Wells's lawyer argued that an electric shock in the water could have killed Cannon. Blackburn told the board: "We were all pushed to the point where mistakes were inevitable." The board ended, a newspaper reported, "without establishing whether he was the one who wore a defective breathing rig on the fatal dive."

The Navy's official answer was carbon dioxide poisoning, and letters of reprimand went to the commander, to Chief Wells and to Mazzone. Wells left the Navy. Bond wrote in his journal: "Not one of us believed that Paul Wells would ever load an empty canister into a Mk IX rig." Barth said: "We lost two good divers that day, Berry Cannon and Paul Wells."

Craven believed somebody had been sabotaging the project. During the week when the surviving divers were slowly decompressing in the chamber on deck, Mazzone found a valve that fed oxygen to their masks open when it should have been shut. He shut it. He found it open again. He shut it and wired it so the wire would break if anyone touched it, and an hour later the wire was broken. A guard was put on the chamber. Craven took his suspicions to Naval Intelligence, which said there was not enough evidence. In July 1969 a congressman told the House that "the exact cause of his death has never been determined." It still has not been.

## After

The Navy closed the Sealab programme by the end of 1970. In 1971 the habitat was cut up for scrap, with an order that it must never be used again or put on display. One of its end caps turned up in a scrapyard in 1991, and it is now on display at a Navy museum in Washington State.

The method lived on. The Navy went on training saturation divers on the *Elk River*, and in the 1970s its divers worked from submarines on the floor of the sea off the Soviet Union, as Craven had wanted. The full story of that night, and of the question of what killed Cannon, is in two companion pieces, "[The open valve](/deeper/six-hundred-feet/the-open-valve/#young)" and "[Four rigs](/deeper/six-hundred-feet/four-rigs/#young)". Bond died in 1983. Mazzone died in 2014. Barth died in 2020.

In 2024 an ocean engineer named Kevin Hardy published the divers' own story. The engineers' logs from 1969 showed that the main power cable to the habitat had electrical faults before the dives and dead shorts afterwards. Hardy believes Cannon was electrocuted. Eleven of the surviving aquanauts signed a letter asking the Navy to clear Paul Wells's name. When Hardy asked the National Archives for the board's report, he was told its records of such boards end in 1953.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Go one step at a time.** Bond and Mazzone went from ordinary pressure to 100 feet to 200 feet, and then from 193 feet to 205. Sealab III jumped from 205 feet to 610 in one go. Every machine on the project had been tested at the old depth or in a chamber, and almost nothing had been tested in the sea at the new one. Helium that seeped out slowly at 205 feet poured out at 610. A hatch that opened easily in a dry dock would not open at all. The place where an experiment goes wrong is the place it has never been tried, and this one went there all at once.

**A fix for one problem can cause the next one.** Pumping more gas into the habitat kept the sea out, which was right. It also held the hatch shut so hard that nobody could get in, which nobody had written down. Keith Moore saw it and asked three times. He was told to hold pressure. When the instrument says one thing and the rules say another, somebody has to be allowed to believe the instrument.

**A checklist only covers what it describes.** Chief Wells filled and checked every canister, and everyone believed him. But the checklist described a rig on a bench. It did not describe a rig that had been changed at the last minute, never tested deep, breathed through a narrow hose by a man who had been awake for a day and was colder than he had ever been, and then left hanging in the sea for half an hour. The board looked for one missed line on the checklist. It did not ask whether the checklist matched the dive.

**Blaming one person is the easiest answer, and usually the wrong one.** The programme ended with reprimands for a chief, a commander and a doctor. The divers' questions about the schedule, the cold, the untested rigs and the electrical faults never had to be answered. One empty canister did not end the Navy's experiment. It was the explanation that let the Navy end it.

So the lesson is simple. When you make a big jump instead of a small step, expect everything you have not tested to go wrong at once, and before anyone goes down, decide who is allowed to say stop.
