## The car

At 2:40 in the morning on August 31st 1994, police in Clinton Township, Michigan, were called about a young man who seemed to be stealing tires from a parked car. They found a seventeen-year-old standing beside a Pontiac who said he was waiting for a friend. They searched the car. In the trunk was a toolbox, padlocked and sealed with duct tape. Around it lay more than fifty little foil-wrapped cubes of grey powder, metal disks and cylinders, a stack of camping-lantern mantles, old clock parts, lumps of rock, fireworks and jars of chemicals. The driver asked them not to open the toolbox. It was radioactive, he said.

The officers thought it might be a bomb. They had the car towed to the police station, which was not a good idea, and called the state bomb squad. Then the state health department came with instruments and found americium and thorium, two radioactive elements. Concentrated thorium is not found in nature, at least not in Michigan. By the end of the day state officials were on the phone with the Department of Energy, the Environmental Protection Agency, the FBI and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission about a teenager's car.

The teenager was David Hahn. He gave the police his father's address and said nothing about his mother's house, or about the potting shed behind it, for nearly three months.

## Who he was

David Hahn was born in 1976 in Royal Oak, Michigan. His parents divorced, and he grew up between two very different houses. His father, Ken, was an engineer at General Motors who worked long hours and was not an easy man to talk to. Ken remarried another engineer, Kathy, and David lived with them in Clinton Township during the week. At weekends he stayed with his mother, Patty, in a neighbourhood called Golf Manor. She struggled with drink and with mental illness, and her boyfriend liked the boy but understood nothing he said.

When David was ten, his step-grandfather gave him *The Golden Book of Chemistry Experiments*, a children's book from 1960 that promised a brave new world. By twelve he was reading his father's college chemistry textbooks and falling asleep on the living-room floor surrounded by open encyclopaedias. He was a terrible speller and a poor student who nearly failed the state tests in mathematics and reading, and aced the one in science. His physics teacher remembered that his dream was to collect a sample of every element on the periodic table. "My dream at that age," the teacher said, "was to buy a car."

He was also dangerous from early on. By fourteen he had made nitroglycerine, an explosive. Small blasts pocked his bedroom walls, so his chemistry was banished to the basement. There one day he hammered a container of red phosphorus and it exploded. His family found him half-conscious with his eyebrows smoking, and for months a doctor picked bits of plastic out of his eyes. After that experiments were forbidden in the house. So the work moved to the one place no adult ever went: the potting shed behind his mother's house.

His parents thought he must be making drugs. They checked up on him at the public library, where he was always exactly where he said he would be, surrounded by chemistry books. Nothing they tried worked. At Scout camp he turned up with his face dyed orange by a chemical he had swallowed, blew a hole in a tent with magnesium powder, and was sent home for stealing smoke detectors to take apart. David said later: "I was very emotional as a kid, and those experiments gave me a way to get away from that. They gave me some respect."

## The badge

On May 10th 1991, five months before his fifteenth birthday, David earned the Boy Scouts' Atomic Energy merit badge. He drew a diagram of nuclear fission, visited a hospital's X-ray department, and built a model reactor out of a juice can, coat hangers, drinking straws, matches and rubber bands. He was the only boy ever to earn the badge in his troop.

The badge pamphlet had been written with help from the nuclear industry, and it read like it. It explained where radioactive materials could be found in everyday life, such as the tiny amount of americium in a smoke detector and the thorium in the mantles of camping lanterns, and it listed government agencies a curious boy could write to for more. The scoutmaster's wife summed up what happened next: "The typical kid would have gone to a doctor's office and asked about the X-ray machine. Dave had to go out and try to build a reactor."

## What he wanted to find out

The goal grew in three steps. At fourteen, the badge. At fifteen, what he called a neutron gun: a source of neutrons, the tiny particles that can change one element into another when they strike it. At seventeen, a model of a breeder reactor, copied from a diagram in one of his father's textbooks.

He understood the physics well enough to know its limit. A real reactor needs a large amount of enriched uranium to start a chain reaction, and he had no way to get it and said so. What he wanted to see was the step before that. Thorium, when a neutron hits it, slowly turns into a kind of uranium that can be used as fuel. A breeder reactor makes more fuel than it burns by exactly that trick. David wanted to catch the turning happening. "No matter what happened," he said, "there would be something changing into something."

He got his information by mail. He wrote as many as twenty letters a day, signing them as a physics teacher at his own high school, to every agency the pamphlet listed. An official at the Nuclear Regulatory Commission took up a correspondence with "Professor Hahn" and, by David's account, sent him tips on separating radioactive elements, a list of the isotopes that can sustain a chain reaction, and the advice that nothing produces neutrons as well as beryllium. When David asked about the dangers, the official assured him they were very slight, because anyone with enough radioactive material to be hazardous would need a licence. Nobody checked who was writing. "The NRC gave me all the information I needed," David said. "All I had to do was go out and get the materials."

## The shed

Every radioactive thing he collected was legal to buy, and still is. Americium came from smoke detectors; one company sold him about a hundred broken ones for a dollar each after he said they were for a school project. Thorium came from camping-lantern mantles, which he bought by the thousand from surplus stores. Radium came from the glowing paint on old clock faces and dashboard dials, which he hunted for in junk shops with a Geiger counter, a radiation meter he had built from a kit and screwed to the dashboard of his car. One day the counter went wild as he drove past a second-hand shop. Inside was an old table clock with a green dial, which he bought for ten dollars. Inside the clock was a little bottle of radium paint, left there decades earlier for touching up the numbers. Beryllium came from a college chemistry department, stolen for him by a friend. Tritium came from the glowing sights sold for guns and bows. He also drove hundreds of miles of northern Michigan looking for uranium ore, and wrote to a foreign firm for samples, pretending to be a professor.

In the shed he concentrated what he had with heat, acids and kitchen equipment, using coffee filters and pickle jars. His protection was a lead apron he made himself, a gas mask, and the habit of throwing away his clothes after a night's work, which often ended at two in the morning. He knew what radium does. In his own notes he wrote that these elements go to the bone marrow and interfere with the making of blood, and that less than a millionth of a gram can be fatal. Then he kept going.

He built his neutron gun by putting the radium inside a block of lead with the beryllium in front of it. He used it to bombard his thorium and uranium powders, and by his reading of the counter the thorium grew more radioactive. Then he built the reactor. He mixed the radium and the americium with beryllium and aluminum shavings and wrapped the mixture in foil. That was the core. Around it he stacked tiny foil-wrapped cubes of thorium and uranium powder, alternating with cubes of carbon, held together with duct tape. That was the blanket. "It was radioactive as heck," he said later.

## What happened

Week by week the counts rose. Then they kept rising with nothing switched on, because nothing in the shed had a switch. The radiation could be detected through the walls of the shed, and then his dashboard counter began picking up the shed from five houses down the street. He had built a source he could not turn off, and for the first time it frightened him. He took the reactor apart. The thorium went into a shoebox in his mother's house. The radium and the americium stayed in the shed. Most of the rest went into the trunk of the car, where the police found it.

What followed was a cleanup of the wrong things. It was Thanksgiving before a state radiation expert got David to admit there was a laboratory behind his mother's house. By the time state surveyors reached it, on November 29th 1994, his mother, terrified that the government would take her home, had already gone through the shed and thrown most of what she found into the household trash: the neutron gun, the radium, the hottest of the thorium, and several quarts of radioactive powder. It went to a landfill and was never traced. "The funny thing is," David said, "they only got the garbage, and the garbage got all the good stuff."

The garbage was bad enough. The surveyors found pie pans, jars of acid and milk crates contaminated with americium and thorium. One vegetable can gave off about a thousand times the normal background radiation. They sealed the shed and called in the Environmental Protection Agency, which found "an imminent and substantial endangerment to public health." On June 26th 1995 a neighbour counted eleven men on her lawn, three of them in sealed suits, and telephoned the boy who had caused it. "Da-a-ve, there are men in funny suits walking around out here. You've got to do something." Over three days the men cut the potting shed into pieces with saws, packed the wood and everything in it into thirty-nine steel drums marked with the radiation symbol, and trucked the drums to a radioactive-waste dump in the Utah desert, where they were buried. The bill was about sixty thousand dollars.

The official report said that forty thousand people in the area could have been put at risk over the years David worked. No one ever measured how much radiation he or anyone else had actually received. The agency arranged for David to be examined at a nuclear plant. He was afraid of what he would learn, and refused.

## After

He fell into a depression. At school they called him Radioactive Boy. He was made an Eagle Scout shortly after the shed came down, though some Scout leaders tried to stop it. His parents sent him to a community college, and he skipped the classes. A few years later his mother took her own life. His parents gave him a choice between the armed forces and the street, and in 1997 he joined the Navy and served on an aircraft carrier, nowhere near its reactor.

In 1998 a journalist named Ken Silverstein found him and told the story in a magazine under the title that stuck: "The Radioactive Boy Scout". Nearly everything anyone knows about the shed comes from the five hours David spent talking to him. A book followed, and a television documentary, and later a long tail of podcasts.

In 2007, at thirty, David was arrested for taking smoke detectors from the ceilings of his apartment building. Police evacuated the building and called the bomb squad, which found nothing dangerous. He was treated at a veterans' hospital and ordered to take his medication. He died in 2016, at thirty-nine, from alcohol and drugs taken together. Check-ups had found no lasting effect from the radiation. "I'm proud of my son and I'm very sad that he's gone," his father said. "He could have done a lot more in this world."

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**It was never a real reactor.** Nothing in the shed came close to a chain reaction, and David himself never said it did. Some parts of his story are hard to believe, and physicists have said so. What is certain is that he collected real radioactive material, made it more concentrated, and left a shed so contaminated that men in sealed suits had to cut it up. The danger was never a bomb. It was breathing the dust, for months, with the door shut.

**The rules did not imagine him.** Every single thing he bought was legal. One smoke detector is harmless. One lantern mantle is harmless. The rules were written one item at a time, and nobody imagined a boy with a hundred of one and thousands of the other. The official who answered his letters told him nothing secret. He just believed the word "Professor" on the letters and never asked who was really writing.

**You cannot feel radiation, so it does not teach you to be careful.** When David blew up a jar of phosphorus, the pain taught him to be careful with fire. Radiation does not hurt, smell or glow. His books, written in the 1950s and 1960s, treated the danger as a small footnote to a wonderful future. So nothing warned him until his counter showed that the shed itself was dangerous, and by then it was too late.

**Banning something without offering anything else just hides it.** Every adult who found out about an accident banned the next experiment. Each ban moved the work farther from where any adult could see it: bedroom, then basement, then shed. The experiments did not stop. They just disappeared from view. Years later a boy named Taylor Wilson wanted to do the same kind of thing. His parents found him experts, a borrowed Geiger counter and a university laboratory, and at fourteen he did real science that other people could check. The difference between the two boys, the writer who compared them said, was mostly support.

**Cleaning up is part of the experiment.** The most dangerous material in the whole story was never put in a barrel. David's mother, frightened, threw it out with the household rubbish. An experiment is not over when you stop doing it. It is over when the dangerous material is gone, and nobody in this story had planned for that.

**Check the story.** Nearly everything we know comes from one long interview David gave years later to a journalist, and he had every reason to make the shed sound bigger than it was. The official records confirm the address, the cleanup and thirty-nine barrels. They do not confirm how much radiation anyone received.

So the lesson is simple. Curiosity needs a grown-up who helps instead of just saying no, and a danger you cannot feel needs rules, a counter and a plan for the cleanup.
