## The ship

The *Bear* was a revenue cutter: a government ship that patrolled the coast and enforced the law at sea. She was built in Scotland in 1874 to hunt seals, with wooden sides six inches thick so that she could push through ice. From 1886 to 1895 her captain was Michael Healy. He had been born into slavery in Georgia in 1839, the son of a planter and an enslaved woman. Nobody in the service knew it, and he never said.

Every spring Healy took the *Bear* from San Francisco to the Arctic coast of Alaska, and every autumn he brought her back. On two thousand miles of coast there was no other government. The *Bear* carried the mail, the teachers and the prisoners. Her deck was used as a courtroom. Her doctor treated anyone who came alongside. She stopped ships from hunting seals illegally and traders from selling liquor in the villages.

## Why reindeer

The people of the Alaska coast, the Iñupiat and the Yupik, lived by hunting walrus, whales and seals. By the 1880s American whaling ships had killed so many walrus and whales that in some winters whole villages starved. In 1881 Healy was on another cutter that called at St. Lawrence Island, where most of the people had died of hunger two winters before. On the Siberian side of the strait, fifty miles away, the Chukchi people kept herds of tame reindeer, and did not starve.

Healy's idea was simple: bring reindeer across the water, and teach the Alaskans to keep them. In 1890 he wrote that reindeer could answer "three vital questions of existence in this country—food, clothing and transportation." That summer he had a passenger, Sheldon Jackson, a missionary who ran the government's schools in Alaska. Jackson took the idea to Washington. Congress would not pay, so Jackson wrote to the newspapers and raised $2,156 from readers.

Jackson wrote down what the experiment was for. He wanted to find out two things. First, whether reindeer from Siberia could live in Alaska, have fawns, and survive being handled by people who had never kept a farm animal. That could be tested by counting the deer. Second, whether a hunting people would become a herding people, and would end up owning the herds. That could only be tested by looking at who owned what. Jackson even wrote down the way the experiment could fail: if white men were allowed to own herds, he said, the deer would never end up in Native hands, and the whole point would be lost. So the rule was that the government owned the deer and lent them out. A Native herder who borrowed a hundred had to give back a hundred, and kept the fawns.

## The first sixteen

In the summer of 1891 the *Bear* bought twenty reindeer on the Siberian coast and landed sixteen of them on an island near Unalaska, with sacks of Siberian moss to feed them. This was only a test, to see whether a reindeer could survive a sea voyage. It could. In 1892 the ship brought 171 reindeer to Port Clarence, near the Bering Strait, and a station was set up to keep them. Four Chukchi herders came to teach, and nine Iñupiaq apprentices were put to learn. By the autumn of 1893 there were 346 deer. Congress, now that there was something to count, gave $6,000.

The Chukchi herders went home, and in 1894 Jackson hired Sámi families from Lapland, in the far north of Norway, to teach instead. The Sámi had herded reindeer for centuries. In January 1895 the first hundred deer were lent to an Iñupiaq herder named Charlie Antisarlook. Missions were lent a hundred each. By the end of 1897 there were 1,500 reindeer in Alaska.

## The great drive, 1897 to 1898

In the autumn of 1897 eight whaling ships were trapped by ice near Point Barrow, at the top of Alaska, with about 275 men aboard and not enough food for the winter. The *Bear* was sent north in November, but no ship could get near Barrow in winter. So on 16 December 1897 she landed a small party on the coast 1,500 miles away: Lieutenant David Jarvis, Lieutenant Ellsworth Bertholf and the ship's doctor, Samuel Call. Their orders were to travel overland by dog sled, borrow the reindeer herds, and drive them to the whalers as food on the hoof.

In January Jarvis reached Charlie Antisarlook and asked for his herd. He did not take it. He explained what it was for and asked. Charlie and his wife Mary talked it over and agreed to lend their 133 reindeer, which were everything they owned, on Jarvis's promise that the government would give them back with the fawns. Jarvis wrote out a receipt. Then a mission schoolteacher named Tom Lopp lent 292 more. Lopp and seven Iñupiaq herders, Charlie among them, drove the herd. The experts had said that only Sámi or Siberians could drive reindeer. Nobody had asked the Alaskans.

They left on February 3rd 1898 with 438 deer. They crossed the sea ice of Kotzebue Sound, a crossing Jarvis had warned against, and nearly lost the herd in the night. They went over the mountains in temperatures of forty degrees below zero. On March 29th Jarvis and Call reached the whalers' camp, and the herd came in behind them: 382 reindeer, more than seven hundred miles in fifty-five days. Four of the whalers had scurvy, a disease caused by going too long without fresh food. Now there was fresh meat. Nobody in the party had died, and only one whaler died after the herd arrived.

The *Bear* reached Barrow in July, when the ice opened, and carried the men home. In 1899 the government paid back the loans with the fawns included: 700 reindeer to Lopp's mission and 300 to Charlie. Charlie died of measles the next summer. Mary kept the herd for the rest of her life, and became known along the coast as Sinrock Mary.

## The number that went up

Every year Jackson published a count of the reindeer in Alaska, and every year it was bigger: 2,800 in 1899; 10,000 in 1905; 27,000 in 1910; 70,000 in 1915; 180,000 in 1920; 350,000 in 1925. In Washington the number was read as proof that the experiment was working.

But the count only answered the first question. The second question, who owned the deer, was going wrong. The Sámi teachers were allowed to own deer, and they were not Natives, so the rule against selling to white men did not apply to them.

In 1914 a businessman from Nome named Carl Lomen bought a Sámi herder's 1,200 reindeer, and by 1929 his family had bought more than 14,000, paying about seventeen dollars a deer. They built cold-storage plants on the coast and shipped reindeer meat to Seattle by the thousand. To make Americans want to eat it, they went to the cities. At Christmas 1926 they put real reindeer into Santa Claus parades in Boston, Chicago, San Francisco and Seattle, with Santa in a sleigh behind them. The first Santa was a Sámi herder named Mathis Nilluka, and the letters from children that the newspapers printed beforehand had been written by the company. People called Carl Lomen the Reindeer King. The cattle ranchers of the western states noticed, and they got local laws passed against selling reindeer meat. By the 1930s the Lomens were said to own 250,000 reindeer. An auditor who went through their books in 1935 reported that this figure was "by computation": worked out on paper, not counted.

Two other things were happening that the count could not show. Reindeer live on lichen, a slow-growing plant that adds only about five millimetres a year. Where too many deer grazed, the lichen was gone, and it takes decades to come back. And the herds had been turned into companies with shares, and the shares were credited with more fawns each year than were really being born. The number on paper was pulling away from the number on the ground.

The Native herders knew. They complained, held meetings and sent telegrams. In 1931 there were hearings in Washington; no Native owner was there. In 1932 the count reached its peak, about 640,000.

## What went wrong

In 1937 Congress passed the Reindeer Act. It said that only Natives could own reindeer in Alaska, and that the government would buy every deer that anyone else owned. The non-Native owners said they had 500,000 reindeer. The men sent to count them in 1938 could find about 180,000. When the roundups were held in the winter of 1939 and 1940, 84,000 reindeer were actually counted and bought, for less than half a million dollars. For the first time in forty-eight years the count was a real count.

Then the herds collapsed. There were 550,000 reindeer on paper in 1937, 250,000 in 1940, and 25,000 in 1950. Wild caribou, which are the same animal as reindeer, came back through the ranges in huge numbers, and the tame reindeer followed them away. Wolves came with the caribou. The lichen was gone. And there were fewer people herding every year, because the money had gone to Seattle.

## What was left

In 1944 the Coast Guard put twenty-nine reindeer on St. Matthew Island, an empty island in the Bering Sea, as emergency food for a small radio station. The station closed. With nothing to eat them and no one to herd them, the reindeer grew to 6,000 by 1963, ate all the lichen, and starved in a single winter. In 1966 there were forty-two left. That graph is in every ecology textbook. Those reindeer were descendants of the ones the *Bear* carried from Siberia.

Today about twenty herders keep about twenty thousand reindeer on the Seward Peninsula, where the first 171 came ashore.

Healy was court-martialled in 1896 for drunkenness, lost his command, and died in 1904. Who his mother was did not become known until long after his death. The Coast Guard's largest icebreaker is named for him. The *Bear* went on to the Antarctic and the Second World War, and sank under tow in 1963; her wreck was found in 2019 and identified in 2021. ## What it teaches

An experiment can only see what its instrument measures. This one's instrument was a count of reindeer, and the count answered the easy question so well, and for so long, that everyone took it as the answer to the hard question too. For forty years an experiment about whether a people would come to own a living was reported as a number of animals. Because the number went up, nobody looked at who owned the animals until it was too late, and when they looked, the number itself was partly made up.

The people who ran the experiment had written down how it could fail, and then let it fail in exactly that way. The one time anyone measured the right thing was Jarvis's receipt: 133 reindeer borrowed from Charlie Antisarlook, 300 given back. That herd outlived Charlie by half a century in Mary's hands. The idea was true wherever anyone bothered to test it. The count is what everyone remembers.
