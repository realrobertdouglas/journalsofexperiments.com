## The piles

Windscale was a factory on the coast of Cumberland, in the north-west of England, built to make plutonium: the metal that goes inside an atomic bomb. Britain decided after the Second World War to build its own bomb, and the work was done very fast. Building started in September 1947, the first reactor was running in October 1950, the second in June 1951, and on October 3rd 1952 the first British bomb was exploded off the coast of Australia.

The two reactors were called piles. A pile is a huge block of graphite, the same stuff as a pencil lead, with thousands of holes through it. Each Windscale pile had 3,440 horizontal channels running from the front to the back, and inside the channels lay rods of uranium in aluminium cans, about 180 tons of uranium in all, in a block of nearly 2,000 tons of graphite. The uranium splits its atoms, which is called fission, and a little of it turns into plutonium. Fission gives off neutrons, tiny particles that keep the chain reaction going, and the graphite slows them down so that they can do that.

Fission also makes heat. The Windscale piles were cooled with ordinary air: big fans blew it through the channels and up a chimney 400 feet high. One scientist, Sir John Cockcroft, insisted that filters be built into the chimneys to catch anything radioactive that might go up with the air. The chimneys were already half built, so the filters had to go on at the very top. People at the works called them Cockcroft's follies. They turned out to be the most important thing on the site.

## The stored energy

Graphite has a strange property when it is bombarded with neutrons. The neutrons knock carbon atoms out of their proper places, and the atoms stay out of place and store up energy, like a spring pressed down. This is called Wigner energy. If the graphite gets warm enough, the atoms jump back into place and give the energy out as heat, which warms the graphite next to them, which gives out its energy too. If nobody is watching, it can run away.

The first time it happened at Windscale, in September 1952, nobody had planned it. After that the engineers worked out a way to let the energy out on purpose, slowly and safely. This was called a Wigner release, or an anneal. Every so often they shut the pile down, switched the fans off, opened the chimney door so that no air would be drawn through, and then started the chain reaction again at a very low power, with no cooling, so that the pile warmed itself up. When the graphite was warm enough the stored energy began to come out on its own, and they stopped the reaction and let it spread. Pile No. 1 was annealed eight times between 1953 and 1956.

It was getting harder each time. In April 1956 an anneal did not work at all, and two others only half worked. The gap between anneals was stretched, from 20,000 megawatt-days of running to 30,000 and then, in September 1957, to 40,000. By that rule the ninth anneal was due in October 1957.

## The thermometers

To do an anneal safely you need to know how hot the pile is, and where. The pile had thermocouples, electrical thermometers on long wires, fixed inside it. The ones in the graphite were four, six and ten feet in from the front. The ones in the uranium were fixed about sixteen feet in, because that was where the fuel got hottest when the pile was running normally, with the fans blowing the heat towards the back.

But an anneal is not normal running. With the fans off, the heat stays where it is made. And to make the heat, the operators arranged the control rods so that the chain reaction happened mostly at the front and bottom of the pile. So during an anneal the hottest uranium was not sixteen feet in. The inquiry worked out afterwards that it was about seven feet nearer the front, and could be 40 per cent hotter than the thermometers said. Nobody had ever worked that out, because nothing had gone wrong before.

There was something else nobody had written down. "It appears that there is nothing in the nature of a Pile Operating Manual," the inquiry found. The only written instruction for an anneal was a memo of about a hundred words from November 1955, saying what to do if the temperature reached 360 degrees. The inquiry had to "piece together the other details of pile operation from Committee minutes and from traditions which did not seem to have any written authority". And to read the thermometers at all, the men in charge had to climb seventy feet to the roof of the pile. There was no lift.

## The second heating

The ninth anneal began on Monday October 7th 1957. The pile was shut down just after one in the morning, the fans were stopped, and at 7:25 that evening the chain reaction was started again to warm the pile up. By about one in the morning of Tuesday the 8th it was running at 1.8 megawatts. Around midnight two channels reached the limit of 250 degrees, so the control rods were pushed in and by four in the morning the reaction was stopped. The graphite temperatures began to rise on their own, which meant the stored energy was coming out.

At about nine on Tuesday morning the physicist in charge looked at the readings and thought the temperatures were starting to fall. The inquiry looked at the same readings later and did not agree: "a substantial number of the graphite thermocouple readings showed steady increases". A second heating had been done three times before, but only after a whole day of falling temperatures. This time the physicist decided to do it straight away. At 11:05 the rods were pulled and the pile was started up for the second time.

The uranium thermometers jumped. One rose by 80 degrees in a quarter of an hour, to 380. The normal rule for heating a pile was two degrees a minute; this was up to thirty. The operators brought it back down with the rods and kept the heating going gently until five in the afternoon. By then, the inquiry believed, the damage was done. Seven feet in front of the thermometers, where nobody could measure, the uranium may have reached 450 degrees. At that heat, the aluminium cans around the oldest fuel, which had already grown brittle, cracked at the ends. Bare uranium in warm air slowly burns, and the burning made more heat.

Nobody could see it. On Wednesday most of the pile should have been cooling, but one graphite thermometer, in channel 20/53, kept climbing all day: 255 degrees in the morning, 405 by ten at night. The physicist shut the chimney door so that the chimney would draw a little air, and when that was not enough he opened the fan dampers for a few minutes at a time, four times during the night. Every reading came down except 20/53, where the rise was only stopped. The dampers were doing two things at once. They cooled the graphite, and they blew air on the fire.

At 5:40 on Thursday morning, after the longest damper opening, the meter at the chimney filters jumped. The physicist on duty noted it and did nothing, because he thought it was just the first air going up the stack after three days of stillness. By noon 20/53 read 428 degrees. The dampers were opened twice more and the chimney meter jumped again, much higher. Now everyone thought a fuel can had burst. The pile had a scanner for finding burst cans, but it needed air flowing through the pile, so the fans were switched on, and then the scanner turned out to be jammed. So the men went to look.

## The fire

At half past four on Thursday afternoon, October 10th, men in protective clothing on the charge hoist pulled the plug out of the hole for the four channels numbered 21/53. The uranium inside was glowing red. The graphite "appeared to have its normal colour". They tried to push the burning fuel out of the back of the pile into the water duct behind it, but the cans were stuck fast. Then they pulled more plugs, and found that it was not four channels on fire but about 150.

They could not use the big fans to cool the pile, because that would blow radioactive smoke over the whole district. They pushed at the burning channels with steel rods all night, and when the rods ran out they fetched scaffolding poles from the building site next door. "They jammed solid," the deputy manager, Tom Tuohy, said afterwards. They emptied a ring of channels all round the fire, and then a second ring, and then a third row above it as the fire tried to climb. Looking down through holes in the roof, seventy feet up, a man saw a red glow at a quarter to seven in the evening, bright flames at half past seven, yellow flames at eight and blue flames at half past eight. Blue meant the graphite itself was burning.

That left water, and water was frightening. Hot uranium takes the oxygen out of water and leaves hydrogen, which can explode. "We were quite honestly frightened of the water," Tuohy told the inquiry, "because we didn't know whether there would be an explosion or not." By midnight the works manager, Davey, had decided that if nothing else worked, water would go in anyway. The fire brigade stood by, the chief constable was warned, and everyone on the site was told to stay indoors and wear a mask. At 1:38 in the morning a thermometer in the fire read 1,000 degrees and a fuel element 1,300.

At 8:55 on Friday morning, October 11th, the water was turned on through two channels above the fire, 300 gallons a minute and then 800. "No dramatic change resulted," the report says; an hour later flames were still coming out of the back of the pile. Then, at ten past ten, someone switched off the fans. Without air the fire "immediately began to subside". The water ran until Saturday afternoon, when the pile was cold. Tuohy went up to the roof again and again to be sure. "I did stand to one side, sort of hopefully," he said years later, "but if you're staring straight at the core of a shut-down reactor you're going to get quite a bit of radiation." He lived to be ninety.

The filters at the top of the chimney, Cockcroft's follies, caught about 95 per cent of the radioactive dust. What got through was mostly a gas, radioactive iodine, which they could not stop.

## The milk

Radioactive iodine falls on grass, cows eat the grass, and the iodine is in their milk the next day. When a child drinks the milk the iodine collects in a small gland in the neck called the thyroid, and too much of it there can cause cancer years later. There was no rule anywhere for how much radioactive iodine milk could safely contain. On Saturday afternoon the milk from Seascale tested far higher than anyone was happy with. That evening the doctors and health physicists sat down with pencils and worked out a limit from scratch, based on how much milk a small child drinks and how small its thyroid is: one tenth of a microcurie per litre. At nine o'clock that night the milk from twelve farms within two miles of Windscale was stopped. By Monday morning the ban covered a strip of coast thirty miles long, about 200 square miles, and the milk from it was poured away for six weeks. The farmers were paid.

Fifty years later, scientists went back over all the measurements and found that about twice as much radioactivity had come out of the chimney as was thought in 1957, and that something nobody had mentioned at the time, polonium, probably did more harm than the iodine. Some estimates say the accident caused about a hundred cancers across Britain and Europe. But in 2024 a study of every child born in that part of Cumberland in the 1950s could find no extra thyroid cancer at all. The limit worked out that Saturday night had held. The companion piece "[The milk](/deeper/seven-feet/the-milk/#young)" tells the whole of this part of the story.

## The report

A committee of inquiry under Sir William Penney sat at Windscale for ten days and reported on October 26th 1957. It said the cause was the second heating, "too soon and too rapidly applied". It said the thermometers "were correctly positioned for normal operations, but not for Wigner releases". It said there was no operating manual, that the organisation was too thin, and that temperatures had been pushed up over the years "without complete realisation of all the technical factors involved". And it said that the men who fought the fire had been "prompt and efficient" and had shown "considerable devotion to duty", and that thanks to them "a worse accident was averted".

The Authority wanted the report published in full. The Prime Minister, Harold Macmillan, had just come back from Washington, where he was trying to persuade the Americans to share their atomic secrets with Britain again. On November 8th 1957 he published a shorter version instead, called a White Paper, with a note saying that "it would not be in the national interest to publish the report". The White Paper said the accident had happened during "a routine maintenance operation", and that it was due partly to the instruments and "partly to faults of judgment by the operating staff". Both piles were shut down for good. The full report was opened to the public in January 1988, thirty years later. The companion piece "[The White Paper](/deeper/seven-feet/the-white-paper/#young)" tells what was published and what was not.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A thermometer only tells you the temperature where it is.** The thermometers at Windscale were in the right place for a running reactor and the wrong place for an anneal, seven feet behind the hottest spot. For five years the men on the roof read a number that was true, and it was the wrong number. Before you trust a measurement, ask whether the instrument is where the thing you care about is happening.

**Doing something eight times is not the same as understanding it.** There was no manual, only a hundred-word memo and "traditions". Each anneal was a little different from the last: longer gaps, higher temperatures, new kinds of fuel. Together they turned a routine into an experiment nobody knew they were running.

**A test can switch off the very things that would show it going wrong.** The pile had a scanner to find burst cans and fans to cool it. The anneal needed still air, so both were turned off, and nothing was left that could find a smouldering can. When you design a test, ask what will warn you if it fails, and whether the test itself has turned that warning off.

**When the instrument finally speaks, listen for something new.** The chimney meter jumped at 5:40 on Thursday morning. It was the first true signal in three days, and it was read as the normal effect of the first air going up the stack.

**A lesson kept secret is not a lesson.** The report said where the thermometers should have been and what the organisation had got wrong. The public were told it was a routine job and the operators had made errors of judgment. Everyone who ran a reactor for the next thirty years could have used what was in that file.

**The one thing that worked was decided in time.** Nobody had a rule for iodine in milk, so on a Saturday night the doctors made one, from first principles, before anyone had been hurt. It held for sixty years. Knowing what you do not know, and working it out before it is too late, is the best preparation there is.

So the lesson is simple. Put your instruments where the danger is, not where it used to be, and write down what you learn so that the next person can read it.
