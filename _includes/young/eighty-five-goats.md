## A diver on the bottom

On the morning of August 31st 1906 a small warship called HMS *Spanker* was anchored at the mouth of Loch Striven, a sea loch on the west coast of Scotland. On her deck a diver was being dressed: a canvas suit, heavy boots, a copper helmet with a round glass window in the front. At eight minutes past eleven the glass was screwed shut and he went over the side.

Two minutes later he was sitting on the bottom, 210 feet down, in soft mud and total darkness. Above him, eighteen sailors took turns at three hand pumps, keeping the air coming down the hose into his helmet. After about five minutes he was called up.

He came up fast, 126 feet in four minutes, and then he stopped at 90 feet and waited. He went up a little, waited, went up a little, waited again: seven stops in all. Fifty-six minutes after he had gone in, he was back on deck, and nothing was wrong with him.

The diver was Lieutenant Guybon Damant of the Royal Navy, who was twenty-five and had never been so deep in his life. That afternoon a second diver, Gunner Catto, did the same dive.

The important number in that story is not 210. It is 90, the depth of the first stop. It is about half the pressure, and the rule that a diver can come straight up to half the pressure on him, and must then go slowly, had been worked out that summer in London, in a steel tank, on goats.

## The man who studied bad air

John Scott Haldane was a Scottish doctor who worked at Oxford University. He was forty-six in 1906 and he was the country's expert on bad air. When a coal mine exploded, he went down afterwards to find out what gas had killed the men who had survived the blast. His way of working was always the same: find the gas, measure it, and then ask what the body does with it.

Around 1905 the Navy asked for his help. Below about 70 feet, a diver who tried to do any real work would often become exhausted or pass out. And divers who went deep and came back up sometimes collapsed on deck, or lost the use of their legs, or died. The Navy set up a committee to find out why, and Haldane was its scientist. Its other members were a ship's surgeon called Oswald Rees and young Lieutenant Damant, who had learned to dive because gunnery officers were put in charge of the divers. A pathologist called Arthur Boycott joined to look after the animals. A chemical manufacturer called Ludwig Mond gave the Lister Institute, a research laboratory in Chelsea, a steel pressure chamber: a tank strong enough to hold air squeezed to many times its normal pressure.

## Two questions

The first question was the easy one. Why did divers pass out at 70 feet? Haldane had the divers fill bottles with the air from inside their helmets. The bottles held far too much carbon dioxide, the gas we breathe out. The reason was arithmetic. A diver's pump pushed down a certain amount of air each minute, but at 100 feet the water squeezes air to a quarter of its size, so the diver was only getting a quarter as much air as the pump seemed to send. His own breathed-out gas built up in the helmet. The fix was a rule: pump more air the deeper the diver goes, and test the pumps for leaks before every dive. Once that was done, the divers were comfortable even at 210 feet.

The second question was the one that killed people. A French scientist called Paul Bert had shown in 1878 what the "bends" were. Under pressure, nitrogen, the gas that makes up most of the air, dissolves into a diver's blood and body, the way gas is dissolved into a bottle of fizzy drink. When the pressure comes off, the gas comes out as bubbles, just as the drink fizzes when you take off the cap. Everybody agreed the answer was to come up slowly. The Navy's rule was to rise at a steady five feet a minute. But divers were still dying.

Haldane did not think "slowly and steadily" was the right answer. He pictured the body not as one tank filling with nitrogen but as many tanks filling at different speeds. The blood fills in seconds. Fat fills over hours. So a diver coming up at a steady rate was still soaking up nitrogen in his slow parts while he was giving it off from his fast parts. A steady rise, Haldane wrote, "is needlessly slow at the beginning and usually dangerously quick near the end."

Then he noticed something that everyone who worked in compressed air already knew. A man who has been in air at twice normal pressure, which is what you get at 33 feet of sea water, can come straight up without any trouble, however long he has been there. Haldane's idea was that this was not about 33 feet. It was about the ratio. If you could safely go from two atmospheres to one, you could safely go from four to two, or from six to three.

The idea had two parts. First, that the body can always stand having its pressure halved. Second, that a safe set of stops could be calculated from the idea of fast and slow parts. The first could be tested in a tank. The second could only be tested by working out a table, sending a man down on it, and seeing whether he came back.

## Why goats

The chamber at the Lister Institute was a piece of a steel boiler lying on its side, about seven feet across inside, with a round hatch at one end, three small windows, a telephone, electric lights, and a little airlock for passing food in.

Haldane's team first tried mice, rats, guinea-pigs and rabbits. They were almost useless. A mouse's blood goes round so fast that it gets rid of its nitrogen before any bubbles can form. You could keep mice at six times normal pressure for an hour and let the air out in under a minute, and they were fine. That same treatment "was invariably fatal to goats." The thing Haldane needed to study was how slowly a large body gives up gas, and he needed a large animal to study it. Goats were the biggest animals he could get in numbers and handle in the chamber. And they show small symptoms, which a mouse cannot.

The scientists said goats were "somewhat stupid and definitely insensitive to pain," but that anyone who knew them could tell when one felt unwell. There were eighty-five of them, "a mixed collection of ordinary English goats of no particular breed." Five to eight went into the chamber at a time. The pressure was raised, held for anything from a few minutes to four hours, and then let out, by whichever method was being tested. Then the goats were let out into the yard and watched for half an hour. The scientists found that the goats showed their symptoms best when they were bored: the trick, they wrote, was "to allow the animals to fall into a state of meditative boredom."

The commonest symptom was that a goat held one leg up off the ground and did not want to put weight on it. The scientists called this the goat's version of the "bends," the joint pain that tunnel workers and divers got. Worse symptoms were paralysis of the back legs, and struggling for breath, which meant bubbles had reached the heart and lungs. The worst was death, which came within minutes when it came.

The first summer's work, 164 experiments on thirty-four goats, taught them mostly how not to do it; the scientists admitted in print that the methods were "so ill-devised that no very definite results were obtained." Five goats died. But they learned something important. Goats differed enormously. Some got the bends almost every time; some never did, and nobody could predict which. The difference between one goat and another was bigger than the difference between the methods they wanted to compare. They did not have thousands of goats, so they changed the plan: every goat got the gentlest treatment first and the harshest last, so that the same animals could be compared with themselves, and as few as possible would die.

## Six atmospheres to three

The main test went like this. Goats were kept at six times normal pressure, the pressure at 168 feet of sea water, and then the pressure was suddenly dropped to a bit less than half. That drop was made about two hundred times. Not one goat showed a symptom.

Then the same goats were given the same size of drop, but starting lower down the scale: from about four and a half atmospheres straight to normal. The results were "very disastrous." About a fifth of the goats died, a third were seriously ill, a third had bends, and only a fifth escaped. The same fall in pressure was harmless at one end of the scale and deadly at the other. What mattered was the ratio, just as Haldane had guessed.

The second test compared his method of stops with the old steady rise. Goats were kept for fifteen minutes at six atmospheres and brought out in thirty-one minutes, either by stops or at a steady rate. By stops, eighteen goats in thirty-four trials had five cases of bends and nothing worse. By the steady rise, in thirty-six trials, one goat died, two were paralysed, one was seriously ill, and eleven more had bends.

Before any diver was tried, the goats' schedules were tried on men. In the last week of July 1906, Damant and Catto went into the Chelsea chamber seven times, at higher pressure each time, up to nearly six and a half atmospheres, and came out by Haldane's stops. Nothing went wrong, apart from itchy forearms.

HMS *Spanker* arrived at Rothesay, on the Isle of Bute, on the evening of August 20th 1906. The next day was spent testing the pumps and the pressure gauges against the sea itself. Then the two divers went deeper by a few fathoms every day: 90 feet, then 138, then 150, then 162, then 174, then 180. On August 28th Catto, working in the dark at 180 feet, got tangled in a coil of wire and could not come up for twenty minutes. The pumps could not keep up with him and he was nearly overcome by his own carbon dioxide. He was brought up slowly, in nine stops over an hour and a half, and was perfectly well. On August 31st both men went to 210 feet, and that is where this story began.

Haldane's rules went into the Navy's diving manual in 1907, with two tables of stops: one for ordinary dives, and a second for the diver who had been stuck on the bottom for a long time. In 1908 the three scientists published everything, 101 pages of it, in the *Journal of Hygiene*. Nobody had tested the halving rule above six atmospheres, they said, "as no experimental data exist."

## The diver the first table did not describe

The first table, for ordinary dives, was never wrong. The second was proved in the hardest possible way.

Soon after the tables came into use, Navy divers were working on a torpedo boat that had sunk in 150 feet of water. They were working in spells of twenty minutes and coming up by the first table, which was the only one yet in use. Then one diver's lines were caught over a spar of the wreck, one each way, so that he could not reach either to free it. A second diver could not free him against the tide. He was down for two and a half hours before the tide slackened.

Rees and Damant brought him up by stops over two and a half hours, which they then believed was enough. He was forty-nine years old and heavily built, and he was so exhausted that he could not climb the rope; he hung on his line through every stop. He was put to bed on the ship, and about a day after he came up, he died. The doctors found bubbles in his heart and his liver.

The scientists put it in their paper and said what it meant: the long stops in their second table were "none too long," and the real danger was a long stay on the bottom at all. The table had been written for the dive they imagined. The man who died was on a dive it had not imagined.

## What came after

The American Navy tested Haldane's tables from 1912 and adopted them. In April 1915, when the submarine F-4 sank off Honolulu in 300 feet of water, American divers went down to it on Haldane's stops and came up well. One of them, who got stuck for three hours and then climbed too fast against his orders, collapsed at 40 feet. He was put into a pressure chamber on deck; he came back to life "as pressure reached 75 pounds," and after some weeks of illness he recovered.

Haldane wrote in 1922 that in the twelve years since his method went into the Navy, the bends had "practically disappeared" except where the rules were not followed. Damant became the Navy's Inspector of Diving, and later led the divers who recovered 44 tons of gold from a sunken liner called the *Laurentic*. He died in 1963. Haldane died in 1936.

The idea behind the tables is still in use. In the 1930s the American Navy found that two-to-one was too generous for the fast parts of the body and too cautious for the slow ones, and gave each part its own number. Every set of diving tables since, and the computer that a diver wears on the wrist today, is Haldane's model with the numbers adjusted.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Choose an instrument that can show you the answer.** Haldane's first discovery was about mice, not divers. A mouse cannot get the bends, because its body gives up gas too fast. If he had used mice, he would have found that fast decompression was safe, and it would have been true for mice and false for men. The animal you pick decides what your experiment can tell you.

**Go a step at a time, and write down where you stopped.** The halving rule was tested on goats at six atmospheres, then on two men in the chamber, then on the same two men in a loch, a few fathoms deeper each day. And the scientists wrote down exactly where their evidence ended: above six atmospheres, no data. Fifty years later another navy went from 205 feet to 610 in one step, in the story called *Six Hundred Feet*, and paid for it.

**When your subjects differ more than your methods do, change the plan.** Some goats always got the bends and some never did, and that difference was bigger than the difference between the two methods being compared. So the scientists compared each goat with itself, and said in their paper that this was a weaker test than they would have liked.

**The rule that fails is the one for the case you did not imagine.** The ordinary table never hurt anyone. The diver who died was stuck for hours, tired, heavy and middle-aged, and the stops that seemed generous for him were not enough. The scientists made the table longer and said why.

**A calculation you cannot check is a claim.** Another scientist, Leonard Hill, pointed out that the arithmetic behind Haldane's tables was never published, and that the "fast parts" and "slow parts" of the body were an idea, not something you could find with a knife. The tables worked, and they have been corrected ever since.

So the lesson is simple. Test your idea on something that can show you the answer, go deeper a little at a time, and when you write it up, say exactly how far your evidence goes and no further.
