## The man and the city

Los Angeles is a city in a dry place. It only rains about fifteen inches a year there, and in some years only seven or eight. By the 1920s it was growing faster than almost any city in the world, and all of its water had to be brought from somewhere else.

The man who brought it was William Mulholland. He was born in Belfast in 1855, went to sea at fifteen, and came to Los Angeles in 1878, when it was a town of about ten thousand people. He got a job as a ditch tender for the private water company, taught himself engineering from books at night, and by 1886 he was running the company. When the city bought the water works in 1902 he came with them as chief engineer. Between 1904 and 1913 he built the Los Angeles Aqueduct, a channel 233 miles long that carried a river from the Owens Valley across the desert to the city. People called him "the Chief". By 1928 he had been in charge of the city's water for forty-two years and had built nineteen dams. Seventeen of them were made of earth or rock. Two were made of concrete.

Above him was a board of commissioners, businessmen and lawyers. None of them was an engineer, and they did not think they needed one. The state of California had a law that said a dam's plans had to be approved by the state engineer, but the law let cities off. So nobody outside the city's own water department checked anything Mulholland built.

## The dam

The aqueduct brought water to the city all year, but the city used more in summer than in winter. So Mulholland wanted a reservoir near the end of the aqueduct, a lake where the winter water could be stored. He chose a canyon called San Francisquito, forty-five miles north of the city, with a creek running down it.

The dam he built there was called the St Francis Dam. It was a gravity dam: a wall of concrete so thick and heavy that its own weight holds the water back. It was curved, like a bow, and its downstream side rose in steps five feet high. It was about 200 feet high and 700 feet long, and from its west end a lower wall, called a dike, ran another 500 feet along a ridge. Behind it the reservoir could hold 38,000 acre-feet of water, which is about twelve billion gallons.

Three things about the dam matter for this story.

First, nobody designed it for that canyon. The water department had just built another concrete dam above Hollywood, on sandstone, and the office engineers took the drawings and the calculations for that dam and used them again. One of them told the inquest afterwards that "there were no changes in the design to fit the different conditions."

Second, the ground was not sandstone. On the east side of the canyon the rock was a kind called mica schist, which splits into thin flakes. On the west side it was a red rock called conglomerate, which is pebbles and sand stuck together with clay. The two kinds of rock met along an old fault line that ran right under the dam. Mulholland had looked at the ground himself. He had dug holes in the red rock, filled them with water, and found that the water stayed there. He had even dug a tunnel into the ridge and said that if he could build a dam as good as that ridge, "I would be through making a dam". He never asked a geologist to look at it.

Third, the dam was made taller while it was being built. The first plan was for a dam 175 feet above the creek. In 1924 ten feet were added, and in 1925 another ten. But the bottom of the dam was not made any wider. A gravity dam is held in place by its weight, and the weight it needs depends on how deep the water is. Nobody worked the sums out again.

Building began in 1924 and finished in May 1926. The reservoir began to fill before the dam was even done.

## The leaks

Every dam leaks a little. Mulholland knew that, and he had a rule for reading leaks. If the water coming out from under a dam is muddy, it means the water is cutting away the earth underneath, and that is dangerous. If the water is clear, the dam is fine. The department measured the leaks every day over a little notch called a weir, and Mulholland said the St Francis was "the driest dam of its size I ever saw in my life".

In 1926 the reservoir was filled a third of the way. In 1927 it was filled to within three feet of the top and then let down again. In early 1928 it was filled all the way, for the first time. On March 5th 1928 the water stood three inches below the lip of the spillway, and there it stayed.

As the water rose, the leaks grew. Water came out of the hillside below the dike. A new leak came out at the foot of the dike and had to be piped away. Cracks in the dam itself were stuffed with rope and pumped full of cement. On Saturday March 10th a rancher named Chester Smith, driving up the canyon, stopped his car to look at "considerable water coming out on the west bank" and shouted down to the keeper's assistant, as a joke, "what are you sons of guns going to do here, going to flood us out down below?" The assistant joked back: "We expect this dam to go out any minute."

On the morning of Monday March 12th the dam keeper, Tony Harnischfeger, telephoned the office. There was a new leak on the west side, and the water looked muddy. Mulholland and his assistant, Harvey Van Norman, drove out at once and were at the dam by half past ten. They walked round it with the keeper for two hours. The new leak was coming out of the west hillside and running across the loose dirt of an old road, and that was what had made it look muddy. Where it came out of the ground, Mulholland said, "it was as clear as glass". Van Norman climbed up close to it and saw the same. It was a big leak, about a cubic foot of water every second, which was more than all the other leaks put together. But it was clear. "It never occurred to me that it was in danger," Mulholland told the inquest later. "I just wanted to see the new leak, naturally." The two men drove back to the city and had lunch.

Chester Smith drove his cattle past the dam that same afternoon and saw the leak still running. "I was a little suspicious, to tell you the truth," he said afterwards, "and left my door open." That night he slept in his barn with the door open.

## The night

On top of the dam was a machine that kept a record of the water level. A float in a pipe moved a pen, and the pen drew a line on a sheet of paper wound round a drum that turned by clockwork. All week the line had been straight. At about half past eleven that Monday night the line began to go down, slowly at first. Then, just before midnight, it went straight down off the paper.

The last people to see the dam were workers from the power house up the canyon, driving home along the road that ran past its east end. At about twenty-five past eleven a nurse named Katherine Spann drove past with a friend and noticed nothing wrong. "It is pretty quiet tonight," she said to him, "terribly quiet." At about ten to twelve a carpenter named Ace Hopewell rode past on a motorcycle. A mile and a half farther on he heard a rumbling behind him, stopped, and smoked a cigarette, listening. He thought it was a landslide.

At two minutes to midnight a power line in the canyon went dead. That is the moment the dam broke. Nobody who saw it happen lived to say what it looked like. The keeper, his six-year-old son and the woman who lived with them were the first to die.

Twelve billion gallons of water went down the canyon in a wall more than a hundred feet high, moving at eighteen miles an hour. A mile and a half below the dam it hit the power house and the cottages where the workers' families slept. Out of about 120 people there, only three lived: a man named Ray Rising, who lost his wife and three daughters, and a woman named Lillian Curtis and her little boy. Three miles farther down, Chester Smith's dog barked. He woke, heard the trees breaking and the wires snapping, ran to the next house shouting "The dam is broke," and ran up the hill with his neighbours. "As we ran up the hill, the water was right behind us."

The flood came out of the canyon into the valley of the Santa Clara River and followed the river fifty-four miles to the sea. It reached a camp of construction workers at twenty past one in the morning and killed more than eighty of the 140 men sleeping there in tents. It reached the town of Santa Paula at ten past three. By the time it got to the sea at five o'clock it was two miles wide. Nobody knows exactly how many people died, because many were never found. The official count that summer was 385. Later counts say more than 430. The marker at the site says more than 450.

## What happened next

When the sun came up there was one piece of the dam left standing in the middle of the canyon, about ninety feet wide, with its two ends sheared off. The whole west side was gone, and the blocks of concrete, some weighing ten thousand tons, lay half a mile down the canyon. On the east side the broken blocks lay almost where they had fallen, and the hillside above them was still sliding.

Within a week six different groups of engineers and geologists were studying the wreck. The governor of California appointed one. The city council appointed one. The district attorney appointed one. The water board hired three geologists. The valley's water district hired its own experts. And the county coroner held an inquest: a court hearing to find out how people had died, with a jury of nine men, six of them engineers and builders.

They all found the same thing. The dam was built on the wrong rock. The red conglomerate on the west side looked like rock when it was dry, but when a piece was put in a glass of water it fizzed, fell apart, and turned into a pile of sand and muddy water within an hour. The governor's commission said that with ground like that "the ultimate failure of this dam was inevitable". The district attorney's board said no safe dam could ever have been built there. Only one report, by the geologist Bailey Willis, said something different: that the east side of the dam had been built on an old landslide, and that the landslide had moved. People still argue about which side went first.

The coroner's jury said one more thing. It said that the mistake in engineering belonged to Mulholland and his department, but that the bigger mistake belonged to everyone who had let one man decide everything. "The construction and operation of a great dam," it wrote, "should never be left to the sole judgment of one man, no matter how eminent." The jury did not recommend that anyone be prosecuted. The companion pieces "[On the stand](/deeper/three-inches/on-the-stand/#young)" and "[Six reports](/deeper/three-inches/six-reports/#young)" tell what the inquest and the reports said.

Mulholland did not argue. He went to the inquest on its first day and answered every question. Asked if he would build the dam again, he said, "I will answer that frankly and say, I would not." When he was called back later he wept on the stand, and he ended with words that are still quoted: "Don't blame anybody else, you just fasten it on me. If there is an error of human judgment, I was the human."

The city paid for the damage without going to court. Mulholland resigned in November 1928, at seventy-three, and died in 1935. In 1929 California passed a new law that put every dam in the state, including cities' dams, under the state engineer. The piece of the dam that was left standing was blown up in May 1929.

## The clear water

There is one more finding, and it is the heart of this story. An engineer from Pasadena took a sample of the leaking water just after the break and had a chemist test it. The water looked clean. But it was carrying ten times as much salt as the water that had gone into the reservoir, and it was full of dissolved gypsum. Gypsum is a mineral that runs through the red rock in veins, and water dissolves it slowly, the way it dissolves sugar. For fifteen months the clear water had been quietly carrying the rock away in solution. An engineering magazine worked out that it could have removed about a hundred tons of rock without ever turning muddy. "Only a chemical test," it wrote, "will show what clear water really contains."

Mulholland's rule, that muddy water is dangerous and clear water is safe, was true as far as it went. But the rock under his dam could be washed away without making the water muddy, so the one test he trusted could not see the danger. He went to look at the leak himself, on the very morning it was reported, and the leak told him exactly what he expected to hear.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A test can only find what it was built to find.** Mulholland had a weir to measure how much water leaked, and his own eyes to see whether it was muddy. The amount was small and the water was clear, so both tests said the dam was fine. Neither test could see the gypsum dissolving in the water, and that was the thing that mattered. If you are checking that something is safe, ask what your test cannot see.

**A plan that worked in one place has not been proved for another.** The dam was designed for sandstone above Hollywood and then built on schist and conglomerate forty miles away, across a fault, without anyone doing the sums again. Then it was made twenty feet taller without being made wider. Each change seemed small. Nobody checked whether the old answer was still right.

**The reading you want to believe is the dangerous one.** Mulholland had a rule, and the rule said clear water meant safety. So when he saw clear water he was reassured, even though the leak had doubled in a fortnight and the keeper had thought it muddy enough to telephone about. Chester Smith had no rule at all. He just saw a big leak and left his door open. The difference between them was not that one knew more. It was that one of them had an idea to protect.

**One person's judgement is only one judgement.** Mulholland was probably the best water engineer in the West, and he had built nineteen dams that stood. That is exactly why nobody checked him: not his board, not the city, not the state, whose law let cities off. The jury's lesson was not that he was careless. It was that no one, however good, should be the only person reading the instrument.

So the lesson is simple. Before you trust a test, ask what it cannot see, and make sure someone other than the person who built the thing is looking at the results.
