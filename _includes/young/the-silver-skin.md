## A disease with no known cause

In the 1880s a disease called beriberi was killing people all over the Dutch East Indies, the colony that is now Indonesia. It started in the legs. A man's feet went numb and weak, then his legs, then his whole body, until he could not stand, and sometimes his heart gave out. It was worst among people who were fed by somebody else: soldiers, sailors, prisoners and plantation workers. In the war the Dutch were fighting in Atjeh, at the top of Sumatra, soldiers went to the front healthy and came back six weeks later unable to walk.

Nobody knew what caused it. In those years scientists had just learned that many diseases are caused by microbes, tiny living things too small to see without a microscope, and they expected beriberi to be the same. In 1886 the Dutch government sent two professors from Utrecht, Pekelharing and Winkler, to the Indies to find the microbe. With them went a young army doctor called Christiaan Eijkman. He was twenty-eight. He had already been to Java once, caught malaria, and gone home to recover, and while recovering he had worked in the laboratory of Robert Koch in Berlin, the most famous microbe-hunter in the world.

The professors worked for less than a year. They found a kind of bacterium in the blood of beriberi patients and wrote that it seemed "scarcely possible" that it was not the cause. Then they went home. Eijkman stayed. In January 1888 he became the director of a small laboratory inside the army hospital at Weltevreden, a district of Batavia, with the job of finishing what the professors had started: finding the microbe.

## The chickens

The laboratory kept about six chickens. In February 1889 Eijkman injected them with bacteria grown from the blood of a man who had just died of beriberi, and waited to see if they would get the disease.

For months nothing happened. Then, on July 10th 1889, a big strong cock fell ill. It walked unsteadily, then fell over, then could not lift its head, and three days later it was dead. It looked very like beriberi. Soon other chickens were falling ill too. Eijkman did what a microbe-hunter does: he took blood from the dead birds, injected it into healthy birds, and watched them get sick. But something was wrong with the experiment. He bought new chickens and injected some with blood and left others alone, as a comparison. All of them got sick, the injected ones and the others alike. He put birds in separate cages so they could not catch anything from each other. They got sick anyway.

So he decided the laboratory's ground itself must be infected, and he moved the next experiment to a different place. And just then, as he wrote, "the disease suddenly came to a halt" in the laboratory yard. No more birds fell ill. The sick ones got better. He had lost his experiment, and he asked why.

The answer was in the kitchen. For months the man who looked after the chickens had been feeding them leftover cooked rice from the hospital kitchen, fetched every day. That had started on June 10th. The disease had started on July 10th, a month later. On November 20th the kitchen stopped giving out the rice. The last chicken died on November 22nd, and then the disease was gone. Forty years later, when he was an old man, Eijkman told the rest of the story: the hospital had got a new cook, and the new cook refused to let army rice be fed to the laboratory's chickens, which belonged to the civilian government. The cook, who knew nothing about any experiment, had ended it.

## Testing the rice

Eijkman was a careful man, and he did not believe a coincidence until he had tested it. On the new ground he put ten healthy chickens in bamboo cages in pairs. Six of them got the laboratory's usual feed, which was raw rice. Four got nothing but the hospital's cooked rice. The six stayed well. The four got sick, within three to five weeks. Then he switched the sick birds back to raw rice, and "from that day they steadily improved." He did it again, and again. The result was always the same. "It is therefore beyond reasonable doubt," he wrote, "that the cause of the disease lies in the feeding with cooked rice."

For the next six years he changed one thing at a time and let the chickens answer. He cooked the rice in pure distilled water in a clean dish, in case the hospital's well water was to blame: the birds still got sick. He fed them rice from his own dinner table: sick. He had fresh grain pounded in front of him every morning, in case old rice went bad in storage: sick. He fed them sago and tapioca, which are starches like rice: sick. He fed them raw meat and nothing else: they got better.

The most important clue came from the cheap "red rice" the laboratory had always fed its chickens. Red rice is rice that has had its hard outer husk knocked off but still has a thin inner coat on each grain, which the Dutch called the silver skin. White table rice, the kind people ate, was polished in a mill until the silver skin came off too, so the grains were smooth and white. Birds fed red rice never got sick. Birds fed white rice did. And when Eijkman fed sick birds white rice that had kept its silver skin, they recovered. The difference, he wrote, "bears no relation to the kind of rice, but to whether or not the silver skin has been kept."

## The wrong explanation

So Eijkman knew, by 1896, that polished rice gave chickens a disease like beriberi and that the silver skin prevented and cured it. The question was why. And here he went wrong, in a way that is the whole point of this story.

In those days every doctor knew that nerves get damaged when something attacks them: a poison such as lead or arsenic or alcohol, or poisons made by microbes. Nobody had ever thought that a nerve could get sick because something was *missing* from the food. There was no word for that idea. The word "vitamin" would not be invented until 1912.

Eijkman actually wrote the right idea down. In his big paper of 1896 he said there were two possible explanations: either the silver skin stopped microbes getting at the grain in the gut, or else it contained "substances which are indispensable for the preservation of life and health" that the white kernel lacked. He tested the first idea and found it was wrong. That should have left the second. But then he thought about his other experiments. Every starch gave the disease; meat delayed it but did not stop it; birds that were starved did not get it at all. The birds were not starving. So, he reasoned, the disease must be a poisoning after all, and the poison must come from the starch, and the silver skin must contain something that "neutralises" the poison: an antidote. That is how he summed it up, and that is the idea he carried for the next thirty years.

He even described, in the same paper, a protective substance that the body stores up and that gets "gradually used up" when a bird eats polished rice. That is almost exactly what a vitamin is. But he described it as an antidote being used up fighting a poison, because an antidote was a thing his science had a name for, and a missing nutrient was not.

## The prisons

Eijkman fell ill and had to leave Java in March 1896. A few days before he left, a colleague came to see him. Adolphe Vorderman was the inspector of the government's medical service for Java, and part of his job was inspecting the prisons. He had read about the chickens and he had noticed something: in prisons where the prisoners were fed red rice, he had never seen beriberi, and in prisons where they ate white rice, he had.

Vorderman was careful too. Instead of saying what he thought, he wrote to every local governor asking two separate questions, what the prisoners ate and whether beriberi occurred, without hinting that the two might be connected. The answers were striking. Of 63 prisons serving white rice, 34 had beriberi. Of 27 serving red rice, 1 did. On March 18th 1896 the government ordered him, in a secret letter, to go and check for himself.

From April to September 1896 he travelled round Java and Madura, prison by prison, about a hundred of them. He did not tell anyone what he was looking for, so that the Chinese contractors who supplied the prisons' food would not change the rice before he arrived. In every prison he took a sample of rice and sealed it. The samples went back to the laboratory in Batavia marked only with a letter or a number, and two doctors there sorted them into kinds of rice without knowing which prison each came from. Only after every prison had been sorted were the beriberi figures handed over. That way nobody's hopes could nudge the result.

The result covered exactly 100 prisons and about 282,000 prisoners. Among the 96,500 prisoners fed unpolished rice there had been 9 cases of beriberi, all in one prison. Among the 150,000 fed polished rice there had been 4,201 cases. That is about 300 times as many. Eijkman put it to the Royal Academy of Sciences in Holland the next year as a simpler sum: on polished rice, one prisoner in 39 got beriberi; on unpolished rice, one in 10,000. The companion piece "[The hundred prisons](/deeper/the-silver-skin/the-hundred-prisons/#young)" tells how the survey was done.

## What happened next

You might think that settled it. It did not. For two years the newspapers in the Indies and in Holland argued about which *poison* in rice caused beriberi: was it in rice imported from Saigon, or in stale rice, or in all rice? The army tried boiling rice bran into the soup of 1,600 soldiers, who would not eat it. A trial of red rice in the prison at Soerabaja nearly collapsed over a contract. The companion piece "[The paper war](/deeper/the-silver-skin/the-paper-war/#young)" follows the quarrel.

And Eijkman himself, who had the chickens, the prisons and the cure, still did not believe that beriberi came from the food. In 1898 he wrote that an investigation "in the direction indicated by the infection theory still has the best chance of success." He could see that beriberi came and went with the seasons and with particular buildings, the way infections do. He did not yet see that the rice contracts came and went too. He knew what to do in practice, and said so: "The main thing is that the grains are in possession of the silver skin." He even worked out that feeding the prisoners meat instead, as some people wanted, would cost about a million guilders a year, while the right rice cost nothing extra. But he thought he had found an antidote, not the answer, and the government did not change the prison food for years.

In Japan, by the way, the navy had already cured its beriberi in 1884 by changing the sailors' rations. Its chief doctor, Takaki, thought the cure worked by adding protein. That reason was wrong, and Eijkman could show it was wrong, so he did not trust the cure either. The companion piece "[The navy that was cured first](/deeper/the-silver-skin/the-navy-that-was-cured-first/#young)" tells that story.

## Reading the chickens again

The man who took over the chickens was Gerrit Grijns, an army doctor who had worked in the laboratory before. He was told to find out what the red colouring in red rice did. He ignored the instruction and did something simpler: he took away the starch. Eijkman had decided that starch was the poison's carrier. Grijns fed chickens on foods with no starch in them at all, such as meat preparations, and some of them got the disease anyway. He fed others on beans, with and without starch, and they stayed well. If there was no starch, there was nothing for a poison to be made from. And if there was no poison, there was no need for an antidote.

In 1901 he published a paper of over a hundred pages and said what was left. There were two ways to explain the disease, he wrote: "either one can think of a deficit, of a partial hunger," or one can imagine some agent in nature attacking the nerves. He chose the first. He said that there must be substances in natural foods "which cannot be dispensed with without serious harm to the peripheral nervous system," that different foods contain very different amounts of them, that polished rice has the least, and that the body probably needs only a tiny amount, which is why no chemist had found them. Every one of those guesses turned out to be right. The substance is now called thiamine, or vitamin B1.

## After

It still took years. Doctors in British Malaya ran trials on people between 1907 and 1909 and got the same answer. In 1910 a congress of tropical doctors in Manila agreed that beriberi came from eating polished white rice. In 1912 the chemist Casimir Funk invented the word "vitamine" for substances like this. In 1926 two scientists in the very same laboratory in Batavia, by then a much bigger building, managed to purify the substance from rice bran. They sent Eijkman forty milligrams of it, and he found that two milligrams added to a kilogram of polished rice was enough to protect his pigeons.

In 1929 Christiaan Eijkman was given the Nobel Prize in Medicine "for his discovery of the antineuritic vitamin." He was too ill to travel to Sweden and sent his speech by post. He died the next year. Grijns, who had read the chickens right, was not given the prize. Vorderman had died in 1902.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**You can only understand a result with the ideas you already have.** Eijkman's experiment was right. Polished rice made chickens ill and the silver skin cured them, and he proved it over and over. But in 1889 doctors only knew of two ways a nerve could be damaged: by a poison or by an infection. Nobody had the idea of a disease caused by something missing from food. So Eijkman fitted his right result into the wrong box, and called the silver skin an antidote. He even wrote down the right explanation and then talked himself out of it.

**The question you were sent to answer is the one you keep trying to answer.** Eijkman was sent to find a microbe. Even after seven years of chickens and a hundred prisons, he still thought beriberi was probably an infection, because the disease behaved in some ways like one. He gave up the microbe for the chickens but kept it for the men.

**Sometimes a big careful count tells you what to do before anyone knows why.** Vorderman's survey had no theory in it at all. It had a question asked without hints, samples sorted without names, and 282,000 prisoners. It said: serve the other rice. That was enough to act on, and it was years before anyone fully did, partly because the experts still disagreed about why.

**Someone has to look at the instrument again.** Grijns had the same chickens and the same cages. The one thing he did differently was to take away the ingredient Eijkman had decided was essential. When the disease appeared anyway, the old explanation fell apart, and the new one, that something was missing, was the only one left.

So the lesson is simple. A right experiment is not the end of the work. You also have to be ready for it to mean something your science has no name for yet, and to go back and read it again when the first reading will not fit.
