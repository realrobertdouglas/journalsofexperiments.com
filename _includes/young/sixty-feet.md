## The valley below the sea

In the far south of California, just north of Mexico, there is a huge bowl of desert that lies lower than the ocean. Its bottom is about 280 feet below sea level. Long ago it was the top of the Gulf of California, until the Colorado River filled the gulf's head with mud and cut the bowl off from the sea. Once in a while, in a big flood, the river had spilled over its bank and run down into the bowl and made a lake there for a season; then it went back to the sea, and the lake dried up into a bed of salt.

In 1900 a company called the California Development Company began to bring the river's water into the bowl on purpose, to grow crops. The engineer who had the idea was Charles Rockwood. The man who built the canal was George Chaffey. The canal started just north of the Mexican border, ran south into Mexico, and then used an old dry river bed called the Alamo as its main channel for forty miles, back into California. The first water arrived in 1901. By 1905 about 80,000 acres were being farmed, about 10,000 people lived there, and there were five towns. They called the place the Imperial Valley.

A railway, the Southern Pacific, ran right across the bowl on its way from Yuma to Los Angeles, forty miles of it below sea level. A salt company dug salt at the bottom. And the government's new Reclamation Service was starting a dam of its own on the Colorado above Yuma.

## The problem with mud

The Colorado is a river of mud. Every day its water carried enough silt into the valley's canals to fill them up a little more. By the summer of 1904 the first four miles of the canal, from the river to the Alamo, were so choked that not enough water could get through. Crops were dying. The farmers were suing the company. And the company had almost no money and only two small dredges, which would have needed all winter to dig the four miles out.

So Rockwood did the only thing he could think of. In late September 1904 he had a dredge cut a new opening from the river to the canal, four miles south of the border, below the choked part. The cut was about sixty feet wide and six to eight feet deep. It took about three weeks. Water came through it, and the valley had water for the winter.

What the cut did not have was a gate, which would have let the company decide how much water came in. Rockwood drew plans for one and sent them to Mexico City, because the Mexican government had to approve any such structure. The approval took more than a year to come.

## Why Rockwood thought it was safe

Rockwood knew a cut without a gate was a risk. He explained later why he took it. The railway had kept a record of the river's height at Yuma every day for twenty-seven years. In those twenty-seven years there had been only three winter floods, and never two in the same winter. The big flood came every summer, when the snow melted in the mountains, and the company meant to close the cut before then with brush and earth, the way it had closed a gap beside its main gate every spring for three years. "It was not probable, then," Rockwood wrote, "in the winter of 1905, that there would be any winter flood to enlarge the cut made by us."

The government's engineers thought he was wrong. Frederick Newell, the head of the Reclamation Service, wrote afterwards that the cut was made "much against the advice of the engineers." But the Service had no power to stop it.

## Five floods

The first flood came down the river on about February 2nd 1905. It did no damage. A second came on February 19th. A third came at the end of February. Three winter floods in one month, in a river that had had three in twenty-seven years.

The company tried to close the cut with piles, brush and sand bags. A fourth flood swept the dam away just as it was nearly finished. They built another. A fifth flood, on April 18th, swept that away too. Then the summer flood came, and nothing could be done until it was over. By June the cut was 160 feet wide. By August the whole Colorado River was running through it, down the Alamo, and into the bottom of the bowl, where a new lake was growing. People called it the Salton Sea.

## The railway takes over

In June 1905 the company borrowed $200,000 from the Southern Pacific. The railway's president was Edward Harriman, one of the richest men in America. In return for the loan he got control of the company, and he put one of his own men, Epes Randolph, in charge. Randolph went to look at the break and wired Harriman that it would cost far more than $200,000 to fix. Harriman wired back: "Are you certain you can put the river back into the old channel?" Randolph: "I am certain that it can be done." Harriman: "Go ahead and do it."

It was much harder than anyone thought. Nearly fifty famous engineers came to look at the break, and no two of them agreed on what to do. In November 1905 the company tried a dam of piles and brush across the river; on Thanksgiving Day a flood came down from the Gila, a side river, carrying so much driftwood that you could not see the water, and the dam vanished. Now the cut was 600 feet wide.

Through the winter of 1906 the company built two gates: a huge wooden one right beside the break, 200 feet long, and a concrete one four miles upstream. Then on April 18th 1906 the great earthquake and fire destroyed San Francisco. Randolph found Harriman there, in a temporary office among the smoking ruins, and asked him for another $250,000. Harriman gave it. Rockwood resigned the next day, and Henry Cory, Randolph's assistant, became the company's chief engineer. He was thirty-five.

## The valley under water

The summer flood of 1906 was enormous. The break widened to half a mile. The whole river poured into the valley. The water spread out over the farms south and west of the towns of Calexico and Mexicali, which stand on the border, four feet deep. The people built a levee: a long bank of earth to keep the water out of their houses.

Then something strange happened. The water running down toward the Salton Sea began to cut a channel in the soft ground, and the channel grew into a canyon, and at the head of the canyon was a waterfall that moved upstream, towards the towns, a mile every few days. If the waterfall reached the flooded ground around the towns, all the water would drain into the canyon and the towns would be saved. If the flood rose over the levee first, they would be drowned. It became a race. On the night of April 1st, in a gale, the people of Calexico fought for three days to hold their levee while men in rowing boats dropped dynamite ahead of the waterfall to hurry it along. The waterfall won. The flood drained away. Then the waterfall walked right through Mexicali, at about a foot a minute, and swallowed the railway station, a hotel and a lot of houses.

In nine months the runaway river dug forty-three miles of canyon, a thousand feet wide and fifty feet deep, almost four times as much earth as the whole Panama Canal. The Salton Sea rose nearly seven inches a day in June 1906. The companion piece "[The New River](/deeper/sixty-feet/the-new-river/#young)" tells that part of the story.

## The gate that broke

When the summer flood went down, the company tried again, with everything the railway had. It built a branch railway to the break, opened a quarry, borrowed 300 enormous steel dump cars called battleships, and hired about two thousand Native American people from six tribes in Arizona and Mexico to do the work, because nobody else could stand the heat. The plan was to send the river through the big wooden gate while a dam was built across the break, and then shut the gate's boards.

They wove a mat of brush a hundred feet wide across the river bed, built a railway trestle on top of it, and dumped rock from the trestle. It worked. By October 10th 1906 nearly all the river was going through the wooden gate. But the gate was not strong enough. On October 11th at eleven in the morning, the first rock train went out on a new trestle above the gate and the trestle collapsed under it. At half past two in the afternoon the gate buckled and most of it floated away downstream. The river ran through the gap where the gate had been. Four months of work and $122,000 were gone.

## Rock

Now the only thing standing was the rock dam on the brush mat, which was dry and could be looked at. Every expert had said that rock dumped into the river would sink out of sight in the mud, or that the water running over the pile would pull the top stones off and wash the whole thing away. Neither had happened. So Randolph and Cory did the thing nobody had suggested: they built trestles across the gap and dumped rock into the river from them, train after train, faster than the river could wash it away. In twenty-four days the river was dammed. On November 4th 1906 it went back into its old bed, towards the sea.

It lasted a month. On December 7th another flood came down the Gila. The new levees beside the dam had been built in a hurry, without a proper trench under them, because the railway's money was for the dam and not the levees. The water went under them in ninety places. Within a day the whole river was back in the valley, through a new gap a thousand feet wide. The rock dam itself still stood.

## The President and Mr. Harriman

The Southern Pacific had now spent about two million dollars and said it would spend no more unless someone shared the cost. The farmers promised $950,000 they did not have. Harriman wired President Theodore Roosevelt on December 13th 1906 and said that "it does not seem fair that we should be called upon to do more than join in to help the settlers." Roosevelt wired back on December 20th that the government could do nothing until Congress met, that the whole danger was the company's fault for making the cut, and that it must close the break "without an hour's delay." Harriman answered the same day that the railway did not own the company and had only lent it money. But he gave the order to go ahead, "trusting that the Government, as soon as you can procure the necessary Congressional action, will assist us with the burden." Roosevelt replied: "Am delighted to receive your telegram." The companion piece "[The telegrams](/deeper/sixty-feet/the-telegrams/#young)" gives them all.

## Fifteen days

The second closing began on December 20th. The river tore the new trestles out three times in a month, and by the middle of January there was not a single pile left to buy in southern California. On January 27th 1907 the trestle was finished for the fourth time, and at five in the afternoon the first battleship tipped its rock into the river. For the next fifteen days, a thousand flat cars and three hundred battleships hauled rock from quarries as far as 485 miles away, over two mountain passes. For about ten days the port of Los Angeles sent out almost no freight, because the railway had no cars to spare. The big stones were blown into pieces on the cars with small charges of dynamite; Newell, who watched, said it sounded like artillery. The river had to be lifted eleven feet to get it back over the lip of its old bed. At eleven o'clock on the night of February 10th 1907, it went. One man had been killed in each of the two closings, swept off a car with a big rock. The companion pieces "[Battleships](/deeper/sixty-feet/battleships/#young)" and "[Fifteen days](/deeper/sixty-feet/fifteen-days/#young)" tell how it was done.

## After

The river never got out again. The levees were rebuilt properly, with a railway track on top so that rock could be rushed to any weak spot, and the floods of 1907 and 1909, both bigger than 1906's, did no harm.

Roosevelt told Congress in January 1907 that making the cut "without controlling devices was criminal negligence." The railway asked Congress to pay it back about $1,660,000 for the second closing. An audit cut that to about $1,080,000. The Senate passed a bill for about $770,000. In the House five congressmen called it "a gift of the people's money to the Southern Pacific Railroad Company," and the bill never came to a vote. The railway was never paid a cent. Shortly before he died in 1909, Harriman rode over the levees and a reporter asked whether he was sorry he had spent the money. "No," he said. "This valley was worth saving, wasn't it?"

The company that made the cut went bankrupt. Rockwood wrote his side of the story in 1909. He said that nobody should be called careless "in failing to foresee that which had never happened before."

The Salton Sea reached its biggest size in 1907: 76 feet deep, fifty miles long, 445 square miles. Everyone expected it to dry up. It never did, because the valley's farms drain their waste water into it. It is still there.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A record of the past is not a promise about the future.** Rockwood had twenty-seven years of river readings, and they said that two winter floods in one winter had never happened. He bet the whole valley that it would not happen now. It happened five times. A record only tells you what the river did while someone was watching. If you look at the chart at the top of this page, which covers eight more years, you can see that 1905 was not even unusual.

**A safety device that you have not built yet does not count.** The gate's plans sat in an office in Mexico City for thirteen months waiting for a signature. During those months the company had a drawing and the valley had a hole. Meaning to build a gate protects nobody. The same thing happened at the end: the levees were built without the trench they needed because the money was for the dam, and the river found the gap within a month.

**Watch for the danger you are not used to.** For four years the company's problem had been getting enough water in. Every tool it had was for measuring that. So when it cut the new opening, it watched the water in the canal and was pleased. The thing it should have watched was the width of the cut: sixty feet, then 160, then 600, then half a mile. Nobody's job was to watch it.

**When the experts disagree, the answer may be the thing none of them suggested.** Fifty engineers argued about gates and dams and mattresses. The river was finally stopped by railway men doing what railway men do, dumping rock from trestles, after an accident showed that the rock did not sink after all. Sometimes the proof you need comes from looking carefully at what is already lying there.

**Decide who pays before the test, not after.** The company had no money. The railway spent three million dollars and was never paid. The farmers promised money and never had to give it. The government, whose land it was and whose President ordered the work done, offered to pay a fifth. Nobody had agreed beforehand who would pay if the experiment went wrong, so in the end nobody did.

So the lesson is simple. If you are going to do something that could let a river loose, build the gate first, watch the thing that can go wrong and not just the thing you want, and do not trust the past to keep you safe.
