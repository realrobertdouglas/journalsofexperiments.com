## The rainmaker

Charles Hatfield sold sewing machines. He was born in Kansas in 1875 and grew up in San Diego and Los Angeles. But he had a hobby. In the public library he read books about the weather and tables of how much rain had fallen each year, and in 1902, on top of a windmill on his parents' ranch, he began heating a secret mixture of chemicals in shallow pans so that the fumes rose into the air. He believed the fumes would make more rain fall nearby.

In 1904, during a drought near Los Angeles, some shopkeepers paid him $50 to try it from a wooden tower in the hills. It rained. After that farmers hired him all over the West. He always wrote the deal the same way. One contract he wrote in 1912 was headed "Four Inches of Rain for Four Thousand Dollars. No Rain, No Pay." He was careful to say that he did not make rain: he only "increased" it, he said, when the weather was already favourable.

## The deal

San Diego is a dry city. In 1915 it owned a big reservoir in the mountains called Morena, sixty miles to the east. A reservoir is a lake made by building a dam across a valley to store water for a city. Morena could hold fifteen billion gallons, but in the eighteen years since it was built it had never once been full. At the end of 1915 it held about five billion.

A friend of Hatfield's in San Diego, a real-estate man called Fred Binney, had been telling the city council for years that it should hire him. In December 1915 Hatfield came to the council himself. He offered three deals, and all of them came to $10,000 if he succeeded. The one everyone remembered was this: he would fill Morena reservoir to the top within a year, and the city would pay him $10,000 when it was full. Nothing if it was not.

Councilman Walter Moore explained why he liked it. "If he fills Morena, he will have put 10 billion gallons into it," he said. "If he fails to fulfill his contract, the city isn't out anything. It's heads the city wins, tails Hatfield loses." The city's lawyer, Terence Cosgrove, was asked whether such a contract would be legal, and he grinned. "If Hatfield fills Morena," he said, "I guess there would be no doubt about the legality."

On December 13th 1915 the council voted, four to one, to accept Hatfield's offer. The minutes of the meeting say the papers were sent to the city attorney (the city's lawyer) "for preparation of a contract." No contract was ever written. And the vote did not say which of the three deals it was accepting. Nobody noticed.

Think about what the city had bought: a test with one lake and one tower. If the lake filled, Hatfield would be paid. But there was no second lake without a tower to compare with, and nobody had written down how much rain Morena would get in an ordinary winter anyway. The only instrument that would be read was the gauge on the dam that showed how deep the water was. A depth gauge can tell you a lake is full. It cannot tell you why.

## The tower and the dams

Hatfield did not wait for a contract. By January 1st 1916 he and his youngest brother Joel had built their tower on a hillside beside the road to Morena dam, about twenty feet high with a platform on top, and were living in a tent at its foot. The only people who saw them regularly were the dam keeper, Seth Swenson, and his wife Maggie, who lived in a cottage at the dam. The brothers raked the ground under the tower after every session so that nobody could find traces of the chemical.

The city owned other dams, and one of them matters to this story. Sixteen miles south-east of San Diego was the Lower Otay dam, built in 1897 by a company president who was a businessman, not an engineer. It was a huge pile of blasted rock, 130 feet high, made waterproof only by a thin wall of steel plate standing up through the middle of it. For its first two years it had no spillway at all. A spillway is the channel that lets extra water out of a reservoir safely when it is full, like the overflow hole in a bath. The spillway they cut later was only 38 feet wide. In nineteen years the Lower Otay reservoir had never been full. The water had never come within fifteen feet of the spillway. Engineers did not like it. As one engineering magazine said afterwards, the company that owned it had always worried far more about not having enough rain to fill it than about a flood that might go over the top.

## The rain

It rained a little at Morena on January 5th. Real rain began on January 10th, and from the 14th it poured. By the 17th the San Diego River was a mile wide across Mission Valley. On the 18th another river washed away a whole village of small farms near the Mexican border, and two women drowned. The newspaper's headline that day was "Is Rainmaker at Work?" The roads to Morena were already washed out, so the paper telephoned the Swensons, who said Hatfield "seemed to be on the job at all hours of the day and considers the downpour due to his efforts."

On the same morning, January 17th, with the valleys under water, the city council put the mayor in charge of helping "persons and property endangered by flood waters", and straight afterwards asked the city attorney for a written opinion on whether the city was legally obliged to pay Hatfield at all, since no contract had been signed. The lake was not yet full, and the city was already working out how not to pay for it.

Hatfield telephoned the city between the storms. "I understand the newspapers are saying I didn't make the rain," he said. "All I have to say is that Morena has had 17½ inches of rain in the last five days and that beats any similar record for the place that I have been able to find."

The Weather Bureau, the government's weather service, had a different explanation. Its forecaster in San Diego, Herbert Nimmo, pointed out that the storm was falling along the whole coast of California, hundreds of miles from the tower. His office had predicted it. Later the Bureau traced one storm across California into Wyoming and the other all the way from Hawaii, and never mentioned Hatfield.

## January 27th

The sun came out for a few days. Then, on the evening of January 26th, the second storm arrived, and it was worse.

At Morena the telephone line failed, but not before Mrs Swenson had taken a message from the city engineer: keep the spillway gates closed, the city wants to store every drop. By midnight the lake was rising two feet an hour. Seth Swenson decided the spillway would never cope, and with no way to ask anyone, he rowed out in the dark to the outlet tower, climbed down the slippery ladder inside it, and opened two valves deep under the water, against his orders. By dawn the water was pouring nearly five feet deep over the spillway, but floating trees and wreckage had jammed against the iron bars in front of it. The lake rose until it was within a few inches of the top of the dam. The dam held. The engineers said afterwards that Swenson's valves had saved it.

At Lower Otay there were no gates to open. The reservoir had been full for six days and the water was pouring through the little spillway. By noon the man in charge, R. C. Wueste, knew the dam would be overtopped before dark. He sent four men down the valley to warn the several hundred people who lived there, and most of them left. At a quarter to five the water reached the top of the dam. At ten to five it began to run down the far side. Then jets of water burst out of the face of the dam, a boulder rolled down, the rock pile began to melt away, and at five minutes past five the steel wall tore from the top and the dam, in the words of the engineer who examined it, "opened outward like a pair of gates."

Fifteen billion gallons of water, more than the reservoir had ever held, emptied out in two and a half hours. A wall of water, said by some to be six feet high and by others twenty, raced ten miles down the valley in forty-eight minutes, carrying away farms, houses, bridges and railway lines.

Nobody is sure how many people died. The coroner guessed fifty the next day. A week later the known dead numbered fourteen, with several missing. The Weather Bureau's man counted eighteen. The government's engineers, writing two years later, said twenty-two people drowned in San Diego County, most of them in the Otay valley. Many of them were Japanese farmers who searched for their own dead from small boats on the bay.

The whole county was cut off: every bridge down, both railways gone, the telephone and telegraph out, and for a month the city could be reached only by ship and by wireless.

## The bill

Up at Morena, Hatfield had seen exactly what he had promised: a lake that filled and overflowed. When the telephone came back on January 30th, the Swensons heard that Lower Otay was gone, and that people in the city were talking about coming up to hang the rainmaker. Hatfield took the tower down, raked the ground where it had stood, and set off for San Diego on foot with his brother, two days' walk through the wreckage.

On February 4th he met the newspapers. He said he had done what he had agreed to do and expected to be paid. Why had it rained all along the coast, if he had caused it? He said he did not claim to make rain, only to increase it. Would he take the blame for the damage if he took the credit for the rain? He said the benefits were bigger than the damage, and that the deaths were deplorable, but he did not feel responsible.

Then he went to see Cosgrove, the city attorney, who was friendly and asked him to write down exactly what he claimed to have done. Hatfield wrote seven pages, and did something he had never done in twelve years of rainmaking. He put a number on it: he had been directly responsible, he wrote, for four billion gallons of the water that had run into Morena.

On February 17th he came before the council to ask for his $10,000. Cosgrove asked him how much water he claimed to have put into the lake.

"Four billion gallons, if not more," Hatfield said.

"But you agreed to put in 10 billion gallons," said Cosgrove. Morena had held five billion when Hatfield started, and it took fifteen billion to fill it.

Hatfield said the four billion was his own work and the rest was "the indirect result" of it.

"You want the city to pay you only for what you yourself did?" Cosgrove asked. "You do not want the city to pay you for what nature did, do you?"

"No."

"Well, why do you ask the city to pay you for 10 billion gallons when you put in only four billion gallons?"

Cosgrove turned to the council and said that by his own words Hatfield had not done what he promised, so the city owed him nothing. Councilman Moore, who had voted to hire him, said, "If Morena overflowed, I think he should be paid his money." Another councilman, Benbough, said, "Four councilmen voted to accept the man's proposition and told him to go ahead. He ought to be paid." But on February 28th the council voted, five to nothing, to refuse the claim, and to refuse Hatfield's offer to settle for $4,000 instead.

The city made him one more offer. It would pay the whole $10,000 if he signed a paper taking responsibility for the flood. Everyone understood why. If Hatfield had made the rain, then the city had hired him to make it, and every family in the Otay valley and every owner of a drowned farm or a broken bridge could sue the city. Hatfield refused to sign. He sued the city for his fee instead.

## What happened after

Flood victims did sue the city. Two of their cases went to trial, and the judges ruled that the rain had been "an act of God", which is the law's way of saying that nobody was to blame. The city engineer used the same words to defend his department, and admitted in the same letter that no engineer of standing had ever approved the design of the Lower Otay dam. It was rebuilt in concrete. The companion piece, "[The hybrid dam](/deeper/four-billion-gallons/the-hybrid-dam/#young)", tells what the engineers made of it.

Hatfield went back to making rain, and when the Depression took the work away, to selling sewing machines. He died in 1958, aged 82. His lawsuit against San Diego sat in the court files for nearly twenty-two years, and in 1938 a court threw it out because nobody had done anything about it. "To this day," he told a reporter thirty years after the flood, "I've never felt right about that San Diego city council." San Diego still calls it Hatfield's flood.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A test needs something to compare with.** The city paid for one lake and one tower. If the lake filled, Hatfield won. But a lake can fill because of a tower or because of a storm, and with only one lake there was no way to tell. Scientists call the thing you compare with a "control". Nobody arranged one. The Weather Bureau, without meaning to, had the control all along: the rest of California, where it rained just as hard with no tower in sight.

**A gauge can only measure what it measures.** The instrument everyone looked at was the depth gauge on the dam. It said "full". That was true, and it answered the council's question, which was whether Morena would fill. But it said nothing about Hatfield's claim, which was about what caused the rain. Depth is not cause. When you set up a test, make sure the instrument you are going to read can actually answer the question you are asking.

**"No rain, no pay" was not a free bet.** The council thought the deal could only cost it nothing. Nobody worked out the other branch: if the lake did fill and the city paid, it would be agreeing in public that the rain was Hatfield's, and the rain had just become the most expensive thing that ever happened to the county. When it went that way, both sides needed the gauge to say something it could not say.

**A number nobody can check settles nothing.** When the city finally asked Hatfield to measure his own work, he said "four billion gallons". There was no gauge behind that number. He made it up, to the nearest billion, in a lawyer's office. The city then used his made-up number to prove he had not done the job. That is what reading an unreadable instrument looks like.

**The instrument that spoke was the one nobody was watching.** Nobody had ever asked whether the Lower Otay dam could survive a flood, because in nineteen years it had never been full. Then it hired a man to fill lakes. The real experiment of January 1916 was not whether chemicals make rain. It was whether that dam would hold, and the answer came in twenty minutes on the afternoon of the 27th.

So the lesson is simple. Before you pay for a test, decide what question you are asking, build in something to compare with, and make sure the instrument you plan to read can tell the difference between your idea being right and your idea being wrong. Otherwise the world will give you an answer, maybe a terrible one, and you still will not know what it means.
