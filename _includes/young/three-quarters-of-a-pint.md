## Twenty thousand children

In February 1930, in the coal and steel towns of Lanarkshire in Scotland, five doctors and seventeen nurses went from school to school weighing and measuring children. They did it for ten school days, in sixty-seven schools, and by the end they had weighed and measured twenty thousand children. Each child stood against a measuring rod in stockinged feet and was measured to the nearest eighth of an inch. Each child stepped on a new weighing machine and was weighed to the nearest ounce. The rules said that the children must take off their boots, empty their pockets, and take off their coats and scarves. Many children were wearing three or four jerseys at once, because it was cold and their houses were cold, and all the jerseys but one had to come off too.

Half of the children were about to get a mug of milk every school morning for four months: three-quarters of a pint, which is a bit more than a modern carton. The other half would get nothing. In June all twenty thousand would be weighed and measured again, and the difference between the two halves would show what milk does for a growing child.

That was the plan. On February 21st 1930 the head teacher of High Blantyre Public School wrote in the school's log book that arrangements had been made to weigh the hundred children in that school who were going to get the milk.

## Why milk

In 1930 a lot of people in Britain were poor, and a lot of children did not get enough to eat. In Lanarkshire about one child in three had a father who was out of work or only partly in work. Doctors believed that milk was the best single food you could give a child, and two earlier tests had seemed to show it. In one, boys in a children's home in England who were given a pint of milk a day grew faster than boys who were not. In another, run in Scotland in 1927 and 1928 on about 1,400 children, the children who got milk grew faster too.

But the Scottish test had a weakness. The children who got the milk had been watched closely, and their families had been visited, so perhaps they had been eating and sleeping better for that reason, and not because of the milk. The Department of Health for Scotland, which was in charge of the country's health, wanted a test so large and so simple that nobody could argue with it. Nobody would watch the children. They would just get the milk, and be measured.

There was a second question too. Milk can carry a disease called tuberculosis from cows to people, and the way to make it safe is to heat it, which is called pasteurising. Some people said that heating milk spoiled it, and that children grew better on raw milk. So half of the children who got milk would get it raw and half would get it pasteurised. Every drop came from the same herds, mixed together at one depot in Glasgow, so the only difference was the heating.

The money, about 7,500 pounds, came mostly from a government board that promoted food from the British Empire and from a fund for families in the coal-mining towns.

## How it worked

Every evening, milk from twenty-six farms arrived by train in Glasgow. Half of it was put raw into churns, and half was heated to 145 degrees Fahrenheit for half an hour and then cooled. From six in the morning, lorries carried the churns to the sixty-seven schools, so that the milk could be handed out at about half past ten. Each school got only one kind of milk, raw or pasteurised, so that no child could get the wrong kind by mistake.

Every child had a card: yellow for raw milk, pink for pasteurised milk, white for a child who got nothing. A child who got nothing was called a "control," because the controls are what you compare the others against. The February weight and height went on the card, and the June weight and height would go on it later.

Now comes the most important part of the story, so read it slowly.

Who decided which children got milk and which did not? The head teacher of each school. The doctors told the head teachers that the milk children should be ordinary, average children, not the thinnest or the most hungry, and that the controls should be ordinary, average children too. The head teachers picked the two lists in one of two ways: either by drawing lots, which is like pulling names out of a hat, or by going down the register in alphabetical order.

And then, the report says, "in any particular school where there was any group to which these methods had given an undue proportion of well-fed or ill-nourished children, others were substituted in order to attain a more level selection." In plain words: if the hat put too many thin, hungry children on the no-milk list, or too many well-fed children on the milk list, the head teacher swapped some over to make it look fairer.

That one sentence is why the experiment could not be read.

## What the children thought

The report tells us what the children and parents felt. Some parents were offended. They thought the milk was charity, or that their child had been "singled out as ill-nourished," which means picked out as badly fed. Some did not want their child "experimented upon." But most were pleased, and the most common complaint was that more children had not been chosen for the milk.

The children had their own names for the two lists. The ones who got milk were the "wets" and the ones who did not were the "drys." The school doctor wrote that the drys "took the decision in a sporting spirit," and that the wets did not gloat about winning the lottery but "seemed to evince a sincere sympathy" for the losers, which means they felt genuinely sorry for them. Most of the children loved the milk and were sad when it stopped in June.

The teachers were asked what they had noticed. They said that after six or eight weeks the milk children looked better: more colour in their cheeks, shinier skin, more energy. One head teacher wrote that "in the playground buoyancy and pugnacity are developing to an alarming extent," which means the milk children were bouncing about and getting into fights. One girl told her teacher that she "could now fight her brother," which she had never been able to do before.

## What the numbers said

In June the twenty thousand cards went to Edinburgh, where a doctor and statistician named Peter McKinlay, who was only twenty-eight, worked out the averages. About 17,000 cards could be used; the rest belonged to children who had been away on the day of the final weighing.

The first thing McKinlay checked was whether the two groups had been the same to start with. They had not. In February, before any milk had been drunk, the no-milk children were on average about a pound heavier and about half an inch taller than the milk children. That was true for boys and for girls and at every age. It was what you would expect if the head teachers had quietly moved the thinner children on to the milk list, because they thought the thin children needed it more. The report printed the numbers in two tables and said the differences were small, and that they did not matter.

Then came the result. Over the four months the milk children had grown more than the no-milk children, both in height and in weight. Seven-year-old boys with no milk put on about eleven ounces; seven-year-old boys with milk put on about fifteen. Raw milk and pasteurised milk seemed to work about equally well. The report ended with three conclusions: milk makes children grow; younger children do not benefit more than older ones; and raw and pasteurised milk are, "so far as we can judge, equal."

The report was dated December 1930. On December 9th 1930 a member of Parliament from Glasgow asked the government to give milk to every child whose father was out of work, "in view of the results derived from the Lanarkshire experiment." The government said no, but that the report would be printed soon, and that a new law already let local councils give milk to school children if they chose.

## Three statisticians read it

The experiment had been meant to be too big to argue with. Within a year, three of the cleverest statisticians in Britain were arguing with it.

The first was a dairy scientist named Stephen Bartlett, who added the ages together and said the numbers actually showed that raw milk was better than pasteurised. In April 1931 he and a famous statistician named Ronald Fisher wrote a letter to the magazine *Nature* saying so.

The second was a man who signed himself "Student." His real name was William Sealy Gosset, and he was the head experimental brewer at the Guinness brewery in Dublin, which did not let its brewers put their own names on scientific papers. In December 1931 he published nine pages about the Lanarkshire experiment. He praised everyone who had run it, and then he explained what had gone wrong.

The experiment had tried to pick the children by chance, he said, and then "after invoking the goddess of chance they unfortunately wavered in their adherence to her." Swapping children over to make the lists look fairer "was a fatal mistake." The head teachers had not done it on purpose, he thought. They had been "swayed by the very human feeling that the poorer children needed the milk more than the comparatively well to do," and had moved the hungry children on to the milk list without noticing what they were doing. But that meant the milk children and the no-milk children were different kinds of children before the milk began, and you could never again be sure how much of the difference at the end was the milk and how much was the children.

He found something else. The children had been weighed in their clothes, in February when it was cold and again in June when it was hot. Children take off a lot of winter clothes by June, perhaps a pound of them, and better-off children have more winter clothes to take off. So the no-milk children, who were better off, would have looked as if they had gained less weight than they really had, simply because they were wearing less. The weights, he said, were "wholly unreliable." The heights were better, because a measuring rod does not care about jerseys.

So was milk good for children? Student thought it probably was. He thought raw milk was probably even better than pasteurised. But "probably" is not what a 7,500-pound experiment on twenty thousand children was supposed to deliver. The conclusion, he wrote, had been "shifted from the sure ground of scientific inference to the less satisfactory foundation of mere authority and guesswork."

Then he said how it should have been done. Choose the children in pairs, two of the same age and sex and the same size and the same sort of home, and toss a coin to decide which one of each pair gets the milk. Never let anyone change the result of the coin. Better still, he said, use twins. Among twenty thousand children there would be at least 160 pairs of twins, and about fifty pairs would be identical twins. Give one twin raw milk and the other pasteurised milk, decided by a coin, and weigh them every week without their clothes. Fifty pairs of identical twins, he said, "would give more reliable results than the 20,000 with which we have been dealing," at perhaps one or two per cent of the cost.

The third reader was a statistician called Ethel Elderton, who worked for Karl Pearson in London. The government lent her all twenty thousand cards, and she spent months sorting them into pairs of children who had been the same height and nearly the same weight in February, one who got milk and one who did not. The milk children in her pairs grew a little more, mostly in height. Raw and pasteurised milk came out about equal. But she could only pair up average-sized children, because the very small and the very large children had no match.

## What happened next

Nobody waited for the statisticians. In 1934 the government started the Milk in Schools Scheme, which offered every child in England and Wales a third of a pint of milk for a halfpenny, and free if the family could not pay. Nearly three million children were drinking it within months. School milk became free in 1946, and for most children it lasted until 1971. The Lanarkshire report was one of the reasons given, though the farmers' need to sell milk was a bigger one.

The experiment was also done again, properly. From 1934 to 1937 a committee tested the school milk on about 8,400 children in five towns. This time every school had all the kinds of milk. The children were sorted into groups by counting off, one, two, three, four, down the class register, before any doctor had looked at them, and nobody was allowed to swap anyone. The result, published in 1939, was that milk made children grow, that more milk did more, and that raw and pasteurised milk were the same. One of the people who planned it was Peter McKinlay, the man who had written the Lanarkshire report.

Student died in 1937. His nine pages are still read today by people learning how to design experiments. In 2022 a statistician named Stephen Senn went back through the whole argument and decided that Student had got it right, and that the biggest mistake of all was one Student had mentioned almost in passing: the report had mixed together the no-milk children from the raw-milk schools and the pasteurised-milk schools, so it had thrown away the one fair comparison it had.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Being kind can spoil a fair test.** The head teachers did nothing wicked. They looked at a list made by chance, saw that it had put hungry children on the no-milk side, and moved them. Anybody with a heart might have done the same. But a fair test only works if chance alone decides who gets the thing being tested. The moment a person decides instead, even for a good reason, you can no longer tell whether the result came from the milk or from the choosing.

**Decide the rules before you start, and then keep to them.** The experiment said "draw lots," and that was right. Then it said "but swap some over if it looks unfair," and that was the fatal mistake. If you know that a fair list might look unfair, decide beforehand what you will do about it, because somebody on the spot will decide for you.

**Bigger is not the same as better.** Twenty thousand children sounds like a lot, and it was. But fifty pairs of twins, chosen by a coin, would have given a surer answer at a fiftieth of the cost. The number of children does not fix a mistake in how they were chosen. It only makes the mistake expensive.

**Something that would not have mattered can start to matter.** Weighing children in their clothes would have been fine if the two groups had been chosen by chance, because both groups would have taken off the same jerseys in June. Once the groups were different kinds of children, the jerseys mattered, and so did everything else nobody had thought about.

**Check what you can check before you learn the lesson.** The report itself printed the tables showing that the groups were different at the start, and said it did not matter. Student read the same tables and showed that it did. The numbers were there all along; somebody had to ask the right question.

So the lesson is simple. When you test something, let chance choose who gets it, write down the rule, and do not let anyone, however kind, improve on the result.
