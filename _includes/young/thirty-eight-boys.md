## The doctor who wanted to be checked

Richard Cabot was a doctor in Boston. He was born in 1868, went to Harvard, and spent his working life in the part of the Massachusetts General Hospital where poor people came to be treated. He noticed that he could not treat a sick person well without knowing how that person lived: whether there was food at home, whether there was work, whether anyone was looking after the children. So in 1905 he hired a social worker (a person whose job is to help families with those problems) to work beside the doctors. The hospital would not pay her, so Cabot paid her himself, and the ones who came after her, for fourteen years. Later Harvard made him a professor of ethics as well as of medicine.

In June 1931 the social workers of America made him the president of their national conference, and he used his big speech to tell them something uncomfortable. Nobody, he said, knew whether social work did any good. Two researchers had followed five hundred men for five years after they left the best reformatory in Massachusetts, a prison for young men that was supposed to reform them, and found that four out of five went back to crime. A study of one of the best charities in Boston found that nearly nine out of ten of its cases were failures. Cabot said the methods of the day were like a pea-shooter fired at a granite fortress.

Then he said the thing that matters for this story. People told him that no charity could ever be judged, because so many other things happen in a person's life. He agreed that this was true for now. But he looked forward to a day "when we shall control our results by comparison with a parallel series of cases in which we did nothing." In plain words: if you want to know whether your help works, you must also watch a group of people just like the ones you helped, whom you did not help, and compare.

## The experiment

Four years later he built exactly that. His wife Ella had died in 1934. In 1935 he set up a charity in her name, the Ella Lyman Cabot Foundation, to pay for an experiment. The idea was simple. Boys who seemed likely to get into trouble would each be given a friendly adult, a counsellor, who would visit the family, take the boy swimming and to ball games, help him with his schoolwork, find him a doctor when he needed one, send him to summer camp, and help his parents when they were in difficulty. This would go on for ten years. Cabot believed that a devoted friend from outside the family could steer a boy away from a life of crime.

The hard part was the comparison. Cabot's staff asked the schools of Cambridge and Somerville, two working-class cities next to Boston, for the names of boys who needed help, and also for the names of ordinary boys, so that being in the programme would not be a mark against anyone. About 1,900 names came in. Every boy was examined by a doctor and tested by a psychologist. A home visitor interviewed his mother. His teacher filled out a card listing ninety-three bad habits. Then a committee of three experts read every file and rated each boy on a scale from minus five (almost certain to go wrong) to plus five (almost certain to be fine). They passed 782 boys.

Then the matching began. The staff looked for pairs of boys who were as alike as two boys could be: the same age, the same kind of family, the same school, the same health, the same marks, the same rating from the committee. They called the pairs "diagnostic twins." They found 325 pairs, 650 boys in all, aged between five and thirteen.

Then came the step that made it an experiment. For each pair, somebody tossed a coin. The boy the coin chose got a counsellor. The boy it did not choose got nothing, and was only watched. Cabot insisted on the coin because if a person had chosen, they might have chosen the nicer boy, or the needier one, every time without meaning to, and the two groups would no longer be alike. A coin cannot mean to.

The coin-tossing finished on May 13th 1939, and the staff set June 1st 1939 as the day the counselling officially began. Cabot had died on May 7th. He never saw his experiment run.

## Five and a half years

The counsellors worked hard. In the first year alone they made more than 2,000 visits to families and more than 2,000 to boys, and went to schools, hospitals and police stations hundreds of times. Each full-time counsellor had about thirty-four boys and tried to see each of them about twice a month. They arranged tutoring, dentists, clothes, jobs and camps. They were not allowed to have anything to do with the boys in the other group.

Then the war came. In 1942 petrol was rationed and some of the counsellors joined the army, and the programme was cut back to 253 pairs. Whenever a boy was dropped, his twin in the other group was dropped too, so the two groups stayed matched. The counselling ended on December 31st 1945, four years earlier than Cabot had planned. By then the treated boys had had their counsellors for about five and a half years on average. More than half had been tutored, more than a hundred had seen a doctor or psychiatrist through the programme, and 125 had been to summer camp.

## The first two readings

In 1948 the staff checked the results for the first time. The counsellors thought many of their boys had turned out better than the committee had predicted. But when the staff tracked down 148 of the untreated boys, almost as many of them had turned out better than predicted too. The court records showed that 96 treated boys had been in court against 92 untreated ones. There was no difference worth the name. Gordon Allport, the Harvard psychologist who ran the Foundation, said to be patient: perhaps the benefit would show when the boys grew up.

In 1956 a young couple, William and Joan McCord, checked the court records again. Still no difference. The men were in their twenties.

## The third reading

Joan McCord went on to become a professor of criminology, the study of crime. In 1975, when the men were about forty-five, she went looking for all 506 of them. Her team searched court records, hospital records, the records of clinics for alcoholics, death records, telephone books and car registrations. By 1976 they had found 480 of the men, 95 out of every 100, and by 1979 they had found 98 out of every 100. They sent questionnaires and later did interviews.

The men who had been given counsellors were asked whether the programme had helped them. Two out of three said yes. They wrote things like "I was put on the right road," "helped prepare me for manhood," and "helped me to have faith and trust in other people." Some said that without their counsellor, "I probably would be in jail." More than twenty of them asked for their counsellors' addresses, thirty years later, so they could write to them.

Then McCord compared the two groups on the records. She made fifty-seven comparisons. The two groups had committed the same number of crimes as boys, and the same number as adults. But among the men who had any criminal record, more of the treated men had gone on to commit a second crime. More of the treated men showed signs of heavy drinking. More of the treated men who had been in a mental hospital had the serious illnesses. The treated men who had died had died younger, at thirty-two on average instead of thirty-eight. More of the treated men had high blood pressure or heart trouble. Fewer of them had office jobs, and fewer were happy in their work.

Seven comparisons out of fifty-seven showed a real difference. Every one of the seven went against the boys who had been helped. McCord worked out that chance would do that less than once in ten thousand tries.

She published the results in 1978. People sent her threatening letters and telephone calls. When she gave talks, people in the audience shouted names at her.

## Thirty-eight boys

The clearest small number in the whole study is this one. When the counselling ended in 1945, the counsellors had named the thirty-eight boys they were surest they had helped. McCord looked at each of those thirty-eight beside his twin, the boy the coin had left alone. In twenty-two pairs the two men had turned out about the same. In four the helped boy had done better. In twelve he had done worse.

## More was worse

McCord kept going. In 1981 she used a stricter test. A man's life was counted as having gone badly only if he had been convicted of a serious crime, or had died before thirty-five, or had been diagnosed as an alcoholic or with a serious mental illness. By that test 105 of the 253 treated men had gone badly, and 81 of the 253 untreated men. In 150 pairs the twins had turned out alike. In the 103 pairs that differed, the helped boy was the one who had gone wrong in 63, and the unhelped boy in 39.

Then she looked for the part of the programme that had worked, and could not find one. Instead she found that the harm grew with the help. Boys whose counsellors visited most often did worse than their twins. Boys who stayed in the programme longest did worse. Boys whose counsellors worked hardest on their personal and family problems did worse. And boys whose families had refused to cooperate, so that the counsellor never really got in, did no worse at all. The harm went wherever the help went.

Years later she found the strongest clue. The counsellors had sent some boys to summer camp, some of them several summers running. Among the boys sent to camp two or more times, two did better than their twins and twenty did worse. McCord thought the boys from the programme had found one another at camp and egged each other on. She also warned that the counsellors, not the coin, had chosen who went to camp, so that clue is not as safe as the rest.

In 2003 she wrote a paper called "Cures That Harm." She listed ten things the programme had done right: careful planning, trained counsellors, doctors and tutors, help for the parents, five and a half years of it, and clients who were grateful. Then she wrote that without the control group, anyone would have called it a success.

McCord died in 2004. She was still the director of the study.

## The fourth reading

In 2016 a new team at Northeastern University in Boston went looking for the men a fourth time. By then they were in their eighties and nineties. The team found 488 of the 506; 446 had died and 42 were alive. Among the treated men, 231 had died, against 215 of the untreated men, but when the pairs were compared the difference was small enough to be chance. The two groups of old men had died at about the same ages, of the same kinds of things. The harm that McCord had measured in their forties had faded. The team said the earlier result might have been a one-off, and did not say why it had faded.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**You cannot tell whether help works by asking the people you helped.** Two out of three men said the programme had helped them. They were not lying. They had liked their counsellors and remembered their kindness. But none of them could know what their lives would have been without it. Only the twins could show that, because the twins were the same boys without the help.

**A control group is the only thing that can hear harm.** Everyone inside the programme, the counsellors, the boys, the families, saw it doing good. The committee's predictions said the boys had turned out better than expected. Every one of those measures was blind to the harm, because none of them could see the other half of the experiment. The coin and the twins could.

**If the harm grows with the dose, the dose is the cause.** The boys who got the most help did the worst, and the boys whose families kept the counsellor out did no worse at all. That is how you tell a real effect from bad luck.

**Decide how long you will keep watching.** The first two checks, in 1948 and 1956, found nothing, because the difference had not appeared yet. It only showed clearly when the men were about thirty-five. If the experiment had stopped reading its results when the counselling stopped, it would have learned nothing at all. And when it kept watching into old age, it saw the harm fade.

So the lesson is simple. Build the comparison before you start, keep it running long after you stop, and believe what it says even when everyone you helped says it is wrong.
