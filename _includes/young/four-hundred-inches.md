## The plant

Three Mile Island is an island in the Susquehanna River in Pennsylvania, about ten miles from Harrisburg. In 1979 it had two nuclear power plants on it. The newer one, Unit 2, had started making electricity only three months before, on December 30th 1978. It was built by a company called Babcock & Wilcox and run by a company called Metropolitan Edison.

A nuclear plant like this one is, at heart, a very large kettle. Uranium fuel in the reactor gives off heat. Water is pumped through the reactor to carry the heat away, and that water is kept under enormous pressure so that it cannot boil, even at nearly 600 degrees Fahrenheit. The hot water boils a separate supply of water into steam, and the steam turns a turbine, and that makes the electricity.

The pressure in the reactor's water is set by a tall tank called the pressuriser. The pressuriser is about half full of water, with a cushion of steam on top. Squeezing or expanding that steam cushion raises or lowers the pressure in the whole system. The operators were taught one rule about it above all others: never let the pressuriser fill up completely with water. If it did, the steam cushion would be gone, and the system would be "solid", and a small change could send the pressure shooting up and damage the pipes.

On top of the pressuriser were three valves to let steam out if the pressure got too high. Two of them were plain spring-loaded safety valves. The third was smaller and worked by an electric signal: it was called the pilot-operated relief valve, and it opened on its own when the pressure rose too high and was supposed to close again on its own when the pressure came back down. Underneath it was a second valve, called the block valve, which the operators could close from the control room if the relief valve ever stuck open.

## The light

A year before the accident, on March 29th 1978, the day after the reactor was first started, a fuse blew and the relief valve lost its electric power. The way it was wired, losing power made it open. It stayed open for four minutes, and the operators did not know, because the control room had no indicator to show whether the valve was open or shut.

Afterwards the plant's staff asked for a proper position indicator: something that would show where the valve actually was. What they were given was a light that showed whether the valve was being told to open. When the signal to open was on, the light was on. When the signal went off, the light went off. The supervisor of operations, James Floyd, said later that "all we're going to get is command valve command signal light," and he did not make a fuss. In January 1979 the plant's engineers asked again for something better. On March 16th 1979, twelve days before the accident, the company's head office wrote back that the change was "not necessary or required."

By then the relief valve had been leaking for five months. The pipe below it, which should have been cooler than 130 degrees, was usually around 170 or 180. The operators had got used to that, and they did not close the block valve, because they were afraid it might stick shut.

## The warning nobody passed on

The same thing had already nearly happened at another plant. On September 24th 1977, at the Davis-Besse plant in Ohio, which had a reactor of the same design, the relief valve opened and stuck. The water level in the pressuriser went up while the pressure went down, and the operators, watching the level, turned off the emergency pumps that were putting water in. They found the open valve after twenty-one minutes and closed the block valve, before any harm was done.

An engineer at Babcock & Wilcox named Bert Dunn wrote a memo about it in February 1978. He said that if the same thing happened at a plant running at full power, it was "possible, perhaps probable" that the core would be left uncovered and the fuel damaged. "I believe this is a very serious matter," he wrote, "and deserves our prompt attention and correction." The man he sent it to decided it had gone to the wrong place, because it was not on the right form. Nobody told the operators at Three Mile Island. The companion piece "[The memoranda](/deeper/four-hundred-inches/the-memoranda/#young)" follows that paper trail.

## Four in the morning, March 28th 1979

Four licensed men were running Unit 2 that night: William Zewe, the shift supervisor; Fred Scheimann, the shift foreman; and two control room operators, Edward Frederick and Craig Faust. All four had learnt their trade in the United States Navy's nuclear programme, and all four had passed the government's examinations with scores above the national average. Scheimann was down in the turbine building, working on one of the machines that cleans the feedwater.

At 36 seconds past four o'clock, a year to the day and the hour after the reactor first started, some valves in that cleaning system shut by mistake. The pumps that fed water to the steam generators stopped, so the steam generators could not take heat away from the reactor. The turbine shut itself down. The water in the reactor got hotter and expanded, the pressure rose, and the relief valve on top of the pressuriser opened, exactly as it was designed to. Eight seconds in, the reactor shut itself down too: its control rods dropped into the core and stopped the nuclear reaction. All of this was right.

At about thirteen seconds the pressure fell back to the point where the relief valve should have closed. The signal to it was cut off, and the light on the panel went out. But the valve did not close. It stayed open, and steam began pouring out of the top of the reactor's water system at the rate of about 220 gallons of water a minute. It would stay open for more than two hours. Nobody in the room knew. The light said it was shut.

## The gauge that read four hundred

At first everything looked like a normal shutdown. The pressure fell, and the water level in the pressuriser fell with it. Then, within a minute, the level turned round and began to rise, fast, while the pressure kept falling. Normally the two move together.

At two minutes, the pressure had dropped so far that the emergency core cooling system switched itself on. Two big pumps began pouring about a thousand gallons a minute into the reactor, more than enough to replace what was going out through the open valve. But the level in the pressuriser kept climbing, and the operators had been trained to fear a full pressuriser. At four minutes and 38 seconds they turned one of the emergency pumps off and cut the other back to a trickle, about 25 gallons a minute, and opened a drain line to take water out. They were trying to stop the pressuriser going solid. "The rapidly increasing pressurizer level at the onset of the accident led me to believe that the high pressure injection was excessive," Frederick said afterwards, "and that we were soon going to have a solid system."

At six minutes the pressuriser gauge reached the top of its scale: 400 inches. It stayed there for two hours. What was really happening was this. With the pressure gone, the water in the reactor had started to boil. Steam collected at the high points of the pipes and pushed the water ahead of it up into the pressuriser. The tank was full because the reactor was emptying. There was no gauge in the plant that measured the water in the reactor itself, and that morning the only gauge there was meant the opposite of what the operators had been taught.

## Two hours of clues

There were plenty of signs that the valve was open, and the operators saw most of them. A drain tank in the containment building, which caught what came out of the valve, filled up and burst at fifteen minutes, and water spread across the floor. The containment building got warmer and its pressure rose. At about 4:24, Zewe had the computer print the temperature of the pipe under the relief valve: 285 degrees. At 5:20 it was printed again: 283 degrees, more than sixty degrees hotter than the pipes under the two safety valves. One of the plant's emergency procedures said that 200 degrees in that pipe meant the valve was open.

Every clue got explained away. The pipe was hot, but it had been hot for months because of the leak, and the valve had opened for a few seconds at four o'clock, so of course it was warm. The drain tank had burst, so the valve must have opened hard. The building was warm because the tank had burst. The pressure was low, but at least it had stopped falling. "It would have concerned me a heck of a lot more if it [the pressure] was still going down," Scheimann said. And the light said the valve was shut.

The one thing that did not fit, the operators knew, was the full pressuriser. "The pressurizer went full and we believed it [the reactor coolant system] was full," Frederick said. "It must have been full of water, but the next confusing thing was the system wasn't reacting as if it was solid." Zewe said: "I didn't know where the water could be coming from." More than a hundred alarms had gone off in the first minutes. "I would have liked to have thrown away the alarm panel," Faust said. "It wasn't giving us any useful information."

They went through the emergency procedures looking for an accident that matched. One of the four they considered was a loss of coolant, which is exactly what they had. They rejected it. "It really did not enter my mind that we had a loss-of-coolant accident," Zewe said. "I always think, in terms of a loss-of-coolant accident . . . that your pressurizer level is a big key. But I had the reverse of what it would have been."

## The pumps

Just after five o'clock the four huge pumps that drive water through the reactor began to shake violently. They were pumping steam as well as water, which is another sign that the water was boiling. The operators, following their training, shut two of them down at 5:14 to save them from damage, and the other two at 5:41.

That was the worst moment. While the pumps ran, the mix of water and steam going through the core had still carried enough heat away to protect the fuel. When they stopped, the steam rose into the top of the pipes, and the water level in the reactor dropped. With the valve still open and almost no water going in, the top of the core was uncovered by about 6:15. The fuel rods got hotter and hotter, and at those temperatures their metal reacts with steam, makes hydrogen, and splits open.

At about six o'clock a new shift supervisor, Brian Mehler, came in from outside. He looked at a full pressuriser and a falling pressure and decided that there must be a leak. He asked the computer for the pipe temperatures again, and the pipe under the relief valve was still the hottest. "From that point," he said, "I assumed that the electromatic was partially opened because of the temperature." He told Scheimann to close the block valve. It was 6:22, two hours and twenty-two minutes after the relief valve had opened. In the first hundred minutes, about 32,000 gallons of water had gone out, more than a third of everything in the system.

Even then, nobody turned the emergency pumps back up properly until 8:26. The investigators could never find out why.

## What happened next

By 6:40 the radiation alarms were going off. A sample of the reactor's water was 350 times more radioactive than normal, which meant the fuel was breaking. "At that point," said George Kunder, the duty engineer, "I realized, 'Oh my God, we're failing fuel.'" At 6:56 the plant declared a site emergency, and at 7:24 a general emergency. By then, the investigators calculated, as much as two-thirds of the twelve-foot-tall core was standing out of the water, at temperatures of 3,500 to 4,000 degrees.

Nobody died, and nobody was hurt by the radiation that leaked out, which was very small. But the next five days were frightening for the people who lived nearby. On Friday a helicopter over the plant measured 1,200 millirems an hour, and officials in Washington recommended that everyone within ten miles be evacuated, then withdrew the recommendation within the hour. At lunchtime the governor of Pennsylvania advised pregnant women and small children within five miles to leave, and many more people left than were asked to. Over the weekend the government's experts argued about whether a bubble of hydrogen inside the reactor could explode. The President, Jimmy Carter, walked through the control room on Sunday afternoon. By that evening the bubble was shrinking, and it had never been able to explode at all. The companion piece "[Friday](/deeper/four-hundred-inches/friday/#young)" tells that part.

It took eleven years to take the damaged fuel out of the reactor. Nearly half of the core, about sixty-two tonnes, had melted, and about nineteen tonnes of it had run down into the bottom of the reactor vessel. It had not got through.

## What changed

The President appointed a commission, led by John Kemeny, the president of Dartmouth College, to find out what had happened. It reported in October 1979. Other investigations had said the cause was "operator error". The commission agreed that if the operators had left the emergency pumps on, there would have been no accident. But it said that was not the real cause. The training was "greatly deficient", the procedures were confusing, and the warnings from Davis-Besse had never reached the people who needed them. Given all that, it wrote, "we are convinced that an accident like Three Mile Island was eventually inevitable."

Seven days after the accident, Babcock & Wilcox finally sent its customers the instruction Bert Dunn had asked for fourteen months earlier: do not turn off the emergency cooling just because the pressuriser is full. The government ordered every nuclear plant in the country to fit a real position indicator on its relief valves, and a meter that tells the operators whether the water in the reactor is boiling.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A light that shows an order is not a light that shows what happened.** The light on the panel showed that the valve had been told to close. It did not show that the valve had closed. Everyone who looked at it read it the second way, because that is what a light next to a valve seems to mean. If you want to know whether something has happened, look at the thing itself, not at the instruction that was sent to it.

**The number everyone trusts had better mean what they think it means.** The operators had been taught that the pressuriser level told them how much water was in the reactor. That morning the pressuriser was full because the reactor was boiling dry. The gauge was not broken. It was measuring the wrong thing, and there was no gauge for the right thing.

**A warning that stays in the office that wrote it is not a warning.** Engineers at the manufacturer worked out, more than a year before, exactly what would go wrong, and wrote it down. The memo went to the wrong person, on the wrong form, and sat there. Knowing about a danger is not the same as telling the people who will face it.

**An alarm you have learnt to ignore cannot warn you.** The pipe under the valve had been too hot for five months, and the operators had got used to it. When it finally read 285 degrees, the one reading that could have told them the truth was the one they had been ignoring all winter.

So the lesson is simple. Before you trust an instrument, ask what it is really measuring. And when you learn how something could go wrong, make sure the people who will be standing in front of the panel know it too.
