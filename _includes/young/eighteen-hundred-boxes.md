## A senator who was late

On the morning of November 15th 1978, a United States senator named Daniel Patrick Moynihan arrived forty-five minutes late to a hearing room in Washington. A senator is one of the hundred people elected to the upper house of the American parliament, and a hearing is a meeting where senators ask experts questions and write down the answers. Moynihan apologised. The computer that ran his airline's flights had broken, and his plane had sat on the ground.

He had called the hearing to find out what ten years of experiments had shown. The American government had spent about 112 million dollars paying a guaranteed income to thousands of poor families in six states. A guaranteed income is a promise: if your family's income falls below a set line, the government sends you a cheque to bring it up. The experiments were meant to find out what people would do if they had that promise.

Moynihan had spent ten years saying it was a good idea. Now he had a statement for the record that said: "We must now be prepared to entertain the possibility that we were wrong."

The reason was a single number: sixty. The researchers in Seattle and Denver said that among the families who got the money, marriages had broken up at a rate as much as sixty percent higher than among families who did not.

Twelve years later, two economists looked at the same numbers again and could not find the sixty percent. By then nobody was asking.

And that same week, far to the north in Canada, the only other experiment of its kind on the continent was quietly ending in a small town on the prairie. Its results were being packed into boxes. There were about 1,800 of them. Nobody opened them for thirty years.

## The two experiments

The American experiments began in 1968. The biggest was in Seattle and Denver, and it is usually called by its initials, SIME/DIME, said as one word. About 4,800 families took part. Some were offered a guaranteed income for three years, some for five. The guarantee was about 3,800 to 5,600 dollars a year for a family of four, which in 1971 was roughly the poverty line or a bit above it. For every dollar a family earned, the payment went down by fifty or seventy cents, so working always left you with more than not working.

About a thousand families were a control group: families chosen the same way who got no payments, so that the researchers could compare. Every family was interviewed every few months, and one of the questions was who was living in the house. That is how the researchers knew who had split up.

The Canadian experiment was called Mincome, from Manitoba, minimum and income. The federal government and the province of Manitoba agreed to it in 1974. It cost about 17 million dollars, and Ottawa paid three-quarters. In the city of Winnipeg, about 1,000 families were picked at random for different plans or for a control group, like the American design.

But Mincome also did something nobody had done before. It picked one whole town and offered the guaranteed income to everyone in it. The town was Dauphin, about 300 kilometres north-west of Winnipeg: a farming town of about 10,000 people with a railway station, a hospital and a factory that made running shoes. Every family in Dauphin could sign up. A family of four with no other income would get 3,800 dollars a year, and for every dollar they earned the payment went down by fifty cents. About 590 families signed up, and at any one time about a third of the town's families qualified.

## What they wanted to know

The experiments were built to answer one question: if people were promised an income, how much less would they work? The answer mattered because it decided how much the policy would cost.

But the politicians cared about a different question. The welfare system America already had paid mothers with children, and in most states it paid nothing while the father lived in the house. Everyone said this split families apart. A guaranteed income would pay the family whether the father was there or not, so it should hold families together. That was the heart of the argument for it. Moynihan himself had written in 1967 that there was not "5 cents worth of research findings" to prove any of it.

The Seattle and Denver experiment was not designed to test the family question. But it had the data, because of the interviews.

## Sixty percent

In 1977 the researchers published what they had found: among the families getting payments, marriages were breaking up at nearly twice the rate of the control group. It was the opposite of what everyone expected. The researchers checked it every way they could think of and it did not go away.

They had an explanation. A payment to a couple does two things. It makes them better off, which helps the marriage. But it is also there for the wife on her own if she leaves, which makes leaving possible. Welfare was there for her too, but welfare was shameful and hard to get, so it did not count for as much in her mind.

The finding reached Washington in 1978. In February, the government minister in charge of welfare came before Moynihan's committee to argue for President Carter's new guaranteed income plan. Moynihan asked him for his evidence that paying families would keep them together. The minister admitted there was no clear evidence either way. In May the researchers came themselves and gave the numbers: break-ups up 61 percent for white families, 58 percent for Black families.

President Carter's plan was never voted on that year. In November, Moynihan held his three days of hearings. One expert told him the marriage effect was small compared with everything else happening to families in the 1970s. "Is that marginal, ma'am?" he said. "That is not what I call marginal." Another expert said he was puzzled by the finding and had tried to find something wrong with it and could not.

The man who ran the experiment said something that nobody took up. The experiment, he said, had been designed to measure work, not marriage. If marriage was going to decide the policy, it could have been designed better.

Moynihan asked a bigger question. "What are the limits of experiments with communities?" What happened to the people the experiment left behind when it ended? A cut in hours worked, he said, was only money. "But when a child loses a father, that is a large event in that child's life."

## What it was like in Dauphin

In Dauphin the experiment looked like a cheque in the post. Eric Richardson was ten when it began, the youngest of six children. His mother said the money was "enough to add some cream to the coffee." The family bought a table and chairs, and Eric went to the dentist for the first time. He had ten cavities. Betty Wallace and her husband raised sheep and trained horses and bought a used truck with the money; she still had it forty years later. Joy Taylor was eighteen and newly married, and her husband was able to borrow from the bank to open a record shop, because the bank could see the family would never fall below the floor.

When the researchers asked people why they had signed up, the answers were plain. "I wanted to spend a year at home with my children." "[John] had broken his leg and we needed help." Some employers in the town were angry. One said the programme was "spoiling people rotten."

In the Mincome office in Winnipeg, the experiment looked like a budget that would not add up. Prices rose fast in the 1970s, and the payments to families rose with them, but the budget did not. More families than expected qualified. The research staff were told to collect their data and file it, but not to study it. One of the people who ran it said later that they never got to analyse it.

## What happened

In America the policy died. A smaller welfare bill passed the House of Representatives in November 1979, but the Senate never acted on it, and in 1980 Ronald Reagan was elected and the idea left politics. The number travelled as it went. One writer said marriages had broken up 430 percent faster. Moynihan wrote in a letter that it was "some 70 percent." The experiment's final report, published in 1983, said 40 to 60 percent.

Then two economists at the University of Wisconsin, Glen Cain and Douglas Wissoker, read the data again. They noticed that most of the families on payments had also been offered a training programme, and that the training changed marriages too, because it gave wives better jobs. They separated the families who got only the payments, used all the years of the experiment instead of the first two or three, and let the break-up rate change over time. The sixty percent shrank to 13 percent, then to 4 percent, then to nothing. They published this in 1990. It was twelve years after the hearings, and nobody in Washington was listening any more.

In Canada, new governments took over in Manitoba in 1977 and in Ottawa in 1979, and neither wanted to pay to study the other's experiment. Mincome "died a quiet death in 1979," two of its economists wrote. The paper files went to a warehouse in Winnipeg. In the 1980s a small institute at the University of Manitoba rescued the Winnipeg work data from old computer tapes, and in 1991 and 1993 two economists, Derek Hum and Wayne Simpson, finally published the work results: men worked about one percent less, wives about three percent less, unmarried women about five percent less. That was all Mincome ever officially reported. The Dauphin files were never looked at.

## The woman who went looking

Evelyn Forget was a nineteen-year-old student in Toronto in 1974 when a professor came into her economics class talking about an experiment in Manitoba that was going to change Canada. She never forgot it. She became a health economist, and in 2008 she went to find out what had become of it.

She found about 1,800 boxes in a government archive in Winnipeg: questionnaires, income forms, interviews, all on paper. The Dauphin results had never been typed into a computer. She could not read them all in a lifetime.

So she went around them. Manitoba had brought in free health care for everyone in 1970, and every hospital stay and doctor's visit since then was in a database. Forget could not find out who in Dauphin had received a cheque, but she could find everyone who had lived in Dauphin from 1974 to 1978, match each of them with three people of the same age and sex in similar towns, and compare their hospital records.

In 2011 she published what she found. During the experiment, people in Dauphin went to hospital about 8.5 percent less than the people they were matched with. The drop was mostly in accidents, injuries and mental illness. More teenagers finished high school. And there was no rise in families breaking up. The Seattle result was not in Dauphin either.

Her paper was read all over the world as proof that a guaranteed income makes people healthier. Then in 2021 another economist, David Green, looked at her published numbers and argued that hospital visits in Dauphin had already been falling before the experiment and kept falling after it. Take out that trend, he said, and the experiment might even have raised hospital visits a little. The two of them had the same numbers and read them differently.

A few of the boxes have been opened since. A sociologist named David Calnitsky found the survey that asked people why they signed up, and found that people in Dauphin did not feel the shame that welfare carried. He also found that more people in Dauphin stopped working than the Winnipeg numbers had suggested: about 11 percentage points fewer were in the labour force. Other researchers reported that violent crime in Dauphin fell by 44 percent during the experiment, and that the rise in break-ups in America had happened in Seattle only, not in Denver and not in Winnipeg. Most of the 1,800 boxes are still paper.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A result gets read when somebody wants to know the answer.** The marriage number in America was read within months, because it landed in front of a senator who had been asking that exact question for ten years. The hospital records in Manitoba sat in a database for thirty-four years and nobody looked, because the people who had asked the question had been told to stop. Both experiments produced results. Only one had anyone waiting for it.

**A number travels faster than the table it came from.** The same experiment gave 96 percent, 61 percent, 60 percent, 43 percent and "40 to 60 percent" depending on which families and which years you counted. Then it became 70 percent in a letter and 430 percent in a book. Each person picked the row that suited them. When someone finally showed the real answer was close to zero, there was no single number left to argue with.

**Studying the results is the last thing in the budget and the first thing cut.** Both experiments had to pay the families and pay the interviewers. Reading the results came out of whatever was left, and in Canada nothing was left. An experiment that cannot afford to study its own results has bought a lot of paper, not knowledge.

**A tool built for one question answers other questions badly.** The Seattle experiment was built to measure work and got used to decide a question about marriage. Forget's hospital database was built to pay doctors and got used to measure an experiment. Neither answer was a lie. But both came from asking a good instrument a question it was not made for.

**Check the story before you learn from it.** Was it 1,800 boxes or 2,000? Was Dauphin's guarantee 3,800 dollars or 4,800? Did hospital visits fall by 8.5 percent or rise by 5? The sources disagree, and anyone who repeats one number without saying so is doing what the senators did.

So the lesson is simple. Finding something out is only half of an experiment. The other half is deciding, before you start, who will read the answer and when, and paying for it. Otherwise the loudest number wins, or no number does, and the people who took part are left behind either way.
