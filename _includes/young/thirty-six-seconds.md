## The number on the tape

Chernobyl was a nuclear power station in Ukraine, which in 1986 was part of the Soviet Union. It had four reactors. A reactor makes heat by splitting uranium atoms; the heat boils water into steam, the steam turns a turbine, and the turbine turns a generator that makes electricity.

At twenty-two minutes past one in the morning of April 26th 1986, in the control room of the fourth reactor, a computer called SKALA saved everything it knew about the reactor onto a tape. It did this every five minutes. If someone had asked it, it could have worked out from that tape the most important number in the rule book: how many control rods' worth of "brake" were left inside the reactor. The rules said there should be at least twenty-six to thirty. If the number fell to fifteen, the reactor had to be shut down at once. Nobody asked the computer that night. It took ten to fifteen minutes to work the number out, and it stood fifty metres away from the men at the controls.

The tape was read later, at another power station, after everything had happened. The number on it was eight.

Thirty-four seconds after the tape was saved, an experiment began. Thirty-six seconds after that, someone pressed the button that shuts the reactor down. A few seconds later the reactor exploded.

## What a control rod does

To understand why the shutdown button blew up the reactor, you need to know three things about this kind of reactor, which was called the RBMK.

The first is the control rods. Splitting uranium atoms throws out tiny particles called neutrons, and each neutron can split another atom. That is a chain reaction. To slow it down, you push in rods made of a material that soaks up neutrons; to speed it up, you pull them out. The fourth reactor had 211 of these rods above a core seven metres tall, and a button called AZ-5 that dropped all of them in at once. That is how you stopped the reactor, in an emergency or at the end of a normal day.

The second is the water. The reactor was cooled by water pumped through it. Water also soaks up neutrons a little. In most reactors, if the water boils away, the chain reaction slows down. In the RBMK it was the opposite. When water turned to steam, there was less water to soak up neutrons, so the chain reaction sped up, which made more steam, which sped it up more. It means the reactor can run away with itself.

The third is the strange shape of the rods. Each rod had a piece of graphite, a kind of carbon, hanging below it on a telescope. Graphite does not soak up neutrons at all. When a rod was pulled all the way up, the graphite sat in the middle of the core, with water above it and water below it. When the rod dropped, the graphite pushed the water out of the bottom of the core. Water soaks up neutrons; graphite does not. So for the first second or so, in the bottom of the reactor, dropping the rods did not slow the chain reaction. It sped it up.

Whether that mattered depended on how many rods were already partly in. If most rods were partly in, their "brake" parts were already in the reactor and dropping them further slowed it down at once. If nearly all the rods were right at the top, dropping them sped the bottom of the reactor up before it slowed anything down. That is what the number on the tape measured. With thirty rods' worth inside, the button was a brake. With eight, it was the opposite of a brake.

## The letter

The people who designed the reactor knew this. In November and December 1983, when a new reactor of the same kind was started up for the first time at a place called Ignalina, in Lithuania, engineers measured the effect. The fourth reactor at Chernobyl was started up at the same time, and they measured it there too. On December 23rd 1983 the scientists in charge of the design wrote a letter to the engineers who had drawn it. They said that if the reactor's power was cut to half, the number of rods inside would fall, and pressing the shutdown button "may lead to the introduction of positive reactivity", which means speeding up instead of stopping. They said that "a more thorough analysis will reveal other dangerous situations."

On February 2nd 1984 the design engineers wrote back to every power station with this kind of reactor. They said they were going to make a rule that no more than 150 of the 211 rods could ever be pulled all the way up. The rule was never written into the operating instructions. The rods were not redesigned. The reactor at Chernobyl ran for two more years with the letter sitting in a file in Moscow, and the operators at Chernobyl were never told. The full story of the letters is in the companion piece "[The letter of 1983](/deeper/thirty-six-seconds/the-letter-of-1983/#young)".

## The experiment

The experiment was not about the reactor at all. It was about the turbine.

If a nuclear power station loses its electricity, the pumps that push cooling water through the reactor stop, and the reactor can overheat even after it is shut down. Chernobyl had diesel generators for this, but they took forty to fifty seconds to start up and come to full power. The designers had an idea for filling that gap. A turbine and its generator weigh about 3,500 tonnes, and when the steam is cut off they go on spinning for a while. While they spin, they still make electricity. The idea was to use that electricity to run the pumps until the diesels were ready.

It was a good idea, and it needed testing. The test was tried on the third reactor in 1982, and the generator's voltage dropped too fast. The engineers changed the regulator and tried again in 1984 and 1985, and it still was not right. In April 1986 the fourth reactor was about to be shut down for its yearly maintenance, and the managers decided to try once more before it went off, or wait another year.

The plan was written at the station and approved by the station's chief engineer, Nikolai Fomin. It was not sent to the scientists or the design engineers in Moscow, or to the government inspectors. It said the reactor should be running at a quarter of its full power. It said the emergency cooling system should be shut off by hand during the test, so that it would not flood the reactor by mistake. It said all eight cooling pumps should be running, so that when four of them slowed down with the turbine, four would still be going. The plan treated the test as an electrical experiment. It did not ask whether the reactor could safely sit there while it happened. The man who wrote it, the station's deputy chief engineer Anatoly Dyatlov, admitted later that the part of the plan about safety "did look blank".

## Nine hours

The test was meant to be run on the afternoon of April 25th 1986, by the day shift, the people who had prepared it. The reactor started coming down from full power at one o'clock that morning. By a quarter to four it was at half power. At two in the afternoon the emergency cooling system was shut off, as the plan said.

Then the telephone rang. The dispatcher who ran the electricity grid for Kiev asked the station to keep the reactor running. Another power station was late coming on line. So the fourth reactor sat at half power, with its emergency cooling switched off, for nine hours.

That did two things. A reactor at half power slowly poisons itself: splitting uranium makes a gas called xenon, which soaks up neutrons like a sponge and builds up for most of a day, so the operators have to pull rods out to keep the reactor going. And the delay meant the test would now be done in the middle of the night, by a different shift.

At ten past eleven that night the dispatcher let the station go. At midnight the night shift came on. The shift leader was Aleksandr Akimov. The man at the reactor controls was Leonid Toptunov, who was twenty-six. Dyatlov was in the room to run the test, with about a dozen other people: turbine engineers, an electrical engineer from an outside company with his recorder, two trainees who had come to watch, and the evening's shift leader, who had stayed on.

## The fall

At twenty-eight minutes past midnight, as the reactor was being brought down to the power the plan called for, something went wrong in the controls. The power did not stop at a quarter. It fell almost to nothing.

This was the moment that decided everything. At almost no power, the xenon poison builds up fast. There were two choices: shut the reactor down and wait a day for the poison to clear, or pull more rods out and climb back up. By one account, Toptunov and Akimov did not want to go back up, and Dyatlov made them. By Dyatlov's own account, going back up was normal and allowed, and the rules said nothing against it. On the rules, he was right: there was none against running at low power, no instrument on the desk showed the number of rods' worth inside, and nobody had told the operators what that number meant.

By one in the morning the reactor was steady at 200 megawatts, about a sixteenth of its full power, and it could not be pushed any higher. The operators decided to run the test there. They started the seventh and eighth cooling pumps. With so little power, the eight pumps pushed far more water through the reactor than usual, which squeezed out the steam, which pushed the power down, which the operators answered by pulling more rods. Almost every rod was now at the top of its channel. The water coming into the reactor was only three degrees below boiling.

At 1:22 and 30 seconds the computer saved its tape. The number was eight.

## Thirty-six seconds

At 1:23 and 4 seconds the electrical engineer gave the order to start his recorder, and a turbine engineer closed the valves that fed steam to the turbine. The turbine began to coast. The four pumps running off it began to slow down. For thirty-six seconds the test went exactly as planned. The water flow dropped a little. The pressure rose a little. Nothing happened that needed anyone to do anything.

At 1:23 and 40 seconds the AZ-5 button was pressed.

Why it was pressed is the one thing nobody has ever settled. The Soviet government said afterwards that the power had started to rise and the operators pressed the button to stop it. The records do not show a rise. Dyatlov, who was ten metres away, said the test was over and the shift leader simply pointed at the button to shut the reactor down, the way you would at the end of any day. A Soviet commission that spent four years on the records wrote one sentence: "The Commission was unable to establish why the button was pressed."

The rods began to drop. The graphite pushed the water out of the bottom of the core. For a moment the power dipped, and then it rose. Three seconds after the button, every alarm in the room went off at once. The power in the bottom of the reactor shot up to many times what it had been. The fuel got so hot that it shattered into pieces. The pieces hit water, the water flashed to steam, and the steam burst the pipes. Nine seconds after the button the computer recorded a burst fuel pipe. The rods stopped about a third of the way down and would go no further. Akimov cut the power to their motors so they would fall under their own weight. They did not fall.

People outside heard two explosions a few seconds apart. The second one was the bigger. The lid of the reactor, which weighed 2,000 tonnes, was thrown off. An American team that studied the accident wrote that the whole thing, from closing the valves to the explosion, took "only about 40 seconds", and that once the power began to rise fast "there was nothing that could have been done to stop it."

## After

Two people died in the explosion. About 130 workers and firemen got radiation sickness, and 28 of them died within the year. Akimov died on May 11th 1986 and Toptunov on May 14th. Pripyat, the town of about 50,000 people who worked at the station, was emptied within two days, and about 116,000 people were moved out of the worst-hit land that year. A concrete shelter was built over the wreck by November. The harm to the people and the land went on for many years and is still argued about.

That summer, Soviet scientists went to Vienna, in Austria, and explained the accident to experts from all over the world. They said the operators had broken the rules and that was why the reactor exploded. They did not mention the graphite on the bottom of the rods. The world's experts believed them. In July 1987 six men from the station, including Dyatlov, Fomin and the station director, were put on trial, and three of them were sent to labour camps for ten years. Nobody who designed the reactor was tried. The story of the trial is in "[The trial](/deeper/thirty-six-seconds/the-trial/#young)".

Then, slowly, the truth came out. A year later the Soviet scientists admitted the effect of the rods. In 1991 a commission of the Soviet government's own nuclear inspectors went through the design and found that it broke the Soviet Union's own safety rules in fifteen places. In 1992 the international experts wrote a second report that said the design was most of the problem, and that it was "reprehensible" that the flaw had been known for so long and never fixed. The two reports are in "[The two reports](/deeper/thirty-six-seconds/the-two-reports/#young)". Dyatlov came out of prison in 1990 and spent the rest of his life arguing that the design was the only cause. Within weeks of the accident the rules were rewritten for every reactor of this kind in the Soviet Union, and the rods were rebuilt.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**An experiment has to have a safe way to stop.** The people who planned the test thought about the steam, the pumps and the recorder. They did not think about the shutdown button, because every reactor has one and it always works. On that night, in that reactor, it did not. Before you start an experiment, work out how you will end it, and make sure the ending cannot be made dangerous by the experiment itself.

**Writing down a danger is not the same as fixing it.** The engineers measured the problem with the rods in 1983. The scientists wrote a letter about it. The designers wrote to every power station about it. Then everyone went back to work, and nothing was changed. Two and a half years later the people in the control room at Chernobyl had never heard of it. A warning that sits in a file is not a warning.

**If the conditions change, it is a different experiment.** The plan said a quarter power. The reactor was at a sixteenth. The plan assumed plenty of rods inside the reactor. There were almost none. The operators changed the test to fit the reactor they had, and nobody had told them that this made any difference. When you cannot run the experiment the way it was planned, the safe thing is to stop and think, not to carry on with a different one.

**The important number has to be where the person deciding can see it.** The one number that decided what the button would do was worked out by a computer fifty metres away, every quarter of an hour, and shown to nobody. The operators had a stop button, and they had been told it was a stop button. Nobody told them that with eight rods' worth inside, it was a start button.

So the lesson is simple. Before you begin, know how you will stop, and make sure that stopping is the one thing that can never go wrong.
