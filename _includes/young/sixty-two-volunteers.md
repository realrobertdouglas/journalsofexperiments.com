## The worst year

In 1918 a disease called influenza went round the world. Influenza is the illness most people call flu: fever, headache, aching all over, a cough. Usually it is miserable and then it goes away. The influenza of 1918 was different. It killed somewhere between twenty and fifty million people in about a year, more than the First World War, which was ending at the same time.

Nobody knew what caused it. Doctors thought they did. Since 1892 they had believed the cause was a tiny organism called Pfeiffer's bacillus, after the German doctor who found it. A bacillus is a kind of bacterium: a living thing so small you need a microscope, which can be grown in a laboratory in a dish.

Nobody knew exactly how it spread, either. Everyone assumed it travelled in the wet spray that comes out of a person's mouth and nose when they cough, sneeze or talk. Cities put up posters telling people not to cough or spit near others. But assuming is not the same as knowing. Nobody had ever proved it.

In November 1918, in Boston, the United States Navy and the United States Public Health Service decided to find out. They would try to give influenza to healthy people on purpose.

## Who did it

The man in charge was Milton Rosenau. He was 49 years old, the first professor of preventive medicine at Harvard, and he had written the standard American textbook on how to stop people getting ill. During the war he had also joined the Navy, as a lieutenant commander.

Three other doctors worked with him. One was Joseph Goldberger, who a few years earlier had proved what caused a disease called pellagra by feeding eleven prisoners a poor diet until they got it. Another, George McCoy, ran the government's Hygienic Laboratory, the small research laboratory that later grew into the National Institutes of Health. Rosenau had run that same laboratory himself, years before. McCoy went to San Francisco to do the same experiment there at the same time.

The man who lent them the place was Surgeon Donald Currie, who ran the quarantine station in Boston harbour. A quarantine station is where people arriving by ship were kept apart for a while, in case they were carrying a disease. Remember his name. He comes back at the end.

## The island

The experiment was done on Gallops Island, a small island of about sixteen acres in Boston harbour, six miles down the bay from the city. It had about thirty buildings. It was chosen for one reason: if the experiment worked and the volunteers caught influenza, the disease could not spread to anyone else, because there was nobody else there.

The volunteers came from a camp on another island nearby, Deer Island. It was a naval detention camp, where sailors who had broken the rules were sent for training and discipline.

Sixty-eight men put their names down. The report printed all sixty-eight names, which is worth stopping on: a government document that lists, one by one, every young man who volunteered to be given a disease that was killing people all around him. Of the sixty-eight, 62 were actually used in the experiments.

They were young. Fifty-four of the 62 were between 18 and 21. The youngest was 15 and the oldest was 34.

They were told the truth about the risk. The report says they "were warned of the danger and believed, as did those who conducted the study, that they were risking their lives." The Navy's own report said the same thing. Everybody — the doctors and the sailors — expected some of these men to get very ill, and expected that some might die.

## What they did to them

The experiments began on 6th November 1918. There were eight of them, and between them they tried every way anyone could think of to give a person influenza.

**They tried the bacillus.** They grew Pfeiffer's bacillus in the laboratory and dripped it into men's noses. Later they made a mixture of thirteen different strains of it, about two billion of them in every teaspoonful, and sprayed it into the noses and throats of nineteen men, who each breathed in deeply as it was sprayed.

**They tried the sick men's secretions.** Secretions are the wet stuff from inside the nose, throat and chest: the mucus and the spit. They collected it from influenza patients in hospital, mixed it in a bottle with glass beads to break it up, and sprayed it into volunteers' noses and throats, and dropped some into their eyes. Some of the volunteers almost certainly swallowed a little.

**They tried it fresh.** The doctors worried that the material was dying on the journey, so they took the sick men and the well men and put them in the same room. Using cotton swabs, they moved the mucus straight from a sick man's nose into a healthy man's nose, and from throat to throat, in under thirty seconds.

**They tried injecting it.** They filtered the secretions and injected them under the skin. They drew blood from the arms of five influenza patients, mixed it together, and injected it under the skin of ten healthy men within three quarters of an hour.

**And then they tried the obvious thing.** On 26th November 1918, ten of the volunteers were taken to the naval hospital at Chelsea, into a ward where thirty people were ill with influenza. Each volunteer went to a patient's bed, shook his hand, and talked to him for a few minutes. Then he put his face about two inches from the patient's face. The patient breathed out hard, five times, straight into it, while the volunteer breathed in. Then the patient coughed in his face five times. Then the volunteer moved to the next bed and did it again, ten times in all. It took each man between half an hour and fifty minutes. Rosenau later described it as being done "muzzle to muzzle."

Some of the sick men whose mucus was used came off a small navy gunboat called the *Yacona*, which had just come into Boston to unload her sick. On that one ship, 80 of the 95 men aboard had caught influenza in about a fortnight. That is more than eight men in every ten. The disease was clearly, obviously, enormously catching.

## Nothing happened

Not one of the 62 volunteers caught influenza.

One man ran a temperature for a week and the doctors could not quite decide what it was, but thought it was his sore throat. Another had a headache for a day. That was all.

It is hard to overstate how strange this was. The doctors had done everything short of climbing into bed with the patients. They had put the suspected germ up people's noses by the billion. They had injected sick people's blood into healthy people. They had stood men nose to nose with coughing patients. And the disease that was killing a city would not pass.

## The day it stopped

While the healthy volunteers stayed healthy, influenza went on doing what it always did, to everyone else.

Donald Currie, the doctor who ran the island and had lent it to the experimenters, caught influenza. Then he developed pneumonia, an infection of the lungs that often follows it. He died at his home on 23rd December 1918. He was 42, and he left a wife and two children.

That is the same day the experiments stopped. The report says: "The experiments were started November 6, and unavoidably discontinued December 23, 1918."

So on one island, in one winter, two things were true at once. Doctors could not give influenza to sixty-two healthy men who were volunteering to catch it. And influenza killed the man who commanded the island, without being asked.

## What people made of it

The government's own doctors were careful. In January 1919 the Public Health Service published the results and added a note saying that the experiments did not settle anything, that officials should carry on assuming the disease spreads in droplets from coughs, and that people should go to bed at the very first sign of illness.

The newspapers were less careful. The news went out on 13th December 1918 under headlines like "Men Grow Fat on Flu Microbes" and "Can't Get Flu When They Try." One report said the only noticeable result was that the volunteers had better appetites.

And some people took it much further. Within four months an advertisement in a Wyoming newspaper was using the experiment to argue that influenza does not pass from person to person at all. By the end of 1919 a newspaper health column was telling readers not to let anyone frighten them with "tales about germs." People are still using these experiments, today, to argue that flu is not catching and that germs do not cause disease.

Rosenau himself said no such thing. In June 1919 he stood up in front of a meeting of doctors and said: "I think we must be very careful not to draw any positive conclusions from negative results of this kind." He said his volunteers might simply have been immune. And he finished with the sentence he is remembered for:

> As a matter of fact, we entered the outbreak with a notion that we knew the cause of the disease, and were quite sure we knew how it was transmitted from person to person. Perhaps, if we have learned anything, it is that we are not quite sure what we know about the disease.

## Why it did not work

Three things probably went wrong, and we can see them now because of work done long afterwards.

**The volunteers had almost certainly had it already.** This is the big one. Every man came from Deer Island, where 186 men out of about 1,000 had been ill with influenza between September and November. You can catch influenza, fight it off without ever feeling properly ill, and be protected afterwards — and nobody in 1918 could test for that. The doctors asked each man whether he had been ill and wrote down his answer, which is the best they could do, and it was not good enough. The table of volunteers even records that one man, listed as never having had influenza, had slept beside a comrade who was coming down with it.

**They aimed at the wrong place.** The virus spreads best as a very fine mist breathed deep into the lungs. The doctors sprayed and swabbed noses and throats, because the nose and throat were where they thought the germ lived.

**They collected it too late.** A person with influenza is most infectious just before and just as they start to feel ill. The donors had been ill for hours or days. By the time a doctor knows a patient has influenza, the most dangerous moment has usually passed.

And there was one more problem, underneath all of it. There was no test for influenza in 1918. None. No swab, no blood test, nothing. Deciding whether someone had influenza meant a doctor looking at them and making a judgement, in a winter when half the country had a cough. So the experiment could not tell for certain whether its donors had the disease, and could not tell for certain whether its volunteers had caught it.

The one thing the experiment transmitted perfectly was something else entirely: a throat infection caused by a streptococcus, a bacterium the doctors could grow and see in a dish. Volunteer after volunteer got tonsillitis from the sprays. The method worked. It moved the germ they could test for, and could not be shown to move the one they could not.

## How we know influenza really does spread

In 1933, in London, three scientists — Wilson Smith, Christopher Andrewes and Patrick Laidlaw — gave influenza to ferrets using filtered material from human patients, and passed it from ferret to ferret. That finally showed what influenza is: a virus, far smaller than a bacterium, small enough to pass through a filter that stops bacteria. Pfeiffer's bacillus was never the cause.

Much later, scientists rebuilt the actual 1918 virus from tissue kept since the pandemic. In a laboratory, that virus spread through the air from ferret to ferret and infected every animal exposed to it.

So the virus does spread. The experiment was wrong about its own result — but not in the way you might think.

Here is the strangest part. In 2013 a team of British and American scientists tried the experiment again, with everything modern science could give them: a known virus, modern tests, a sealed building with the air controlled. They gave the virus to 52 volunteers, and 42 of them caught it, which was easy. Then they put 75 other healthy volunteers in with them for four days. They expected roughly one in six to catch it. One person did. One, out of seventy-five.

A hundred years later, with every instrument Rosenau did not have, the same experiment failed almost the same way.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A "nothing happened" result only means something if you can make something happen.** When Walter Reed's team in Cuba wanted to know how yellow fever spread, they ran two experiments side by side: one group slept in the dirty bedding of people who had died and stayed well, and another group was bitten by mosquitoes and got ill. The second half is what made the first half mean anything. You can read about that in "[Building Number One](/experiments/2026/10/20/building-number-one.html)". On Gallops Island nobody ever got ill, so the doctors never showed that anything they were using was capable of causing the disease at all. When your experiment never works once, you cannot tell the difference between "this does not spread that way" and "my equipment is empty."

**You cannot measure what you cannot measure.** There was no test for influenza, so every single judgement in the experiment was a doctor's opinion. The one time a volunteer clearly fell ill, a senior doctor said that "in an epidemic the case would surely be called influenza." That is not a diagnosis; that is an honest admission that nobody could tell.

**Your subjects are part of your experiment.** The 62 men were not blank pages. They were young sailors who had just lived through an epidemic in a crowded barracks, which is exactly the group most likely to be already protected. In Japan, at the very same time, Professor Yamanouchi did almost the same experiment on people who had not had influenza, and 18 out of 18 of them caught it. The six in his group who had already had influenza stayed well. Same methods, same months, opposite answers — and the difference was who was in the room.

**Other people decide what your result means.** Rosenau said "be careful, this proves nothing." The health service said "carry on, and go to bed early." The newspapers said germs make you hungry. Within four months, strangers were using his work to tell people that disease is not catching. Once you publish a result, you do not own it any more, and the careful warnings are the first thing people drop.

So the lesson is simple. An experiment that finds nothing has told you something about your experiment, and usually not very much about the world. Before you believe a "nothing happened," ask whether anything could have happened — whether the people, the method and the measurement were all capable of showing you a result if there had been one. Sixty-two brave young men were sprayed, swabbed, injected and coughed on, and the honest answer at the end of it was Rosenau's: we are not quite sure what we know.
