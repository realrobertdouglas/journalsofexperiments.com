## A cold January

Fort Dix is an Army camp in New Jersey where new soldiers are trained. In January 1976 it was very cold, and thousands of recruits arrived after Christmas to start their training. Within two weeks many of them were ill with coughs and fevers. Most had an ordinary flu that was going round the whole country that winter. But when the state laboratory tested samples from the sick soldiers, it found a few viruses it could not recognise.

On February 4th 1976 a recruit called David Lewis got out of his sick bed to go on a five-mile night march with his unit. He collapsed and died. A sample from his throat grew the same unknown virus.

On February 12th the government's disease laboratory in Atlanta, the Center for Disease Control, worked out what it was. It was swine influenza: a flu virus that normally lives in pigs. Nobody under fifty had any protection against it, because it had not been seen in people since the 1920s. And scientists believed it was the same kind of virus that had caused the great influenza of 1918, which killed about half a million Americans and more than twenty million people around the world.

## Who ran it

The man in charge of the Center for Disease Control was David Sencer. He had run it for ten years and knew everything about it. Above him in Washington were Theodore Cooper, a heart surgeon who was the government's chief health official, and David Mathews, the Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare, who had come from running a university. Above them was President Gerald Ford, who was fighting to keep his job in an election year.

The Army investigated the camp. The New Jersey state epidemiologist, Martin Goldfield, investigated the towns around it. An epidemiologist is a doctor who studies how diseases spread through groups of people.

## What they were trying to find out

The big question was this: was the Fort Dix virus the start of a new worldwide epidemic, like 1918? And if it was, could the whole United States be vaccinated before it arrived in the autumn?

Nobody knew the answer to the first question. The experts on the government's vaccine committee privately guessed the chance was somewhere between two percent and twenty percent, which is to say, between one in fifty and one in five. But they did not say those numbers out loud. They said the chance was "unknown." When the President's advisers asked, that was the answer they got.

Meanwhile the virus itself had done something nobody paid much attention to. The soldiers who caught it were all taken ill between January 19th and February 9th. After that, no more. By the end of February the Army had tested the blood of over a thousand recruits and found that about two hundred had been infected, but nobody new was catching it. New recruits arriving at the camp in late February and March stayed healthy. Goldfield's laboratory tested hundreds of sick people in the towns around the camp and found only the ordinary flu. The swine virus never left Fort Dix.

## What they built

Sencer wrote a nine-page memorandum. He wrote it carefully, so that it would be very hard to say no to. It said the virus was related to the 1918 virus. It said there was "a strong possibility" of a national epidemic in the winter. It said the choice was "go or no go," and that the decision had to be made within two weeks, because vaccine is grown in eggs and the drug companies were about to kill off the roosters that fathered them. It said the aim should be to vaccinate "213 million people in three months": everyone in the country.

Sencer took the memorandum to Mathews on March 15th. Mathews asked what the probability of an epidemic was. "Unknown," he was told. Later he said that at that moment the decision was already made: no politician could say afterwards, if the epidemic came, that the chance had seemed too small to bother about.

On March 24th 1976 President Ford met a room full of famous scientists, including Jonas Salk and Albert Sabin, the two men who had made the polio vaccines. He asked for a show of hands. Every hand went up. Then he went to the press room and announced that he was asking Congress for $135 million "to inoculate every man, woman, and child in the United States," and asking every American to get a shot that autumn. "No one knows exactly how serious this threat could be," he said. "Nevertheless, we cannot afford to take a chance with the health of our Nation."

Congress gave the money in three weeks. The drug companies started growing vaccine. The Center for Disease Control built a plan for every state, bought jet injectors (guns that fire vaccine through the skin without a needle), and built something new: a surveillance centre, staffed day and night, with a computer, to watch for the epidemic and to count every illness that happened to people after they were vaccinated. Before a single shot was given, the staff worked out how many old people would be expected to die of heart attacks on any ordinary day, so that when some died the day after a vaccination, the numbers could be compared.

## What went wrong in the summer

Three things went wrong before anyone was vaccinated.

First, the vaccine did not work well in children. Tests in June showed that one dose gave children fever but little protection, and nobody had tested two doses. Children were left out until November.

Second, the insurance companies refused to insure the drug companies against being sued. Without insurance the companies would not put the vaccine into bottles. A hundred million doses sat in vats all through July while the government, the companies, the insurers and Congress argued. In the end Congress passed a law in August saying that anyone injured by the vaccine would sue the government, not the companies. The law only took effect on October 1st, so nobody could be vaccinated before then.

Third, there was less vaccine than promised. By October 1st about 25 million doses were ready, not 200 million.

And all through the summer, there was no swine flu anywhere in the world.

## The programme

The shots began on October 1st 1976. On October 11th, at one clinic in Pittsburgh, three old people with heart disease died within hours of being vaccinated. Newspapers and television began counting deaths. The Center for Disease Control counted too, and showed that the people who died after shots were old and ill, had died of heart attacks, and were fewer than the number you would expect to die on any ordinary day. On October 14th President Ford had his own shot in front of the cameras, and the programme went on. By the middle of November more than six million people a week were being vaccinated.

## The count

Guillain-Barré syndrome is a rare illness in which the nerves stop working and the body becomes paralysed, usually starting in the legs. Most people recover, but some do not, and some die. In 1976 about five thousand Americans a year got it, from many different causes.

In the middle of November a doctor in Minnesota reported a patient who had become paralysed after a flu shot. The state's immunization officer, Denton Peterson, telephoned the surveillance centre in Atlanta. Nobody there was very interested in one case. Peterson looked it up in the medical books and found that it had happened before. "We felt we were sitting on a bomb," he said later. Within a week he had three more cases, one of them fatal. He telephoned again. The same day, Alabama reported three cases, and the next day New Jersey reported one.

Now the surveillance centre went to work. Four states asked every nerve specialist for every case of the syndrome since the end of September, and counted how many of the patients had been vaccinated and how many had not. Because about a third of adults had been vaccinated by then, you would expect about a third of the patients to have been vaccinated if the vaccine had nothing to do with it. Instead, most of them had. In the four states the rate of paralysis among vaccinated people was about seven times the rate among unvaccinated people.

On the morning of December 16th 1976 Sencer held a telephone conference with twenty experts. They agreed the programme should be suspended for a month. Sencer telephoned Cooper, who was eating lunch in the White House. Cooper telephoned Salk in Paris. Then Cooper, Mathews and the President's adviser walked down the hall to Ford, who listened, sighed and agreed. That afternoon the programme stopped. It never started again.

By then more than forty million Americans had been vaccinated in ten weeks, more than in any flu season before. The swine flu had never come.

## What happened afterwards

The counting went on. By the end of January 1977 every state had reported its cases: 1,098 people with the syndrome in four months, 532 of them vaccinated. When the scientists did the arithmetic, the vaccine had caused just under one extra case of paralysis for every hundred thousand people who received it. A later study put the number of cases caused by the vaccine at between 211 and 246, and the risk of dying from it at about one in two million.

President Carter's new health secretary, Joseph Califano, held a public meeting in February 1977 and ended the programme. The same day, he dismissed Sencer.

A government audit in June 1977 said the department "lost its gamble." Two Harvard professors, Richard Neustadt and Harvey Fineberg, wrote the official history in 1978 and concluded that going ahead without more evidence "was a mistake." Sencer never agreed. Thirty years later he wrote: "When lives are at stake, it is better to err on the side of overreaction than underreaction."

Where the Fort Dix virus came from, and why it stopped, nobody has ever found out.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**One decision was really two.** The drug companies needed an answer within two weeks about whether to make the vaccine. Nobody needed an answer that quickly about whether to inject it. But the memorandum tied the two together, "go or no go," and after that nobody ever asked the second question on its own. One scientist, Russell Alexander, asked at the very first meeting: at what point do we stop and store the vaccine instead of giving it? Nobody answered him. If you have to decide something before you know enough, write down what would make you change your mind, and when you will check.

**"Unknown" is not a number.** The experts privately thought the chance of an epidemic was somewhere between one in fifty and one in five. They would not say so, because saying a number felt unscientific. So they said "unknown." But "unknown" cannot get smaller. In March, with the virus fresh, it was unknown. In July, after five months with no case anywhere in the world, it was still unknown, and so the President was told nothing had changed. A number could have been lowered week by week. A word could not.

**Listen to the instrument that says nothing is happening.** By March the Army's blood tests already showed that the virus had stopped spreading in early February. The planners knew this. They decided it proved nothing, because flu can hide and come back. That was true. But the stopping was the only evidence there was going to be, and the plan had no place for it. If the only reading you will accept is "it is here," you do not really have an instrument.

**The count that worked was the one they did not want.** The surveillance centre was built to spot the epidemic and to show the vaccine was safe. Instead it found a rare paralysis, about one case in every hundred thousand shots, and it found it within a few weeks, quickly enough to stop the programme. No vaccination campaign had ever been stopped by its own count before. But there is a twist. If the swine flu had really been spreading, one case of paralysis in a hundred thousand would have been a price worth paying, and the same count would have been read as good news. An instrument tells you what happened. Whether to stop depends on what else is happening.

So the lesson is simple. When you must act before you know, build two things: a way to find out whether the danger is real, and a way to find out what your action is costing. Then read both, especially the one that tells you what you do not want to hear.
