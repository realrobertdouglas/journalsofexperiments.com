## The hospital with two doors

In the 1840s the biggest hospital in the world for women having babies was in Vienna, the capital of Austria. It was part of the General Hospital, which had opened in 1784. Any woman could go there to have her baby for nothing, on two conditions. She had to let the students watch and examine her, because the hospital was where doctors and midwives were trained. And if she was healthy, she had to stay for a while afterwards to feed babies in the foundling hospital next door, where babies with no parents were kept. Most of the women were poor and unmarried.

The lying-in hospital, which is what a hospital for childbirth was called, had two parts, called the First Clinic and the Second Clinic. From 1840 all the medical students, who were men, were taught on the First Clinic, and all the pupil midwives, who were women, were taught on the Second. A midwife is a woman trained to help at births. The two clinics were in the same building, so close that they shared a waiting room.

The women were sent to one clinic or the other by a timetable. On Monday at four in the afternoon the First Clinic opened its door for new arrivals and kept it open for twenty-four hours. Then the Second Clinic took over for twenty-four hours. Then the First again, then the Second, and so on through the week. A woman in labour went to whichever clinic held the door when she got there. She could not choose. The day of the week chose for her.

That timetable is the most important thing in this story. Because of it, the two clinics got exactly the same kind of women, about four thousand a year each, with the same food, the same sheets and the same weather. Anything that was different between the two clinics had to come from the clinics themselves. Nobody had planned that.

## The thing that was different

One thing was very different: how many of the mothers died.

A few days after giving birth, a woman could get a fever, with a racing pulse, a swollen belly and terrible pain, and in most cases she died of it within a week. It was called childbed fever. Every hospital of the time had it. But on the First Clinic, the doctors' clinic, it was far worse than on the Second. In the six years from 1841 to 1846, about one woman in ten died on the First Clinic. On the Second Clinic, the midwives' clinic, it was about one in thirty. In 1846 it was one in nine on the First and one in thirty-seven on the Second.

The women knew. Some of them, on their way to the Second Clinic, lost their way in the big building and ended up on the First, where they saw a room full of men and understood which clinic they were in. Ignaz Semmelweis, who worked there, wrote that he saw such women "kneeling and wringing their hands," begging to be let out. Women who were already dying of the fever swore they were perfectly well, because they knew that once the doctors started treating you, you did not get better.

Semmelweis also wrote about a bell. When a woman was dying, a priest came to give her the last rites, and a server walked ahead of him ringing a little bell. To reach the sick room on the First Clinic the priest had to walk through five other wards, so the women in the beds heard the bell several times a day. So did Semmelweis, in his room. "This little bell was a painful admonition to search for this unknown cause with all my strength," he wrote. He asked the priests to come by a back way without the bell. They did, and just as many women died.

## Who he was

Ignaz Semmelweis was born on July 1st 1818 in Buda, which is now half of Budapest, the capital of Hungary. His father was a merchant. He went to Vienna to study law, went to an anatomy lecture with a friend, and changed to medicine. He became a doctor in 1844 and asked for the job of assistant on the First Clinic. The assistant was the professor's second-in-command, who lived in the hospital and examined every woman in labour before the professor's morning round.

He got the job, properly, on March 20th 1847, when he was twenty-eight. He had just come back from a holiday in Venice. The first news he heard was that his friend Jakob Kolletschka, a professor who taught by doing post-mortems, was dead. A post-mortem is the examination of a dead body to find out why the person died. During one, a student's knife had slipped and cut Kolletschka's finger. The finger had swollen, then the arm, and in a few days he had died of what was called blood poisoning, with inflammation in his chest, his belly and the membranes of his brain.

Semmelweis read the report of Kolletschka's post-mortem and saw something that nobody else had seen. Kolletschka had died of exactly the same thing the mothers died of. He had seen hundreds of post-mortems of women dead of childbed fever, and the inflamed chest, belly and brain were the same. "Day and night," he wrote, "the picture of Kolletschka's illness pursued me."

## The idea

Kolletschka had died because something from a dead body had got into a cut in his finger. "Not the wound," Semmelweis wrote, "but the contamination of the wound by cadaveric particles had brought about his death." Cadaveric particles meant tiny bits of dead body. Nobody knew about germs yet.

Then he asked himself the question. Were bits of dead body getting into the mothers too? "To this question I had to answer yes."

Here is why. Vienna's doctors were proud of their post-mortems. Every morning the students and the assistant went to the dead-house and dissected, and then walked straight to the labour room and examined the women with their hands. They washed with soap, but Semmelweis noticed that a hand that had been inside a dead body still smelt of it for hours, so soap was not taking everything off. The midwives on the Second Clinic did not do post-mortems. They practised on a leather model. And the women who gave birth in the street on the way to the hospital, who were not examined at all, hardly ever got the fever.

So his idea had two parts. First, the fever and Kolletschka's blood poisoning were the same disease. Second, the doctors were carrying it to the mothers on their hands, and if the hands could be cleaned properly, the mothers would stop dying.

## The basin

In the second half of May 1847, Semmelweis put a basin at the door of the labour room. In it was a solution of chlorinated lime, a bleaching powder that smells of chlorine, like a swimming pool. He chose it because chlorine took the smell of the dead-house off the hands. The rule was simple: nobody, himself included, could examine a woman without first washing his hands in the basin. At first he used a liquid chlorine solution, but it was expensive, so he changed to chlorinated lime, which was cheap.

Then he counted. The Second Clinic went on as before, and gave him something to compare with.

In April 1847, before the basin, 312 women gave birth on the First Clinic and 57 of them died, which is more than one in six. In May, 294 gave birth and 36 died. In June, after the basin, 268 gave birth and 6 died. In July, 3 died. In August, 5. For the seven months from June to December 1847, about 1,800 women gave birth and 56 died, which is about one in thirty-three. The First Clinic had become as safe as the Second.

The year 1848 was the first whole year with the basin. About 3,600 women gave birth on the First Clinic and 45 died, which is about one in eighty. In March not one mother died, and in August not one. The midwives' clinic that year lost 43 of about 3,200. For the first time, the doctors' clinic had done slightly better than the midwives'.

## One more lesson from the ward

In October 1847 a woman came in to give birth who had a cancer of the womb, which gave off a foul discharge. The examiners went from her to the next woman, and the next, washing with soap in between, which the rule allowed. Of twelve women who gave birth that day, eleven died. Semmelweis changed the rule: hands were to be washed in chlorine between every patient, not just at the door. And he changed his idea. Anything rotten, from a living person or a dead one, could carry the fever. But most people only ever heard the first version, about dead bodies, and argued against that.

## Why people did not believe him

You might think the hospital would have been overjoyed. Some people were. Three famous Vienna doctors, Rokitansky, Skoda and Hebra, believed him at once. Hebra wrote up the result in a medical journal in December 1847, since Semmelweis would not, and in April 1848 he called it a discovery as important as vaccination. A young English doctor, Charles Routh, who had worked on the First Clinic, read a paper about it in London in November 1848 and called the fall in deaths "a speaking fact." A professor in Kiel, in Germany, tried the washing at once and it worked.

But the professor of the First Clinic, Johann Klein, was not pleased, and he was Semmelweis's boss. Skoda asked the university to set up a committee to check the whole thing properly, and the university agreed by a big majority; then Klein complained to the government and the committee was forbidden to meet. When Semmelweis's two years as assistant ended, in March 1849, he was not kept on. Klein would not let him see the clinic's records any more, and said that publishing them would be informing against the hospital.

There were other reasons. Semmelweis hated writing. He said so himself: he had "an inborn aversion to everything called writing." For thirteen years he did not publish a single page of his own about the discovery. Other people wrote it up for him, in short pieces, and they wrote down the first version of his idea, the one about dead bodies. Doctors in other cities pointed out that their hospitals had the fever too, with hardly any post-mortems, and said that proved him wrong. Nobody could explain how a tiny amount of something on a hand could kill a healthy woman, because nobody knew about germs. And a colleague named Zipfl went through his own old records and found that the women examined soonest after a post-mortem had died least, which he himself said was probably just chance.

In May 1850 Semmelweis finally gave a talk to the Vienna Society of Physicians, three years after the basin. In October 1850 the university at last gave him permission to teach, but only on the leather model, not on real patients. He took that as an insult, and five days later he left Vienna for ever and went home to Hungary.

## What happened in Vienna after he left

The basin stayed. The rule did not. Without Semmelweis watching, the students washed less carefully, and the deaths went up again. By 1854 the First Clinic was losing one mother in eleven, almost as bad as before he came. The chlorine was still there. The checking was not.

## What happened to him

In Hungary he ran a hospital ward where there were no students and almost nobody dissected, and over six summers 933 women gave birth there and 8 died of the fever. In 1855 he became professor of obstetrics, which is the medicine of childbirth, at the university in Pest. In 1860 he finally wrote his book. It was more than five hundred pages long, full of tables, and full of anger at everyone who had argued with him. The next year he wrote open letters to the leading professors of Europe, telling them that their teaching was murdering mothers and that "not to be of my opinion is the same as 'to be a murderer'."

In July 1865, when he was forty-seven, his mind gave way. His wife and his colleagues took him by train to Vienna, where his old friend Hebra took him to the asylum, the hospital for people who were mentally ill. When he tried to leave, six attendants held him down, put him in a strait-jacket and shut him in a dark room. The doctors found a wound on one of his fingers that had gone bad. He died two weeks later, on August 13th 1865, of blood poisoning, the same disease that had killed Kolletschka and thousands of mothers.

By then the world had begun to catch up. In 1863 the professor of the Second Clinic in Vienna, after a terrible winter in which one mother in eight died on his own clinic, stood up and recommended washing the hands in chlorinated lime, keeping anything rotten out of the wards and separating the sick from the well. He did not mention Semmelweis's name. A few years later a surgeon in Glasgow called Lister began using chemicals to kill germs in wounds, and after that doctors slowly came to understand why the basin had worked. Today Semmelweis is one of the most famous doctors who ever lived, and the washing of hands is the first thing every nurse and doctor learns.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**The experiment was already running. Somebody had to read it.** The timetable sent the same kind of women to two clinics on alternate days for seven years, and the hospital counted the dead every month. The comparison was sitting in the records. It is the same thing that happened in the story of [Thirty-Eight Grains](/experiments/2026/10/21/thirty-eight-grains.html), where two water companies had sorted the people of south London without meaning to. In both stories, one man noticed that an accident had made a fair test, and read it. Reading it was the work.

**You can test an idea by changing one thing and counting.** Semmelweis did not just have a theory. He put a basin at the door, changed nothing else, and watched the number fall from one in six to one in thirty, and then to one in eighty, while the clinic next door stayed the same. That is what an experiment is.

**A result nobody writes down is a result nobody can check.** He waited thirteen years to write his book. In the meantime other people wrote short versions, and the short versions were argued with. In the story of [Two Oranges and a Lemon](/experiments/2026/10/19/two-oranges-and-a-lemon.html), James Lind proved that oranges cured scurvy and then waited six years to publish; the Navy took forty-eight years to act. Semmelweis waited thirteen, and the doctors took about twenty. When you find something out, write it down properly, and soon.

**A rule only works while somebody is checking.** The basin stayed in Vienna after Semmelweis left, but the deaths went back up, because nobody was making sure the hands were washed every time. A good rule is not the same as a rule that is kept.

**Being right is not enough, and being angry does not help.** Semmelweis was right, and he called the people who disagreed with him murderers. They did not change their minds because of that. They changed their minds, years later, when they had an explanation of their own for why he had been right. People can agree with a number and still refuse to act on it until they understand it.

So the lesson is simple. When the world hands you a fair comparison, read it carefully, change one thing, count, and then write it all down while people can still check it. And remember that a rule is only as good as the person watching the basin.
