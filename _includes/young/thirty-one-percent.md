## The idea

A hurricane is a huge storm that spins around a calm centre called the eye. Around the eye is a wall of cloud, the eyewall, and that is where the strongest winds are. The tighter the ring of cloud around the eye, the faster the wind.

In 1961 an American weather scientist named Robert Simpson had an idea for making hurricanes weaker. High up in the clouds, water can stay liquid even when it is colder than freezing. Scientists call it supercooled water. If you drop a chemical called silver iodide into it, the water freezes into ice crystals, and freezing gives off heat. Simpson thought that if you froze the water in the clouds just outside the eyewall, the heat would make the eyewall move outwards. A bigger eye means a slower spin, the way a skater slows down when she stretches her arms out. He thought the strongest winds might drop by 10 or 15 per cent.

The silver iodide came from the Navy. Chemists at a Navy weapons station at China Lake, in the California desert, had found a way to make a smoke full of silver iodide by burning a flare. In May 1961 one of them, Pierre Saint-Amand, went to Washington and burned a little of it in Simpson's office. Simpson wanted to try it in a hurricane that very summer.

The project that grew out of it was called Project Stormfury. It was run by the Weather Bureau, which later became part of NOAA, the United States ocean and weather agency, together with the Navy. It lasted from 1962 to 1983.

## The first try: Esther

The Navy built the flares in eight weeks. They were called Cyclops. On September 16th 1961 a Navy jet bomber flew into Hurricane Esther, far out in the Atlantic, and dropped eight of them into the wall of cloud. Nothing went to plan. The jet's engines would not start until a mechanic stopped a leak with his bare hand. The eye was too small to circle in, and the cloud wall was far higher than the jet could fly, so the pilot dumped fuel to climb.

That evening, back at the base in Puerto Rico, everyone looked at the film of the radar screen. Half of the cloud wall seemed to vanish, and the eye changed shape for about an hour and a half. Everyone agreed something dramatic had happened. The next day the jet dropped eight more flares, but they missed the cloud and fell into the eye, and nothing happened at all.

One hit and one miss, and the storm had changed on the day of the hit. It looked like proof. But the Navy's own report noticed something: nobody had looked at the parts of the storm that were not seeded, so there was nothing to compare with. The companion piece, "[Cyclops](/deeper/thirty-one-percent/cyclops/#young)", tells the story of the flares and the first drop.

## Beulah and the empty years

In August 1963 the project seeded Hurricane Beulah on two days. On the first day the flares missed and nothing happened. On the second day they hit, and the crews said the eyewall broke up and a new one formed farther out, and the strongest winds dropped by about 20 per cent. Again: a miss and nothing, a hit and a change.

Then the project waited six years. There was a rule: a hurricane could only be seeded if there was almost no chance, 10 per cent or less, of it coming within 50 miles of land in the next day. That kept people safe. It also meant that most hurricanes were never allowed. In 1967, for the first time in fourteen years, not one hurricane qualified. In 1968, none again. The companion piece, "[The years without a storm](/deeper/thirty-one-percent/the-years-without-a-storm/#young)", is about the rules and the waiting.

While they waited, the scientists built a computer model of a hurricane and asked it where heat should be added to weaken the winds. The model said: outside the ring of strongest winds, never inside it. It said the winds might drop by about 15 per cent.

## Debbie

The alert came on Saturday, August 16th 1969. Hurricane Debbie was coming across the Atlantic towards the right area. By Sunday thirteen aircraft were waiting in Puerto Rico: Navy jets to do the seeding, Navy radar planes, two Weather Bureau DC-6 planes to measure the wind, and Air Force planes to watch from high up.

On Monday, August 18th, the first Navy jet flew into Debbie at 33,000 feet, crossed the eye, and dropped 208 flares in a line leading away from the centre. Two hours later another jet did the same, and then a third, a fourth and a fifth. Over a thousand flares went into the storm that day. All day, one of the DC-6s flew back and forth across the hurricane at 12,000 feet, measuring the wind.

In the morning, before the first flare, the strongest wind was 98 knots, which is about 180 kilometres an hour. Five hours after the last flare, it was 68 knots. The wind had dropped by 31 per cent.

On Tuesday the storm built itself back up. On Wednesday they did it all again. The strongest wind went from 99 knots to 84: down 15 per cent.

There was one odd thing on the Wednesday. The radar showed Debbie had two rings of cloud around her eye, one inside the other. The scientists thought this showed the seeding was working: the inner ring was weakening first. Keep that picture in mind.

## What the scientists said

The scientists were careful. Their report said the winds had definitely dropped after the seeding. Then it said, in plain words, that they did not know whether the seeding had caused it, or whether the hurricane had just changed on its own, because hurricanes do change by that much by themselves. Then it said that the data "strongly suggest" the experiment had worked.

Why did they believe it? Because the wind fell at about the time the computer model said it should, and by about as much. And because a drop that big was rare: when they looked through records of other storms, a change like the one on the Monday had happened in only about 9 per cent of the cases.

What they did not have was the one thing that would settle it: a careful record of what hurricanes do on days when nobody touches them. The only planes that could make such a record were the project's own, and they were kept for the experiment.

The 31 per cent became famous. The project got more money and was named a national pilot project. Reporters flew on the planes. The scientists worked out that if they could weaken just one big hurricane by 10 per cent, it would pay back the whole cost of the research ten times over.

Inside the laboratory, though, the idea was already changing. Measurements showed there was not nearly enough supercooled water in the clouds for freezing to give off the heat the model needed. As the 1970 report put it, once the water in a cloud has frozen, "one cannot keep refreezing it to get more heat." So the scientists changed the explanation: the seeding worked, they now said, by making clouds grow taller, not by freezing water. The result stayed. The reason for it had been swapped.

## Waiting again

Then the hurricanes stopped coming. In 1970 no storm qualified. In 1971 the only one was Ginger, which was a big, messy storm with no small clear eye, the wrong kind for the experiment. They seeded her anyway and nothing happened. Ginger was the last hurricane the project ever seeded. In 1972 every storm was too weak, too close to land, or too far away. The Navy pulled out in 1973. The old Weather Bureau planes were worn out and were retired.

The project's answer was to go to the Pacific, where there are two or three times as many big storms, which are called typhoons there. The plan was to fly from the island of Guam from 1976 with two new aeroplanes. But Japan and China objected to having their typhoons experimented on, and the plan was dropped. Plans for the Pacific coast of Mexico and for Australia ran into the same trouble. In 1974, when a hurricane called Fifi caused terrible damage in Honduras, people said the Americans had done it, and the government had to explain that it had not seeded anything since 1971.

## The new aeroplanes

The two new aeroplanes arrived anyway, in 1976 and 1977. They were Lockheed P-3 Orions, and they were much better than anything the project had flown before. They had radars that could see the rain in three dimensions, and probes that photographed every drop and crystal that passed through them, so they could tell liquid water from ice.

For the first time, the project flew through big hurricanes it had no plan to seed: Anita in 1977, David and Frederic in 1979, Allen in 1980. And for the first time it measured the things the idea had always assumed.

## What they found

They found two things, and together they ended the project.

The first was about the clouds. Hurricane clouds, it turned out, do not hold much supercooled water at all. The air in them rises slowly, so the rain either falls out before it reaches freezing height or freezes as soon as it gets there. In the clouds outside the eyewall, where the seeding was supposed to happen, the scientists counted 40 or 50 natural ice crystals in every litre of air. The seeding was designed to add about 10. The clouds were already frozen. Silver iodide could do nothing to them.

The second thing was about the storms, and it went right back to Debbie. In 1979 and 1980 the new planes watched Hurricane David and Hurricane Allen grow a second ring of cloud outside the eyewall, exactly like the two rings on Debbie's radar. Then the outer ring grew stronger and moved inward, the inner ring died, the eye got bigger, and the strongest winds fell. Nobody had seeded David or Allen. Big hurricanes just do this, on their own, every so often. It is now called an eyewall replacement cycle.

So in 1985 the project's own scientists went back and looked at the four days when the seeding had seemed to work. On three of them, Esther, Beulah, and Debbie's second day, the storm already had a second ring forming before the first flare was dropped. The storm was already doing what the seeding was meant to make it do. Only Debbie's first day, the day of the 31 per cent, could not be explained that way, and one day is not enough to prove anything.

The project ended in 1983. In 1985 four of its scientists, Hugh Willoughby, David Jorgensen, Robert Black and Stanley Rosenthal, who had built the computer model himself, wrote its history for a science journal. They said the goal had been "captivating" and had seemed possible at the start. They said the idea had two fatal flaws: there was too little supercooled water and too much ice in hurricanes for seeding to work, and the changes seeding was supposed to cause were often the same as the changes hurricanes make on their own. And they said that if the project had paid more attention to how unseeded storms change over a day, it might have seen the problem much sooner.

The two aeroplanes bought to seed typhoons never seeded anything. They are still flying into hurricanes today, measuring them.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A number is not an answer until you know what would have happened anyway.** Debbie's winds really did fall by 31 per cent on the day she was seeded. That was true. But to say the seeding caused it, you need to know what Debbie would have done with no planes in the sky. Nobody knew, because nobody had ever watched a big hurricane that closely on a day they left it alone. Without that, the number is just a description of one day.

**If you only ever look at the days you did something, every change looks like a result.** Esther changed on the day the flares hit and not on the day they missed. Beulah too. Debbie changed twice. It looked like cause and effect. But hurricanes change on their own, and the project never watched the days it did nothing. So the hits looked like successes and the misses looked like mistakes, and there was no way to tell.

**Write down what you are assuming, and test it.** The scientists tested the physics: they argued about it, modelled it, and in the end measured it and gave it up. But the other assumption, that a change after seeding meant the seeding caused it, was never written down as something that could be tested. It was mentioned every year as a difficulty, and then set aside. A difficulty you mention every year is not the same as a question you try to answer.

**Be suspicious of a result that outlives its explanation.** By 1970 the scientists knew there was not enough supercooled water for the original idea to work. They kept the result and changed the explanation. That is the moment the number took over from the science.

**It is honest to say so.** The people who ended the project were its own scientists. They wrote it up under their own names, said what had been wrong, and said that the idea had seemed a good one at the time. That takes more courage than being right.

So the lesson is simple. Before you say your experiment worked, find out what would have happened if you had done nothing. Measure that too, with the same care, and with the best instruments you have. The storms were doing it the whole time. Nobody was there to watch.
