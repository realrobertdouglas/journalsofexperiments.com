## A house on the sea floor

In 1969 four scientists lived for two months in a steel house on the floor of a bay in the Virgin Islands, and never once came up for air. The house was called Tektite, after the small glassy stones that fall from space and land in the sea. It stood in about fifty feet of water in Great Lameshur Bay, on the island of St John, a United States national park in the Caribbean.

The house was two white steel tanks, each about as wide as a small room and as tall as a two-storey building, standing side by side on a base and joined by a short tunnel. Each tank had two floors. On one side was the bridge, where the controls and the laboratory were, with the crew's bedroom below it. On the other side was the engine room, with the wet room below. In the floor of the wet room was a hatch that opened straight into the sea. The water did not come in because the air inside the house was kept at the same pressure as the sea outside, pushing down on it like a hand.

That pressure was the whole trick. When a diver breathes air under pressure, gas dissolves into his body, and if he comes up too fast it fizzes out like the bubbles in a shaken bottle, which can hurt or kill him. Ordinary divers can only stay down a short time. But after a day or so under pressure the body is full, and it cannot take up any more: the divers call this being "saturated". After that, a diver can stay down as long as he likes and pay for it with one long, slow climb back to normal pressure at the very end. The Tektite crew could swim out of the hatch as often as they wanted, and come back to a dry house, for as long as the food lasted. The price was that they could not come up quickly. Getting back to the surface safely would take about twenty hours.

## Who built it and why

Three parts of the American government paid for Tektite, and each wanted something different. The Navy wanted to learn about living on the sea floor. The Department of the Interior, which employed the four scientists, wanted to know whether a house like this was a useful tool for studying the sea. And NASA, the space agency, wanted something that had nothing to do with water.

NASA was planning to send crews into space for months at a time, and it wanted to know how small groups of people behave when they are shut in together, far from home, doing hard and dangerous work. It is very hard to test that on Earth. People who are shut in a room for an experiment know it is only an experiment, and they can always knock on the door and leave. The people in Tektite could not leave. They had real work to do, real danger around them, and no quick way out. In November 1966, psychologists from NASA and the Navy had agreed in a corridor that a crew on the sea floor was the closest thing to a crew in space that anyone could watch. Two of those psychologists, Roland Radloff and Robert Helmreich, had already watched the Navy's divers in a habitat called Sealab II in 1965.

So the General Electric company built the house, mostly with its own money, and the Navy shipped it to the Virgin Islands and winched it to the bottom. The experiment was really three experiments at once. The doctors would find out whether people could live for two months breathing a mixture that was 92 percent nitrogen and only 8 percent oxygen. The scientists would study the reef and its lobsters. And the psychologists would watch the scientists.

## Watching

Every room in the house had a television camera and a microphone that was always on. The pictures and the sound went up a cable to a van on a barge near the shore. In the van, behind a folding wall, sat two young sailors with six television screens in front of them, and they watched the crew from a quarter past six in the morning until a quarter to midnight, every day for sixty days.

They did not write sentences. Every fifteen minutes one of them punched a card, a piece of stiff paper with holes in it that a computer could read, recording where each of the four men was, what he was doing, and whether he was talking. The other sailor punched cards for every meal, every trip from one room to another, every conversation and every call to the surface. Switches on the stove, the radio and the television, and buttons by the hatch, recorded themselves on paper tape. Every week the cards were flown to Washington and run through a computer. By the end of the sixty days there were more than four hundred thousand of these little observations.

The sailors had been given a rule for how to think about the job. Each one, the manual said, "was asked to consider himself the scorer of a ball game."

The crew knew they were being watched. In the first days they held up hand-written signs to the cameras, and afterwards they joked about the "shrinks". Only six times in sixty days did they switch a camera off. But they switched the microphones off fifty-four times, and the psychologists worked out why: the crew wanted to complain about the people on the surface without the people on the surface hearing.

## Sixty days

The four men went in at eleven in the morning on February 15th 1969. Their names were Richard Waller, who was in charge, Conrad Mahnken, John VanDerwalker and Edward Clifton. Things went wrong at once. The machine that cleaned the carbon dioxide out of the air did not work as well as it had in tests, and for two weeks the crew had to change its chemical every four hours, day and night, taking turns to stay awake. All four got ear infections from the water. One of them was kept out of the sea by the doctors for twelve days.

But the house held. Around March 17th they passed the record for the longest stay under the sea, which had been thirty days. By the end they were each spending five hours a day in the water, swimming as far as a third of a mile from the house, following lobsters that they had fitted with tiny sound-beacons, and mapping the sea floor. At four in the morning on April 15th they left the house, and about twenty hours later they stepped out on to the surface. The doctors could find nothing wrong with them. The President of the United States sent a telegram: "The aquanauts join the astronauts as space pioneers."

## Tektite II

The next year, 1970, the house went back into the same bay, and this time it was busy. Between April 4th and November 6th, eleven crews of five lived in it in turn, most of them for two or three weeks, fifty-three people in all. Each crew had four scientists and one engineer to look after the house. The watching got bigger too. Now the van was on the hillside, the watchers were students from the University of Texas, and they watched twenty-four hours a day, in pairs, punching a card for each person every six minutes. Over 182 days they recorded more than thirty-five thousand observations on every single person.

One crew was all women, and the newspapers came for it. Mission 6-50, from July 6th to July 20th, was led by Sylvia Earle, a botanist, with three other scientists and an engineer named Margaret Lucas. In two weeks Earle found 154 kinds of seaweed in the bay, twenty-six of them never seen in the Virgin Islands before, and showed with wire cages that the reef was bare of plants because the fish ate them. The cards showed something else: the women's crew spent more of its time working than the men's crews did, and less of it resting. Helmreich wrote that down carefully, and then wrote down all the reasons it might not mean what it seemed to mean: the women had been picked more strictly, everyone had told them they were proving whether women belonged in the sea and in space at all, the support divers had been keener to help them, and the newspapers had watched them more than anyone. He did not say they were better. He said they had been excellent, and that nobody could tell why from the numbers.

## What it looked like from inside

NASA also had a team write down, in plain words, what happened on the evenings of one mission. The crew's names were replaced with numbers. On the first evening, No. 1 played a Beatles cassette, then a Mozart violin concerto, and spent four minutes looking for jam. No. 5 watched the huge tarpon fish that swam past the window every night and said, "Those things go by like blimps." At 8:18 the carbon dioxide was rising, and No. 4 was told from the surface to change the chemical, and found there was no thread on the bolt; supper went cold while he fixed it, and No. 3 played the ukulele. They ate at a quarter to ten.

On another evening No. 4 asked what was for supper and got a beer, which was not allowed, and the watcher wrote that "a very few cans of beer have found their way somehow to the habitat." On another, No. 1 pointed the camera at his crewmates and said they should put on plays for the people in the van: "We'll call ourselves the Tektite II players." On the eleventh day he said, "I'm going to go up and party, No. 4. I can't stand it any more," and then, "I don't know how those guys could have stood it for 60 days on Tektite I. I would have gone bananas. NASA is running out of cassettes."

Once a hurricane came near, and one crew was brought up to the surface early, through the whole twenty hours of slow decompression. An hour or two after they arrived, the storm turned away, and they were sent straight back down, the report says, "with hardly a chance for a cigarette."

## What the cards found

Helmreich wrote his report in March 1971. The biggest surprise was how different the crews were. In the same house on the same reef, the scientists of the hardest-working crew spent 9.4 hours a day on their science, and the scientists of the least hard-working crew spent 5.1 hours. That is a difference of more than four hours every day. It was not because of the length of the mission, or storms, or breakdowns. As far as the cards could tell, each crew decided in its first few days how hard it was going to work, without anyone telling it, and then kept to that level to the end. Crews led by a scientist worked more than crews led by an engineer. Crews where the engineer helped with the science, and the scientists helped with the housework, did more science than crews where each stuck to his own job. People who talked a lot with their crewmates got more done.

The cards found something uncomfortable too. When the crews came up and were asked how well they had done, the people who were proudest of themselves had often worked the least, and the people who were unhappy with their own work had often worked the most. What people say about themselves, the report concluded, is not a good measure of what they did.

Radloff had also given every aquanaut a long questionnaire about the first nineteen years of their lives: where they had lived, how big their family was, how they had done at school, whether they had been ill, when they had first earned their own money. With forty scientists, a handful of these answers seemed to predict very well who would work hardest under the sea. The authors called it an extremely effective test, and said it only needed more checking. The companion piece "[The questionnaire](/deeper/every-six-minutes/the-questionnaire/#young)" tells that part.

## After

The last crew came up on November 6th 1970, and the house was shipped back to Philadelphia. There was never a Tektite III. The money for living under the sea had dried up after a Navy diver died at Sealab III in February 1969, the very day Tektite I began, and the government was spending less after the war in Vietnam. The house was sold for a dollar, driven across America to San Francisco as a museum, and finally cut up for scrap in 1991.

The cards went to Texas. Helmreich wrote that a great archive of real human behaviour had been collected that researchers could study for years. A few studies came. Then, in 1986, when NASA was planning a space station and asked him what the sea had taught, he wrote that after "years of neglect" people were interested again, and he admitted something about the predictions: very few of the findings about personality in confined groups had ever been checked by anyone else and found to be true.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**An instrument only measures what it was built to measure.** The cards could record, every six minutes, whether a scientist was in the water. They could not record what she found there. So the whole experiment ended up measuring how busy people were, not how good their science was. Sylvia Earle's 154 kinds of seaweed were counted by Sylvia Earle, not by the cards. When you build a way of measuring something, ask whether it measures the thing you really care about, or just the thing that is easy to count.

**Two experiments in one place get in each other's way.** The scientists said the psychologists' rules and tests took up to a fifth of their waking time. The psychologists said the other parts of the project kept trying to make the house into a pretend spaceship, which would spoil their experiment. Both were right. If you run two experiments on the same people at once, decide beforehand whose experiment gives way when they clash.

**People being watched will find the blind spot.** The crew got used to the cameras in days. But they turned the microphones off fifty-four times, nearly always to talk about the people on the surface. The thing a crew most needs to say when it is shut in together was the one thing the record could not hear.

**The famous number is not always the important one.** Everybody read that four men had lived sixty days under the sea. The number that mattered was the four hours a day between the hardest-working crew and the laziest, set in the first few days by nobody in particular. That number came with a simple lesson, which was to tell a crew what you expect of it. But the programme ended before anyone could use it.

**A pattern found in forty people is a guess, not a fact.** The questionnaire seemed to predict who would work hardest. But with forty people and fifty questions, you will always find some questions that seem to fit. The honest thing the scientists did was call it preliminary. The honest thing Helmreich did, fifteen years later, was admit that almost none of it had been checked.

So the lesson is simple. Before you watch people to learn something, decide what you really want to know, and make sure your instrument can see it. Then, when you find a pattern, check it again on new people before you believe it.
