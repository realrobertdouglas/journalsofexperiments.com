## The disease

Scurvy is what happens to a body that gets no vitamin C for a long time. Your gums swell and bleed. Dark spots come out on your legs. You feel tired and weak, old wounds open up again, and in the end you die. Fresh fruit and vegetables have vitamin C in them. Salt beef and ship's biscuit do not, and in the 1700s that was nearly all a sailor had to eat once his ship had been at sea for a few weeks. Nobody knew about vitamins then. All anyone knew was that about six weeks into a long voyage, men began to fall ill.

The worst case anyone had heard of was Commodore George Anson's voyage round the world, from 1740 to 1744. By June 1741 the disease had killed more than 200 men on his flagship, the *Centurion*, and there were only six sailors in each watch still able to work. When the next war ended, in 1763, a magazine printed the Navy's own count of the cost: nearly 185,000 sailors and marines had served; about 1,500 had been killed in battle or by accident; about 134,000 had died of disease or gone missing.

## The ship and the surgeon

The *Salisbury* was a warship of fifty guns with a crew of 300 men. She was a new ship, and her captain was George Edgcumbe, a young officer who was also a Member of Parliament. Her job was to patrol the English Channel and the Bay of Biscay, off the coast of France, stopping merchant ships and chasing French privateers: privately owned warships that were allowed to attack enemy trade.

Her surgeon was James Lind. He was thirty years old, from Edinburgh, and he had been in the Navy since he was twenty-two. A ship's surgeon in those days was not a doctor with a university degree. He was a craftsman who had learned by apprenticeship to set bones, pull teeth, treat wounds and hand out medicines, and he looked after everyone on board.

The captain kept a logbook, and the Navy kept a muster book that listed every man on the ship and said whether he was present, dead, run away or sick. Both books still exist. The log for the spring of 1747 lists the beef, bread, pork, raisins, rice, butter, cheese and beer that came aboard at Portsmouth. It does not mention any oranges or lemons.

## The twelve men

The ship sailed at the end of March 1747. By the middle of May, after seven or eight weeks at sea, thirty or forty of her men had scurvy. On May 20th 1747 Lind picked twelve of them who were about equally ill. He put them together in one part of the ship, the fore-hold, and gave them all the same food: sweet gruel in the morning, mutton broth or puddings at midday, and barley, rice, raisins and a little wine in the evening.

Then he split them into six pairs, and gave each pair a different remedy. All six were things that other surgeons already believed in.

- Two men got a quart of cider a day.
- Two got drops of elixir of vitriol, which is watered-down sulphuric acid, three times a day.
- Two got spoonfuls of vinegar three times a day.
- Two, the sickest, drank half a pint of sea water a day.
- Two took a paste of garlic, mustard seed, horseradish and other strong things, three times a day.
- Two were each given two oranges and one lemon every day.

The fruit lasted six days, and then it ran out. By then, Lind wrote, one of the two men who had eaten it was fit for duty. The other was so much better that he was made the nurse for the rest of the sick. The cider pair were a little better after two weeks. The men on vinegar, sea water and the paste were no better at all. Lind summed it up in one sentence: "the result of all my experiments was, that oranges and lemons were the most effectual remedies for this distemper at sea."

The *Salisbury* came back to Plymouth in the middle of June. Lind left the ship and never went to sea again.

There is one strange thing. The muster book, where the Navy counted its sick, shows one or two men sick that spring, and usually none at all. Thirty or forty men had scurvy, but the official list said nobody was ill. That was how the Navy kept its lists. A captain did not like to have sick men on his ship, so the list said he had none.

## The book

Lind went home to Edinburgh, became a doctor, and spent six years writing a book. *A Treatise of the Scurvy* came out in 1753. It is 456 pages long. The experiment takes up five of them.

The book says oranges and lemons are the best cure. But it says a great many other things too, with just as much confidence and no experiment behind them. Lind said cider was the best drink against scurvy, even though it had only come second in the fore-hold. He said every sailor should bring a bag of onions. And because fresh fruit went bad and was hard to find in many ports, he gave a recipe for keeping it.

The recipe was this. Squeeze the oranges or lemons, let the juice settle, and pour it into a china bowl. Stand the bowl in a pan of water over a fire and keep the water nearly boiling for many hours, until the juice has boiled down into a thick syrup. Bottle it. Lind said it would stay good for years. He called it the rob, and he said the Navy could have it cheaply.

Here is the problem. Heat destroys vitamin C. In 1975 a scientist named R. E. Hughes made the rob exactly as Lind described and measured it. Half the vitamin C was gone by the time the syrup was made, and after a month on a shelf nearly all of it was gone. The fresh fruit had cured two men in six days. The syrup Lind made from it, and asked the Navy to carry, did almost nothing.

## The hospital

In 1758 Lind was made the chief physician of the Navy's new hospital at Haslar, near Portsmouth. He called it "an immense pile of building," and said it would be the largest hospital in Europe when it was finished. It had a thousand beds when he arrived and two thousand when he left, twenty-five years later, and the fleet filled them. In 1759, Lind wrote, "the scurvy raged."

So Lind saw more scurvy than any doctor alive. In 1772 he brought out a new edition of his book with a long postscript from the hospital. In it he said he had given lemon juice to "many hundred patients" and that it gave the quickest relief in the worst cases.

And yet in the same book he wrote that no cure for scurvy would ever be found from a theory, and that "more enlarged experience must ever evince the fallacy of all positive assertions in the healing art." In plain words: the more you see, the less sure you can be that anything works. He had come to believe that the main cause of scurvy at sea was being shut up in a ship for too long. He had cured hundreds of men with lemon juice and still did not believe that lemon juice was the answer.

## Captain Cook

From 1772 to 1775 Captain James Cook sailed the *Resolution* round the world for three years and eighteen days, and lost only four men: one to a long illness that was not scurvy, two drowned, and one killed in a fall. In 1776 he wrote a letter to the Royal Society, the country's leading scientific body, explaining how.

He gave most of the credit to malt. He had carried a great deal of it, made it into a sweet drink called wort, and given it to any man who looked like getting scurvy. "This is without doubt one of the best antiscorbutic sea-medicines yet found out," he wrote. He also praised pickled cabbage, rest, dry clothes, clean hammocks and fresh greens wherever the ship touched land. He had carried Lind's rob too. His whole comment on it was that "the surgeon found it useful in several cases." A few months later he wrote that he had "no great opinion" of it.

Cook was right about the rob. It did not work, because the boiling had ruined it. But the Navy took a different lesson from his voyage: that malt and cleanliness were the answer. Malt has no vitamin C in it. What had really kept Cook's men alive was that he put into port for fresh food whenever he could.

## Dr Blane's numbers

In 1780 a young Scottish doctor named Gilbert Blane went out to the West Indies as physician to Admiral Rodney's fleet. He did something simple: he counted. The fleet had about 12,000 men. In one year about 1,500 of them had died of disease, one man in every seven, and 350 more were too ill to serve.

He also noticed two ships. In May 1780 the *Alcide* and the *Invincible* stopped at the island of Montserrat for two days and got nothing there except a few bushels of limes. Twelve days later, 60 men on the *Invincible* and 114 men on the *Alcide* had gone back to duty from the sick list. On every other ship in the fleet, which had no limes, the scurvy got worse.

In 1781 Blane wrote to the Admiralty, the men who ran the Navy. He listed the causes of the fleet's sickness. The second on his list was the lack of lemon or lime juice, which he said was "infallible in preventing and curing scurvy." In his book of 1785 he called lemons and oranges "real specifics in that disease," and gave Lind the credit for proving it first. He also wrote that he knew "for certain" that the rob was nothing like as good as the fresh fruit.

## The Suffolk

In 1793 an admiral called Sir Alan Gardner, who was about to take a squadron to India, asked Blane how to keep his men well. Blane told him to ask for a large supply of lemon juice, and he got it. The squadron was cancelled, but the juice went to a smaller force under Admiral Peter Rainier, and there was enough for the whole crew of his flagship, the *Suffolk*.

The *Suffolk* sailed from England on April 2nd 1794. Every man on board had a small measure of lemon juice in his rum each day, with two ounces of sugar. She sailed for twenty-three weeks and a day without once touching land, and anchored at Madras in India on September 11th 1794 without losing a single man. Fifteen men were on the sick list, none of them with scurvy.

The next year, 1795, the Admiralty ordered lemon juice to be supplied to the fleet at the government's expense: three-quarters of an ounce a day for every man, with sugar. Within eighteen months, when the head of the Admiralty went to Haslar hospital to see a case of scurvy, there was not one to show him. Blane wrote many years later that scurvy had been "totally rooted out" and had stayed that way.

James Lind did not live to see it. He died at Gosport on July 13th 1794, while the *Suffolk* was still at sea.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**An answer only helps if someone is asking the question it answers.** Lind's experiment asked a clear question: of these six remedies, which one works? It gave a clear answer in six days. But Lind, and everyone else, were really asking a different question: what causes scurvy? The experiment could not answer that, so nobody treated its answer as the answer.

**If you change the thing that worked, you have to test it again.** The oranges worked. Then Lind boiled them into a syrup so they would keep, and told the Navy to carry the syrup. Nobody tested the syrup. When Captain Cook finally tried it, it did nothing. The boiling had destroyed the very thing that made the fruit work. The thing you tested and the thing you hand out are not the same thing until you have tested the second one too.

**Big numbers move big organisations.** Twelve men in a fore-hold did not change the Navy. One man in seven dying did. A hundred and fourteen men back on duty after twelve days of limes did. A ship at sea for twenty-three weeks with nobody ill did. Blane showed the Admiralty its own numbers, in the form it was used to reading. Lind never asked for anything at that size.

**A list that is kept to look good cannot warn anyone.** The Navy's sick list said the *Salisbury* had almost nobody ill while thirty or forty men had scurvy. A Navy that counted its sick that way could not see how big the problem was, and so had no reason to buy lemons.

So the lesson is simple. Getting the right answer is only half the work. The other half is making sure that someone is asking the right question, and that the thing you give people is still the thing you tested.
