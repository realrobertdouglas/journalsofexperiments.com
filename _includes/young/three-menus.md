## The men under the stadium

In the autumn of 1944 the Second World War had been going on for five years. Most young American men were in the army or the navy. But about twelve thousand were conscientious objectors: men who had told the government that they would not fight, because they believed that killing was wrong. The law let them do other work instead, without pay, in camps run by three churches that had always refused to fight, the Mennonites, the Quakers and the Brethren. The objectors built trails, fought forest fires and looked after patients in mental hospitals. Some of them volunteered to be tested on by doctors. The newspapers called them human guinea pigs.

At the University of Minnesota, in the city of Minneapolis, a scientist named Ancel Keys ran a laboratory that measured what hard conditions do to the human body. It was in an odd place: under the seats of the university's football stadium. Keys had already invented the K ration, a packet meal that American soldiers carried in their pockets. In 1944 the news from Europe said that millions of people in the countries the Germans had occupied were starving. When the war ended, the armies would have to feed them, and nobody knew the best way to do it. Nobody even knew exactly what six months of hunger did to a healthy person.

Keys and his team decided to find out the only way they could: by starving healthy men on purpose, measuring everything, and then feeding them back in different ways to see which worked best. In May 1944 a leaflet went out to the objectors' camps. On the front it asked: "Will You Starve That They Be Better Fed?" Hundreds of men wrote back. Thirty-six were chosen. They were between twenty and thirty-three years old, all unmarried, and all had been to college. Among them were a law student named Max Kampelman, a Mennonite named Lester Glick, a Quaker named Marshall Sutton, and men named Sam Legg, Harold Blickenstaff and Jasper Garner. They were paid five dollars a month.

## The plan

The men arrived on November 19th 1944. Glick wrote in his diary that night that they lived in the basement of the football stadium, in a room with no windows and forty-six beds in two rows. For the first twelve weeks they ate normally, about 3,500 calories a day. A calorie is a measure of how much energy a food gives you, and a grown man needs a few thousand a day. While they ate, the laboratory measured them: their weight, their hearts by X-ray, their blood, their strength, how fast they could react, how well they could balance, and their minds, with tests and long questionnaires. Each man had to walk twenty-two miles a week, work fifteen hours a week in the laboratory, keep a diary, and go to classes about how to run relief work in Europe, which is what most of them hoped to do afterwards.

Then, on February 12th 1945, the hunger began. "Frankly, I'm scared!" Glick wrote the night before. For twenty-four weeks the men ate two meals a day, from three menus that were served over and over: brown bread, potatoes, turnips, cabbage, a little meat and milk. It was about 1,570 calories a day, less than half of what they had been eating, and it was meant to be like the food people were living on in the famine areas of Europe. The goal was for every man to lose about a quarter of his body weight. Every man was weighed, and every Friday the laboratory pinned up a list saying how much bread and potato each man would get the next week to keep him on his curve. The men dreaded the list.

After the hunger would come twelve weeks of feeding, with the men split into four groups that got different amounts of extra food, and some of them extra protein, and some extra vitamins, to find out which mattered. That was the question the relief workers wanted answered.

## What hunger did to the body

The body changed just as the scientists expected. Within three months the men's hearts had slowed from about fifty-five beats a minute to about thirty-five, and their hearts shrank. Their body temperature fell. They were cold all the time, even in July, when they walked downtown in sweaters and asked for extra blankets. They became weak. Garner got stuck in the revolving door of a department store because he could not push it, and had to wait for someone to come along. He could not pull open the big doors of the university library. Legg and his partner walked in the gutter, looking for a driveway, because the kerb was a step. Their hair fell out. Their ankles and faces swelled up with water, which happens in famines. They tripped on cracks in the pavement.

## What hunger did to the mind

This was the part nobody had expected. The two men who ran the classes, Harold Guetzkow and Paul Bowman, wrote afterwards that they had never thought losing a quarter of your weight could change a person so much. "But we were mistaken."

The men thought about food all the time. Almost all of them licked their plates clean, even in front of guests. They made strange mixtures of their food to make it seem like more, and some took two hours to eat a meal. They collected cookbooks. One man stayed up until five in the morning reading them. One man, Carlyle Frederick, had nearly a hundred by the end. They drank up to fifteen cups of coffee a day until the laboratory limited them to nine. They chewed gum, until one man was chewing forty packets a day and the laboratory limited everyone to two. Glick and a friend went to a restaurant, bought nothing but black coffee, and watched a woman leave half her pork chop; the friend followed her out and lectured her about world hunger until she ran away. One day Glick took the lead out of a pencil and chewed the wood. "It tasted all right."

Everything else drained away. The men stopped laughing. They stopped writing letters. They stopped being interested in girls. They stopped going to their classes, and the whole programme that was meant to train them for Europe quietly fell apart. They became grumpy and bored with people and wanted to be left alone. Most of all, they stopped caring about the world they had volunteered to help. When the news came that Japan had surrendered and the whole war was over, the men were eating. Garner remembered: "The food was the important thing. We didn't care whether the war was over or not as long as we got our food."

Two men broke the rules early. One ate ice-cream sundaes and milkshakes, then stole and ate raw turnips, and then fell to pieces so badly that he was taken to hospital. From April 16th 1945 no man was allowed out of the stadium without a buddy, another man of the experiment, to keep him away from food. Two more failed later: they did not lose weight even when their food was cut, which meant they were eating in secret. So four of the thirty-six did not finish properly, and thirty-two did. What kept the thirty-two going was not that they were less hungry. It was that they could not bear to let the others down. Blickenstaff said that walking past a bakery "was like walking by a bank. It might be nice to have what's in there, but it's out of the question."

## The war ended first

On May 8th 1945 Germany surrendered. The men were only halfway through their hunger. In Holland and in the German camps that the armies had just freed, people had to be fed right away, before the experiment could tell anyone how. The relief doctors guessed. They thought that starving people would not be able to eat ordinary food, so they had tubes and drips and special liquids made ready. When the moment came, they found that nearly all the starving people could eat ordinary food straight away, in small amounts, and that powdered milk and water worked as well as anything in a bottle. One of the British doctors who planned the relief, Sir Jack Drummond, wrote later that it had been "frightening to realise how little any of us knew about severe starvation."

Keys did what he could to get his results out quickly. He sent a short report to the relief agencies in October 1945, before the experiment was even over, and told a meeting in Chicago that a starved man needs about four thousand calories a day for months, and that extra vitamins and protein are no use without them. In January 1946 Guetzkow and Bowman published a little book for relief workers called *Men and Hunger*, about what hunger does to people's minds and how to treat them. It cost a dollar. But the big report with all the numbers took five more years. One of the men, Earl Heckman, said long afterwards: "the experiment was a little late."

## Sam Legg

The hunger ended on July 28th 1945. That morning Legg stepped on the treadmill for the last test of the last day, and lasted ten seconds. He cried for a long time. He felt he had let everyone down.

Then the feeding began, and it was worse. The men had dreamed of this day for six months. "FOOD! FOOD! FOOD!" Glick wrote. But the lowest group was given only a little more than before, and after six weeks some of them had gained almost nothing. "Now I go away from meals hungry three times a day instead of two," one man said. Legg was in the second-lowest group. In the first week he let his car fall off its jack onto his left hand, crushing a finger, and admitted to one of the staff that he had done it on purpose, to be sent home. It was not enough to get him out. Ten days later he was at a friend's house for dinner with Marshall Sutton. The fire was getting low. Legg went out to the woodpile with a hatchet and brought it down on his left hand, cutting off three fingers. He spent five days in hospital, and then came back and finished the experiment. Fifty years later a radio reporter asked him about it. "I admit to being crazy mixed up at the time," he said. "I am not ready to say I did it on purpose. I am not ready to say I didn't."

## The end, and the book

At noon on October 20th 1945 the rules ended, and at five o'clock the laboratory served a farewell dinner. Several men had to leave the table with stomach pains, staring at food they could not eat. The next week the men who stayed ate more than five thousand calories a day each. Some made themselves ill. One had to have his stomach pumped. Glick was told the day before the end that he had tuberculosis, a lung disease, went home to his mother, gained seventy-nine pounds in six months, and got better. By the spring of 1946 most of the men were nearly themselves again, though they said it took anything from two months to two years to feel strong.

The experiment had answered the relief workers' question. The men who got the most food recovered fastest. The extra protein made no difference, and neither did the vitamins. What a starved man needed was plenty of ordinary food, about four thousand calories a day, for months. The full report came out in April 1950: two thick books, 1,385 pages. On the first page the scientists wrote: "We regret, of course, that this work was not published when the hunger of the world was more acute than now." And they wrote something else. They had hoped that the relief workers in Europe would have kept records of how they fed people, so the two sets of facts could be put together. They looked hard. They found almost none.

## After

The men went off to do the work they had starved to prepare for. Seven of them crossed the Atlantic on ships carrying cattle to Europe. Blickenstaff spent two years in Poland. Sutton fed refugees in Gaza. Several became ministers. Kampelman became a lawyer and then a diplomat who negotiated with the Soviet Union about nuclear weapons. In 1991 the men held a reunion, and sixteen of the thirty-six came. In 2003 and 2004 a researcher found eighteen of them still alive, all in their eighties, and every one said he would do it again. "Now remember," one of them said, "I'd do it again if I were 24 again!"

Keys went on to study why so many American men were dying of heart attacks, and became famous for it. He died in 2004, aged a hundred. His big report found its readers in the end, but they were not the relief workers of 1945. Today the chapters about what hunger does to the mind are read by doctors who treat people with eating disorders, illnesses in which people starve themselves, to show them that the obsession with food, the cold, the sadness and the strange habits at the table are what a starved body does to any mind. Nobody has ever repeated the experiment, and the rules about experiments on people, written after the war, mean nobody is likely to.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**An experiment made for a certain moment has to be finished before the moment passes.** The experiment needed a year, and the war ended in the middle of it. The scientists did the right thing: they sent out a short report and a little book while the full one was still being written. If you are testing something so that people can use it on a certain day, decide at the start what you will tell them if the day comes early.

**People who act without the facts find them out anyway, and usually do not write them down.** The relief doctors in Holland guessed wrong, found out the truth by feeding people, and learned the same lesson the experiment learned. But almost nobody kept records. Five years later the only careful account of what the relief had needed to know was the book that came too late.

**The most useful result can be one nobody planned.** The experiment was built to compare feeding diets. The diaries and questionnaires were only there to describe the men. But what they described, hunger's effect on the mind, is the part that is still read eighty years later, and the first book the laboratory published was about exactly that.

**The hardest thing to control was the men themselves.** Thirty-six men were starved in the middle of a city full of food, with nothing to stop them eating but their own word and a buddy. Four broke. The scientists could only tell by watching the scales. If an experiment depends on people being honest, expect the experiment itself to make honesty harder.

So the lesson is simple. Measure everything, even the things you are not studying, because they may turn out to be the answer that lasts. And if your work is meant to help people at a certain moment, plan from the start what you will do if the moment comes before you are ready.
