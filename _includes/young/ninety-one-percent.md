## The steel rooms

Krasnoyarsk is a city on the Yenisei river in the middle of Siberia, in Russia. In the 1960s the scientists there built something nobody had built before: a set of sealed rooms in which people could live on air, water and food made by plants, with nothing coming in from outside except electricity.

It was called Bios-3. It was in the basement of a research institute, and it was made of welded stainless steel, like the inside of a submarine. It was 14 metres long, 9 metres wide and 2.5 metres high, and it was divided into four rooms of the same size. One room was for the crew: three tiny bedrooms, a kitchen, a toilet, a control room, and a workshop where wheat was threshed and ground into flour. The other three rooms were farms. Each had twenty big lamps hanging from the ceiling, each lamp as bright as sunlight, and under the lamps grew wheat, carrots, beetroot, radishes, onions, dill, cucumbers and a plant called chufa, a sedge grown for the oil in its roots. The lamps never went off, day or night. The whole thing used as much electricity as a few hundred homes, and it came from a dam on the river thirty kilometres away.

The doors had rubber seals, like a fridge door, and that rubber was the only rubber in the building. Every other seal was silicone. There were round windows, like portholes, so the people inside could see out and the scientists outside could see in.

## Who built it

The idea came from a rocket engineer. Sergei Korolev was the man who put the first satellite and the first man into space, and he paid for the first work in Krasnoyarsk, starting in 1961. He knew that people who went to the Moon or Mars could not carry enough air, water and food for years, so they would have to make their own, the way the Earth does: with plants.

The scientists who did the work were a physicist called Leonid Kirensky, who ran the institute; another physicist, Ivan Terskov; and a doctor and biologist called Josef Gitelson, who led the work for most of his life and died in 2022, aged ninety-four. Boris Kovrov designed the building. Genry Lisovsky was the farmer: he chose the crops and bred a short wheat that gave more grain and less straw. Yuri Okladnikov was the doctor who looked after the crews.

Before Bios-3 they built two smaller systems. In the first, from 1964, one person sat in a small sealed cabin while tanks of green algae, tiny plants that live in water, took in the air he breathed out and gave back oxygen. It worked. But algae are not food: people who eat a lot of them get ill. So in the second system they added a small room of wheat and vegetables. That worked too. Then they built the big one. You can read about the small ones in "[Chlorella](/deeper/ninety-one-percent/chlorella/#young)".

## What they wanted to find out

The question was simple to ask. Can a few square metres of plants under lamps keep two or three people alive, with fresh air, clean water and food, for months on end, with the door shut?

To answer it they had to weigh everything. Every day someone wrote down what went in through the small airlock and what came out: the dried meat passed in once a month, the samples of air and water and blood passed out to be tested, the dried waste taken away. If the plants were really making the air and the water, the books would show it.

Three things were decided before the door closed. The crew would get meat from outside, because, as the scientists put it, Siberians must have their meat. Human waste would be dried and stored, not fed back to the plants. And the straw, which is more than half of a wheat plant and cannot be eaten, would be burned to make carbon dioxide for the plants, and the ash taken out. So the system was never meant to be completely closed. It was meant to be nearly closed, and to say exactly where it was not.

## The first winter

On December 24th 1972, three people walked in and the door was sealed behind them: two men and a woman, an agronomist (a farming scientist), an engineer and a doctor. The engineer was Nikolai Bugreev. His bedroom was five square metres, with a bed, a table and a shelf for his clothes. He could talk to the outside by telephone, and his family came on weekends and looked at him through the porthole.

They stayed 180 days, until June 22nd 1973. At first two farm rooms were running and the plants gave them all their oxygen and about a fifth of their food. Then one farm room was shut off and the algae tanks were used for the air instead, and the agronomist swapped places with an algae specialist for a while. There were always three people inside.

The air they breathed was strange. Outside, carbon dioxide is about four parts in ten thousand of the air. Inside Bios-3 it was about one part in a hundred, thirty times more, because that suited the wheat. The crew breathed a little faster. Otherwise the doctors could find nothing wrong with them.

## The second winter

In the winter of 1976 to 1977, three men went in for four months. This time the aim was to see how much food the plants could make. Each man was given up to about thirty square metres of crops. The crew's washing water and even their urine went into the wheat's feed, and the wheat took more than half of its nitrogen from it.

This was the run where things drifted. With so many plants the oxygen rose too high and straw piled up, so the crew burned the straw inside to put carbon dioxide back into the air, and the chart of the air for that winter shows sharp spikes every time they did. Tiny amounts of metals began to build up in the plants and the water: lead and tin from the solder on a steel screen, nickel and chromium from new steel, other metals from a filter and from the air cleaner. Bacteria on the plants multiplied when a crop was sickly. The scientists changed the rules: the crew stopped washing their clothes and used clean ones from store, they wore masks among the plants, and a new air cleaner was fitted that heated the air to 650 degrees and burned off every smell. One of the three men left before the end. The records do not say why.

## The third winter

In November 1983 two men went in, and they came out in April 1984, after five months. This was the best-measured run of all. The three farm rooms held 63 square metres of crops. The wheat gave about half a kilogram of grain a day. For 103 days all of the crew's urine went into the wheat's feed, and the wheat did not mind. The metals did not build up this time, because the solder and the steel had been changed.

At the end the scientists added it all up. Without recycling, one person needs about 13 kilograms of stuff a day from outside: nearly 11 kilograms of water, more than a kilogram of oxygen, nearly a kilogram of dry food, and a little soap and salt. Inside Bios-3, one person needed only 598 grams a day from outside: some dried meat, the chemicals to feed the plants, soap, salt, and a pinch of filter material. Everything else, all the water and all the oxygen and most of the food, the plants had made. The scientists called it 91 percent closure.

Nobody has lived sealed inside Bios-3 since April 1984.

## What the numbers mean

Here is the catch. When people say Bios-3 was "91 percent closed" or "95 percent closed", most of that number is water, because water is most of what a person uses. The plants made all the water and all the oxygen, which is wonderful, but it would have been nearly as high a number if they had grown no food at all.

Food is the hard part. In the third winter the plants gave the crew about three quarters of their dry food and about 70 percent of their calories. Nearly all of the starch came from the wheat, but only a third of the fat and two thirds of the protein, because the fat and the protein were in the meat, and the meat came from outside. And the minerals that plants need, like potassium and copper, were only about 20 percent recycled, because what went out in the dried waste and the ash never came back.

So the same two men in the same steel box can be described as 95 percent closed, or 91, or 77, or 70, or 20. All of those numbers are in the scientists' own papers, and all of them are true. They are just percentages of different things. You can see every one of them laid out in "[The ledger](/deeper/ninety-one-percent/the-ledger/#young)".

## Afterwards

In 1989 a group from NASA, the American space agency, came to see Bios-3, and one of them, Frank Salisbury, later wrote the first full account in English with Gitelson. But by then the Soviet Union was falling apart, and so was the money. In the 1990s the institute could not afford to run the building; one newspaper found it without electricity, water or heating. In 2005, with the seals about to be renewed and new work in prospect, Bugreev was heard to say, "A renaissance! We waited twenty years for this!" Some European money did come, and the rooms were used for smaller experiments, but no crew has gone in. In 2021 the institute's director proposed a Bios-4 that would close the food cycle too. The steel rooms are still there.

There is a famous American experiment that is often told as the opposite of this one. In 1991, eight people were sealed into a huge glass building in Arizona called Biosphere 2, with a rainforest, an ocean and a farm inside, and after sixteen months the oxygen had fallen so far that it had to be pumped in from outside. The full entry "[Two Years Under Glass](/experiments/2026/10/18/two-years-under-glass.html)" tells that story. People say the Russians succeeded where the Americans failed. But the two buildings were sealed to the same standard: each leaked about a fortieth of one percent of its air a day. The difference was in what was counted, and what was promised. Krasnoyarsk wrote down in advance that the meat would come from outside and the waste would go out, and nobody called it cheating. Arizona promised a sealed world, and every exception became a scandal.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**One number can hide a lot.** "Ninety-one percent closed" sounds like one fact. It is really a sum, and most of it is water. The plants made all the air and all the water, three quarters of the food, and only a fifth of the minerals. If someone gives you one number for a complicated thing, ask what it is a percentage of. The smallest line in the sum is usually the one that matters.

**Say in advance what you are not going to do.** The Krasnoyarsk scientists decided before the door closed that meat would come in and waste would go out, and they wrote it down. So when the answer came out as 91 percent, everyone knew what the other nine percent was. The experiment in Arizona did not do that, and so even sensible things, like a machine to take carbon dioxide out of the air, looked like cheating when people found out.

**In a sealed room, the room itself is part of the experiment.** Nobody planned for the solder on a steel screen to put lead into the wheat. Nobody in Arizona planned for the concrete walls to eat the oxygen. In both places it was the weighing that found it. When you seal something up, everything inside it joins in, including the walls.

**A balance that people keep by hand only proves that people can keep it.** The crews burned straw when the oxygen rose, swapped a crew member when the plan changed, and ate whatever the plants grew because they were told to. The air stayed level because they kept it level. That is a real result, but it does not tell you what would happen if they stopped. Three people running a farm for six months proves that three people can run a farm for six months.

**A measurement nobody repeats is just a measurement.** The institute says its result has never been matched by anyone. That is true. It is also true that nobody, including the institute, has repeated it since 1984. A thing that was measured once, forty years ago, is worth knowing. It is not a law.

So the lesson is simple. When something is described as closed, or complete, or finished, ask for the ledger, not the total, and find out what was decided in advance to leave out.
