## The two men

James Glaisher was a scientist who measured the weather. He was born in London in 1809, and from 1840 he was in charge of the weather and magnetism department at the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, the place where the official time was kept. He spent his life reading thermometers and barometers, writing the numbers down, and teaching other people to do the same. A thermometer measures how warm the air is. A barometer measures how heavily the air presses down, and because the air gets thinner the higher you go, a barometer can tell you how high you are. In 1862 Glaisher was fifty-three years old. He had never been up in a balloon.

Henry Coxwell was an aeronaut: a person who flies balloons. He was born in Kent in 1819 and had trained as a dentist, but from 1848 he made his living by taking people up in balloons at fairs and pleasure gardens. By 1862 he had made more than four hundred flights. He was forty-three.

The two men were brought together by a committee of the British Association for the Advancement of Science, a club of the country's leading scientists. The committee wanted to know how the air changes as you go up. They had tried before with old balloons, and the old balloons had torn, or leaked, or would not rise. They had spent all their money.

## The question

For a hundred years scientists had believed in a simple rule: the air gets one degree colder for every 300 feet you go up. The rule came from measurements made on mountains. But a mountain is not the open sky. The rocks warm up in the sun and cool at night, and they stir the air around them. Nobody knew whether the rule was true in the free air, miles above the ground. The only way to find out was to go up there with good instruments and read them.

So the committee's question was: how does the temperature of the air, and the amount of water in it, change with height, "as high as possible"? Behind it was a second question that nobody wrote down so plainly. Could a man sit at a table of instruments five or six miles up and still read them properly?

## The balloon

Coxwell did some arithmetic and showed the committee that no balloon in England was big enough. Then he offered to build one at his own cost. He hired a schoolroom, took on every seamstress in his neighbourhood, and made a balloon of oiled cloth that stood eighty feet tall when it was full of gas. The newspapers called it the Mammoth. It was the biggest balloon ever built in England.

The gas came from the gasworks at Wolverhampton, a town chosen because it is about as far from the sea as anywhere in England. A balloon goes where the wind takes it, so the further from the coast you start, the longer you can stay up. The engineer of the gasworks, Thomas Proud, made a specially light gas for them and kept it in a tank of its own.

Glaisher brought the instruments. There was a barometer that had already been up in a balloon ten years before. There were thermometers with tiny bulbs that could follow a change in the air in ten seconds. There were instruments for measuring how damp the air was, a magnet, sealed glass tubes to collect samples of air, papers that changed colour, and a pigeon basket. All of them were fixed to a board that lay across one end of the basket, right in front of Glaisher's seat, with a watch set to Greenwich time opposite him and his notebook in the middle. Coxwell stood at the other end of the basket, with the sandbags and the ropes. The rope that opened the big valve at the top of the balloon, to let gas out and come down, was coiled up on the wooden hoop above their heads.

On the first flight, on July 17th 1862, the wind was so strong that Glaisher could not fix a single instrument before they left the ground. He screwed them in as the balloon rose. They reached five miles, came down through a cloud a mile and a half thick, and hit the ground so hard that nearly every instrument broke. On the second flight, on August 18th, everything worked, and they went up about four and a half miles and down again gently.

## September 5th 1862

The third high flight waited for days on bad weather. When it came, the gas was the lightest they had yet had, and Coxwell decided to go up fast.

They left the gasworks at three minutes past one in the afternoon. At 5,000 feet the air was 41 degrees. They passed through a cloud and came out on top of it into bright sunshine and a deep blue sky, with "a magnificent sea of cloud below". Glaisher tried to take a photograph and could not, because the balloon was rising too fast and spinning round and round. It kept spinning all the way up.

At two miles the air was at freezing point. At three miles it was 18 degrees. Coxwell was panting for breath. At four miles it was 8 degrees, and the air was so dry that the dew-point instrument could find no water in it at all. At five miles the thermometer read two degrees below zero. Glaisher threw out pigeons on the way up. One opened its wings and "dropped as a piece of paper". One "fell downwards as a stone".

Glaisher himself felt fine. He wrote afterwards that he had "experienced no difficulty in breathing". But at 22,000 feet he wrote in his notebook that he was finding it hard to read the instruments. At 26,000 feet he wrote: "Lost myself; could not see to read the instruments."

He read them anyway. He asked Coxwell to help him, but the spinning of the balloon had twisted the valve rope, and Coxwell had to climb up out of the basket into the ropes above to untangle it. Glaisher looked at the barometer one more time. It said they were 29,000 feet up, about five and a half miles, and still rising fast. It was six minutes to two.

Then his arms stopped working. He laid one on the table and found he could not lift it. The other one was the same. His head fell on one shoulder, then the other, and he fell back against the side of the basket, looking up at Coxwell in the ropes. He tried to speak and could not. Then everything went black, although he could still think clearly. He thought he was going to die unless they came down quickly. Then he fell asleep.

Up in the ropes, Coxwell's hands had frozen. He had taken his gloves off to handle the sandbags, and the hoop was as cold as the air. He got the valve rope free, but he could not climb down with his hands, so he laid his arms on the hoop and dropped. He looked at Glaisher, saw his legs stretched out and his arms hanging down and his face "serene and placid", and understood. He felt himself starting to faint too. He could not pull the rope with his hands. So he took the rope in his teeth and bent his head down two or three times, until the balloon turned and began to fall.

Glaisher came round to the sound of words. "Temperature." "Observation." Then, "Do try—now do." He could hear but not see or move. Then he saw the instruments dimly, then Coxwell, and sat up "as though waking from sleep". "I have been insensible," he said. "You have," said Coxwell, "and I, too, very nearly." Glaisher picked up his pencil and started writing numbers down again. Coxwell showed him his hands, which had turned black, and Glaisher poured brandy over them. The water in the jar that fed one of the thermometers had frozen into a solid lump.

By Glaisher's reckoning he had been unconscious for about seven minutes, and no reading had been taken for thirteen. The balloon fell three miles in nine minutes. They landed in a big grass field at Cold Weston in Shropshire a little before three o'clock, with two pigeons still in the basket, one of them dead. There was no cart or carriage to be had, so they walked seven or eight miles to a railway station. Glaisher sent a telegram, and the next morning he wrote his report before and during breakfast. It was in the newspapers the same day.

## How high?

Nobody had read the barometer at the top. Glaisher had fainted, and the instruments were on his side of the basket, where Coxwell could not reach them. So Glaisher worked the height out afterwards, in three ways.

First, he knew they had been rising at 1,000 feet a minute when he fainted and falling at 2,000 feet a minute when he woke, and he supposed the balloon had kept going up at that speed until the valve opened. That gave 36,000 or 37,000 feet. Second, a special thermometer that remembers the lowest temperature it has seen read twelve degrees below zero, and using the rule for how fast the air cools with height, that also gave 37,000 feet. Third, Coxwell remembered glancing at the barometer as he came down out of the ropes and seeing its needle in line with a rope, which would mean seven inches, which also meant about 37,000 feet. Three answers that agreed. "Fully seven miles," Glaisher wrote.

The newspapers loved it. One said six miles, one said seven, and *The Times* said the two men had been "nearer by some miles to the moon and stars than all the race of men before them". A comic magazine printed a schoolboy's poem about it. Seven miles went into the record books, and stayed there for a generation.

But look at the three answers again. The first assumes the balloon kept climbing as fast as ever for minutes after the last reading, which balloons do not do; they slow down near the top and drift along level. The second uses the very rule about temperature that the whole experiment was meant to test. The third is the memory of a man with frozen hands who was about to faint. None of the three was a reading. In Glaisher's book, the chart of the flight shows the line of the balloon's path drawn solid up to 29,000 feet and dotted above it. The dots are the guess.

In 1898 an American weather scientist called Hazen read Glaisher's report carefully and added it up again. The gas could not have lifted the balloon above 30,000 feet. A man pulling a rope with his teeth could not have let out much gas. And the cold thermometer, worked out properly, gave a rise of about 3,000 feet above the last reading, not 8,000. His answer: not more than about 29,500 feet. Most people who have looked at it since agree with him. We also now know, from the training that pilots get, that a person with no oxygen can stay useful for only a minute or two at 30,000 feet and for less than a minute at 35,000. Glaisher was reading instruments for several minutes above 26,000 feet, was unconscious for seven, and lived, and Coxwell never fainted at all. That is what happens near 30,000 feet. It is not what happens at 37,000.

## What the flights found

The flight was a frightening story, but it was also an experiment, and the experiment worked. Glaisher had a temperature and a measure of dampness for every thousand feet up to 29,000. Put together with his other flights, twenty-eight in all between 1862 and 1866, they showed that the old rule was wrong. Near the ground on a sunny afternoon the air cooled a degree for every 140 feet, more than twice as fast as the rule said. Higher up it cooled more and more slowly, until near 30,000 feet it took a thousand feet to lose a degree. Clouds changed everything. The dampness came in layers, with damp air and dry air above and below each other, and above 20,000 feet the air was almost perfectly dry. And on a night flight in 1865 Glaisher found that after dark the air can get warmer as you go up, not colder.

After September 5th the committee asked Glaisher not to go higher than four or five miles again. He agreed. Everything above five miles, he said, could be worked out from what was measured below it, and Coxwell thought the balloon was no longer safe for such heights. Glaisher went on flying, with other balloons and other aeronauts, until 1866. He died in 1903, aged ninety-three. Coxwell died in 1900, aged eighty.

One more thing. Of the six pigeons, one flew back to Wolverhampton two days later. Another, thrown out at four miles on the way down, flew in a circle and landed on top of the balloon. Thirty-five years later a Swedish explorer called Andrée was crossing the Arctic ice in a balloon, and one of his own pigeons came back and flew round the car. He wrote in his diary: "Perhaps it has done the same as Glaisher's pigeon?" Nobody who read the diary knew what he meant. He meant the one that sat on the balloon.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Every instrument has a limit, including the person reading it.** The committee tested all its instruments before the flight. They tested the barometer, they timed the thermometers, they tried the aneroid under an air pump. They did not test Glaisher. Nobody asked how high a man could go before he stopped being able to read. The answer turned out to be about 29,000 feet, and the experiment found it the hard way.

**A number you work out afterwards is not the same as a number you read.** Seven miles was worked out three ways, and all three were guesses that leaned on the same thirteen minutes with no readings. Glaisher was honest about it: he drew the dotted line on his chart. But the newspapers printed the number, not the dots, and the number lasted for a generation. When you see a famous number, ask whether anyone measured it.

**Safety has to work when the person is not working.** The rope that could bring the balloon down was coiled up high in the rigging, so that it would not be pulled by accident. That was sensible. But it meant that the one thing that could save them needed a climb into the cold with bare hands. A doctor suggested afterwards that the valve should open by itself if the aeronaut let go of the rope. Nobody built one. The committee's answer was better and simpler: do not go so high again.

**Write it down, even while it is going wrong.** Glaisher's description of losing the use of his arms, then his sight, then his mind, is still read by doctors today. He could write it because he had spent thirty years learning to observe, and he kept observing when the thing he was observing was himself. And Coxwell's first fear, when his friend lay still, was that he "would never take any more readings". Both of them knew what the experiment was for.

So the lesson is simple. Know how far your instruments can be trusted, and that includes you. Measure what you can, say clearly which numbers are guesses, and when you find the edge of what you can measure, stop there, and do not let the guess become the thing everyone remembers.
