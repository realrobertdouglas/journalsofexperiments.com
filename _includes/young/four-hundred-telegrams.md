## The airship

An airship is a huge bag of gas with engines and a cabin hung underneath it. The gas, hydrogen, is lighter than air, so the bag floats, and the engines push it along. In 1926 an Italian engineer called Umberto Nobile built one and flew it with the Norwegian explorer Roald Amundsen from Spitsbergen, the group of islands north of Norway, right over the North Pole to Alaska. Nobody had ever crossed the top of the world before. Afterwards Nobile and Amundsen quarrelled about whose expedition it had been, and they stopped speaking to each other.

Nobile decided to go back with an airship of his own. He called her the *Italia*. She had three engines and could fly at 115 kilometres an hour. Sixteen men flew in her on her last flight: Nobile, three naval officers to find the way, a Swedish weather scientist called Finn Malmgren, a physicist from Milan and one from Prague, an engineer, six mechanics and riggers, a radio operator called Giuseppe Biagi, and a journalist. There was also Nobile's little dog, Titina.

## What they wanted to find out

Nobile wanted to answer two questions. The first was whether an airship could live in the Arctic: fly out from a base at Kings Bay in Spitsbergen for two or three days at a time over sea that nobody had ever seen, and even lower men on to the ice to do science. He planned three flights: one east to some islands north of Siberia that nobody had mapped, one to the North Pole, where he meant to let Malmgren down in a basket to measure how deep the sea was, and one along the north coast of Greenland.

The second question was about weather. An airship cannot fight a storm. So the whole plan depended on reading weather reports, which came by radio from a weather station at Tromsø in Norway, and choosing three or four calm days in a row. "You get us three or four days of high pressure in the polar regions," Malmgren told the journalists, "and then we'll vamoose." He had spent three winters frozen into the ice on one of Amundsen's ships.

Nobile had also thought about what would happen if the weather report was wrong. There was only one airship hangar in the Arctic, at Kings Bay, so every flight had to come back to it. If the wind turned against them on the way home, he wrote, it "would have to be faced and overcome". So he loaded enough petrol for about eighty-five hours of flying. That was his answer to a wrong forecast: fly into the wind and push through.

## The flights

The *Italia* reached Kings Bay on May 6th 1928. Her hangar had no roof, and when wet snow fell it piled up on top of her, ten tons of it, and had to be swept off with brooms, which tore the fabric in places. The first flight, on May 11th, ran into fog and came back after eight hours. The second, from May 15th to 18th, lasted nearly three days and flew east almost to the islands she was looking for before turning back. Nobile was pleased with her. "Practically speaking," he told a journalist, "nothing can happen to it."

## The last flight

The ship left for the North Pole at half past four in the morning on May 23rd 1928. Malmgren did not like the weather. "The weather is not of the best," he said, "but one hasn't the face to keep walking the decks of the ship when one has already donned one's flying-kit." They flew to Greenland and then north with a strong wind behind them, and twenty minutes after midnight on May 24th the officers with the sextant called out that they were at the Pole.

They could not land, because of fog. They flew down under it, and Nobile dropped the Italian flag, the flag of Milan and a cross that the Pope had blessed on to the ice. The wind was blowing hard from the south. Going home meant flying straight into it. Nobile wanted to go the other way instead, with the wind, to Canada. Malmgren said no. "This wind will not last long," he said. "Once we are on the way back it will drop after a few hours." So at twenty past two in the morning they turned for home.

The wind did not drop. The ship was making less than fifty kilometres an hour over the ice. Malmgren said, "Let us get out of this zone quickly," and Nobile started the third engine and drove the ship as hard as she would go. Ice built up on the ship, and lumps flew off the propellers and tore holes in the fabric. Nobile had not slept for two days. There was no second pilot to take over from him.

On the morning of May 25th, after more than fifty hours in the air, they thought they were nearly home. In fact the wind had pushed them far to the east. The elevator, the control that points the ship up or down, jammed with ice, and the ship floated up into sunshine above the fog, then came down under it again to about 300 metres. At half past ten the chief rigger, Natale Cecioni, said two words: "Siamo pesanti." We are heavy.

The ship was sinking, tail first, towards the ice. Nobile ordered full power on all the engines, then, when that did nothing, engines off and the ballast chain dropped. The rope holding the chain would not come untied. "The pack was there, a few metres beneath us," Nobile wrote, "terribly broken up. The blocks of ice grew larger, came nearer and nearer. An instant later we struck." It was 10.33 in the morning. From "we are heavy" to the ice had taken two or three minutes.

The cabin smashed on the ice and tore off the ship. Ten men fell out of it. One of them, a mechanic called Vincenzo Pomella, was killed. The airship, suddenly lighter without its cabin, rose into the fog and drifted away to the east with six men still inside. Twenty minutes later some of the men on the ice saw a column of smoke in that direction. Nobody ever saw the ship or the six men again.

## On the ice

Nine men were alive on the ice. Nobile had a broken leg and arm, Cecioni a broken leg, Malmgren a hurt arm. But scattered around them they found a tent, food for nearly two months if they were careful, a pistol, instruments, and a small spare radio that Biagi got working that same evening. Malmgren shot a polar bear with the pistol. Biagi painted the tent with red dye so that it could be seen from the air.

Every two hours Biagi sent the same message: "SOS Italia, Nobile. On the ice near Foyn Island, north-east Spitsbergen," with their position, and a request to answer on a wavelength of 32 metres. The little radio could not send very far, but it could hear very well. The men in the tent could hear their own base ship, the *Città di Milano*, at Kings Bay. They heard her sending the journalists' stories and the sailors' private telegrams home. Every two hours they heard her send a message to them, telling them to have confidence, and giving a guess at where they were that was more than a hundred kilometres wrong. They heard her, and she did not hear them.

## The radio nobody listened to

On the evening of May 29th a radio operator on the ship called Pedretti did hear them. He heard the word "Italia" and the end of the message, and grabbed his chief by the arm. The chief said it could not be the *Italia*. It must be the Italian station in Somaliland, in Africa, which called Rome every day. Nobody checked. The next day three of the men on the ice, Malmgren and two naval officers, Mariano and Zappi, set off to walk to land to get help, because they had given up hope that anyone would ever hear the radio.

Why did the ship not listen? Nobile asked her captain afterwards. The captain explained the logic. The airship had a spare radio. If she had crashed, Biagi could have sent an SOS. No SOS had come. So Biagi must be dead. And if Biagi was dead, there was no point in listening, except for a few minutes each hour as a formality. The rest of the time the radio room was busy sending telegrams. On one day alone there were four hundred of them. "And so," Nobile wrote, "for twelve days running, nobody on the *Città di Milano* listened to our call for help."

The person who did hear was a young Russian radio amateur called Nikolai Schmidt, in a village more than two thousand kilometres away, on a receiver he had built himself. On June 3rd he picked up pieces of the message and told Moscow. Moscow told Rome. Rome told the ship to stop sending telegrams and listen. On June 8th, fourteen days after the crash, the ship finally heard the whole message and answered it. Nobile said afterwards that Schmidt was the person to thank "if half the members of the expedition are alive today". The companion piece "[The twelve days](/deeper/four-hundred-telegrams/the-twelve-days/#young)" tells the radio story in full.

## The rescue

Now everybody knew where the tent was, but nobody could get to it. Norwegian pilots flew over it and circled for an hour without seeing it; the men on the ice lit fires and waved, and the planes flew away. "There were millions of tents to be seen on the ice," one of the pilots said. On June 20th an Italian pilot, Umberto Maddalena, found the tent by letting Biagi steer him by radio, "slightly to the right, slightly to the left, now you are right above us", and dropped food, boots and a new battery.

Two days earlier, Roald Amundsen, Nobile's old enemy, had taken off from Tromsø in a French flying boat with five other men to join the search. Asked whether he would help the man he no longer spoke to, he had answered, "Instantly." The flying boat was never seen again, and Amundsen and the five men with him were never found.

On the night of June 23rd a Swedish pilot, Einar Lundborg, landed a small aeroplane on skis beside the tent. He could take only one man. He asked for the General. Nobile said no, Cecioni with his broken leg should go first, then the others, and himself last. Lundborg said his orders were to take the General first, and that the plane might not lift the heavy Cecioni. Cecioni cried, and Nobile went, with Titina. Lundborg flew back alone a few hours later for the next man, and his plane tipped over on landing. Now he was stuck on the ice too, for thirteen days, until another Swedish plane fetched him. Nobile was blamed for the rest of his life for leaving first. The companion piece "[Midsummer Eve](/deeper/four-hundred-telegrams/midsummer-eve/#young)" tells Lundborg's story in his own words.

In the end it was a ship that saved the rest. The Soviet ice-breaker *Krassin* smashed her way through the ice for three weeks, losing a propeller blade on the way. On July 12th she found Mariano and Zappi on a tiny floe. Malmgren was not with them. He had broken down about two weeks after leaving the tent, with frozen feet, and had asked the other two to leave him and go on. "Go on, go on!" Zappi said he called after them. "You can save the others by leaving me to die." His body was never found. That same evening the *Krassin* reached the red tent and took off the last five men, forty-nine days after the crash. In the whole story seventeen people died: eight from the airship, six with Amundsen, and three Italian airmen whose flying boat crashed on the way home from the search.

## Afterwards

The Italian government set up a committee to find out what had gone wrong. It sat all winter and reported in March 1929. It said the crash was the fault of "an erroneous manoeuvre" by the commander, that Nobile had "limited technical qualities as a pilot and a negative capacity for command", and that leaving the ice first was against military honour. Nobile left the air force. He went to work in Russia and then America, and only in 1945, when the government that had judged him was gone, was he given back his rank.

The committee's own expert had written something different at the end of his report. The real cause, he said, lay in how the crew was put together: there was no trained second pilot on board, and nobody whose job it was to take over when the commander was worn out. Ninety years later a doctor who studies tiredness in pilots looked at the whole record and agreed with him.

One more thing happened. In the summer of 1930 sealers were still looking along the shores of the Arctic for the six men who had drifted away in the airship. On July 9th 1930 a Norwegian captain landed on White Island, east of Spitsbergen, to look for them, walked over a stretch of bare ground, found a rusty tin box and a wooden peg, and left when the ice came in. Four weeks later other sealers landed on the same beach and found the camp of Andrée, the Swedish balloonist who had vanished in 1897, with his diaries. The captain had been standing on it. That story is told in "[Sixty-Five Hours](/experiments/2026/10/19/sixty-five-hours/)". The six men of the *Italia* left nothing, and the last trace of them is a column of smoke.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A forecast is not a promise.** Malmgren was the best weather scientist in the Arctic, and he said the wind would drop in a few hours. It did not. The real mistake was not the forecast. It was that when the forecast turned out wrong, nobody stopped to think again. They just pushed harder, with a third engine, through fog and ice, for a whole day and night. Nobile had planned the whole flight around the idea that a head wind could be beaten with enough petrol. A plan like that has no point where you say, "This is not working, let us do something else."

**Tired people make bad decisions, so someone else has to be ready to decide.** Nobile had not slept for more than two days when the ship went heavy. There was no second pilot and no deputy to take over. The committee blamed Nobile for his manoeuvre in the last three minutes. Its own expert said the real fault was that an airship had been sent to the Pole with one exhausted man in charge and nobody to relieve him.

**Listening is a job, not a feeling.** The men on the ice had a radio, and it worked from the first evening. A boy in Russia heard it from two thousand kilometres away. The expedition's own ship, much closer, did not hear it for twelve days, because the people on the ship had decided, by what they called logic, that the radio operator must be dead, so there was no point in listening. While they reasoned, they sent four hundred telegrams in a day. Listening means someone sitting at the receiver, at set times, on the right wavelength, whose only job is to listen, and a rule that says a faint signal heard once must be checked. The ship had none of that, and because it had none, three men walked off into the ice and one of them died.

**Blaming one man is the easy answer.** When something goes badly wrong, people want a name. The committee gave them Nobile's. But the things that really went wrong, the roofless hangar, the patched fabric, the one tired pilot, the ship that did not listen, were about how the whole expedition was built.

So the lesson is simple. When the world tells you your plan is not working, stop and decide again instead of pushing harder. And when someone is calling for help, make sure that listening is somebody's actual job, every hour, on the right wavelength, and that a faint signal gets checked before anyone decides it cannot be real.
