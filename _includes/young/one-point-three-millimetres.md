## The telescope

The Hubble Space Telescope is a telescope that flies around the Earth, about 600 kilometres up, above the air. Air blurs starlight, which is why stars twinkle. A telescope above the air can see things much more sharply than one on the ground. Hubble was built to see ten times more sharply than the best telescope on Earth.

A telescope like Hubble has two mirrors. The big one, the primary mirror, is 2.4 metres across, about as wide as a car is long, and weighs about 830 kilograms. It is made of glass, polished to a very exact curve and coated with a thin layer of aluminium. Light from a star bounces off the big mirror onto a small one, and from there into the cameras.

NASA, the American space agency, hired a company called Perkin-Elmer, in Connecticut, to make the mirrors. The contract was signed in 1977. Because the big mirror was the hardest part of the whole telescope, NASA also had a second company, Kodak, make a spare mirror by completely different methods, so that if one went wrong the other could fly.

## Why a mirror is hard to test

The shape Hubble's big mirror needed is called a hyperboloid: a bowl a little deeper at the edge than a plain curve. The glass had to be right everywhere to about one hundredth of a wavelength of light, which is a few millionths of a millimetre. No ruler can measure that. Opticians measure it with light.

Here is how. A small set of mirrors and lenses, called a null corrector, is built to project a pattern of light shaped exactly like the mirror that is wanted. The null corrector is a template, like a dressmaker's pattern. When the mirror is right, light sent through the null corrector and bounced off the mirror comes back as straight, evenly spaced stripes. When the mirror is wrong, the stripes bend. The polishers polish, look at the stripes, polish again, and stop when the stripes are straight.

There is a catch. If the template is wrong, a wrong mirror will give straight stripes too. The stripes only tell you that the mirror matches the template. They cannot tell you whether the template is right.

## The instrument at the top of the tower

Perkin-Elmer built a null corrector of its own design: two small round mirrors and one lens, held in a frame at the top of a tall test tower, looking down at the big mirror below. The company said it would prove the null corrector was right by measuring each of its parts, and the distances between them, very carefully. The distances had to be right to ten thousandths of a millimetre. They were set with metal rods of exact lengths, and a beam of light was bounced off the end of each rod to find exactly where it was. To keep the beam on the centre of the rod's rounded end, each rod wore a little cap with a hole in the middle. The top of the cap was painted black, so that it would not reflect light. Only the rod, seen through the hole, was supposed to shine the beam back.

Perkin-Elmer did not plan to test the mirror any other way. NASA agreed. Kodak, when it bid for the job, had offered to test the finished telescope as a whole, mirrors together, for about 10 million dollars. Perkin-Elmer had not offered that, and NASA did not ask for it. The whole telescope would end up costing about 1.5 billion dollars.

## The cap with the chipped paint

Some time in 1979 or 1980, in a locked room at Perkin-Elmer, the lens of the null corrector was put in its place. Nobody wrote down exactly what happened, so what follows is what the investigators later worked out, and tested, as the most likely story.

Somewhere on top of one rod's cap, a patch of the black paint had chipped away. When the operator shone the beam at the rod, the light bounced back from the shiny patch on the cap instead of from the rod's end. So the operator thought the end of the rod was in a different place from where it really was. The lens was moved up to where the beam said the rod ended. It would not go far enough: the adjusting screws ran out. So spacers, small washers, were put under the lens to push it the rest of the way. The lens ended up 1.3 millimetres too far from the mirror below it. That is about the thickness of a coin.

A difference of 1.3 millimetres inside the null corrector made the template a little too flat near its edge. The big mirror was then polished until it matched the template. So the big mirror came out a little too flat near its edge too: by about two thousandths of a millimetre, a tiny amount, but ten times more than the telescope could stand.

## Two warnings

The error should have been caught, because two other instruments in the tower saw it.

The first was a small gadget called an inverse null corrector, built to imitate a perfect mirror. It was swung in under the null corrector at the start and end of every test. If the null corrector was right, the stripes through the two together would be straight. In February 1981 they were curved, and they stayed curved every time. The engineers decided the gadget itself must be faulty.

The second was an older, simpler null corrector made of two lenses, which had been used for the rough shaping. In May 1981 it was used once more, to measure the curve at the centre of the mirror. Its photograph showed curved stripes across the whole mirror, the exact error that would later be found in space. Nobody read them, because the engineers believed the old corrector was not accurate enough to trust.

The engineers had a word for the difference. The new null corrector was "certified". The other two were "uncertified". When investigators asked, years later, what certified meant, nobody could show them a definition or a document. The two instruments that were set aside turned out to be accurate enough to show the error several times over.

Some people did ask for more testing. In May 1981 a review team at the company wrote that another kind of test should be done, "to uncover some gross error such as an incorrect null corrector." The company's former chief scientist said afterwards that he had asked for a week to run an independent test, and was told no. The programme was far over budget and behind schedule, and in danger of being cancelled. The final check of all the test data was dropped to save money. In February 1982 the mirror was tested one last time against the null corrector, matched it beautifully, and was accepted. Kodak's spare mirror, which was the right shape, went into a crate.

## The launch and the blur

Hubble was launched on the Space Shuttle Discovery on April 24th 1990, thirteen years after the contract was signed. The first pictures were sharper than any from the ground, but not nearly as sharp as promised. The engineers moved the small mirror back and forth to focus. The pictures did not get better. Each star had a blurry halo around it. The telescope's own sensors measured the shape of the incoming light and gave the answer: the big mirror had the wrong shape. Instead of putting 70 percent of a star's light into a tiny circle, as the specification required, the telescope was putting only 15 percent there.

On June 27th 1990 NASA told the world. The news was everywhere. A comedian joked that the telescope was fine; the universe was just blurry. Senators held hearings. One of them, Albert Gore, said that when you build a ship you test it in the water, and when you build a car you drive it, and that a telescope should have had a dress rehearsal before it flew. The company's president, asked why his firm had not offered a final test, said he had only found out that day that Kodak had offered one.

## Finding the 1.3 millimetres

NASA set up a board of six experts to find out what had gone wrong, led by Lew Allen, the head of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory. Everything at the factory was impounded: the papers, the photographs and the instruments. The null corrector had been left at the top of the tower, untouched, since 1982. The board ordered that nothing be moved, because it was the only record of the mirror's real shape.

On July 22nd 1990 the engineers swung the inverse null corrector under it again and took a photograph of the stripes. They curved. Then someone found, in an employee's notebook, a photograph taken the same way on June 22nd 1982. It was almost exactly the same. The error had been photographed eight years before and filed away.

The board worked through the possibilities. Was the lens in backwards? No. Was it made of the wrong glass? No. Was it the wrong distance from the mirror below? The rod that had set the distance no longer fitted, so the engineers measured it another way, with a beam of light and a flat mirror, and then with a new rod. The lens was 1.3 millimetres too far away. Worked through the sums, 1.3 millimetres gave exactly the blur the telescope was showing in space. A few weeks later someone looked closely at the rod's cap and saw the chipped paint. "We would now judge," Allen said, "that it was an error to place that much confidence in a single instrument."

The board's report came out in November 1990. It said the company had relied on one instrument alone, had never checked it, and had ignored two instruments that disagreed with it. It said NASA's inspectors had not known enough about optics to spot the problem, and had been kept out of the room where the null corrector was built. One of the board members told reporters: "The culture has to be encouraged where you don't shoot the messenger."

## The fix

A mirror 600 kilometres up cannot be re-polished. But because the error was so simple and so exactly known, it could be cancelled out. Tiny mirrors, about the size of a coin, were ground with the opposite error and put in front of the telescope's cameras, like a pair of glasses. A new camera was built with the correction inside it. For the other instruments, a box the size of a large refrigerator, called COSTAR, was built to hold the little mirrors. It cost about 50 million dollars.

In December 1993 seven astronauts flew to Hubble on the Space Shuttle Endeavour. They caught the telescope with the shuttle's robot arm and went outside on five days in a row. On December 7th two of them swapped the old camera for the new one. On December 8th two others, Kathryn Thornton and Thomas Akers, took out an instrument and slid COSTAR into its place. "I think everybody's going to breathe a sigh of relief," Akers said.

On January 13th 1994 NASA showed the first new pictures: a galaxy called M100, photographed with the old camera a few days before the repair and with the new camera after it. In the old picture only the brightest stars could be seen. In the new one, the faint ones stood out too. A senator held the two up and said the trouble with Hubble was over.

The government later settled with the companies for 25 million dollars. Kodak's spare mirror, still in its crate, went to the National Air and Space Museum in Washington in 2001. COSTAR joined it there in 2009, when the last of the old instruments was replaced. Hubble has since made more than a million and a half observations. ## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Matching the template is not the same as being right.** Every test showed that the mirror matched the null corrector. Everyone read that as proof the mirror was right. But it only proved the mirror matched the template, and nobody had ever tested the template. When you check something against a standard, you have to know the standard is good.

**"Certified" is a word, not a measurement.** The engineers trusted one instrument because they called it certified, and ignored two others because they called them uncertified. Nobody could say what the word meant. The instruments they ignored were good enough to show the error, and they did show it. A name is not a reason.

**When two instruments agree with each other and disagree with the one you trust, look again.** The two spare instruments were telling the same story from two sides. Instead of listening, the engineers found a separate excuse for each one. In the story of the Space Shuttle Challenger, "[Fifty-Three Degrees](/experiments/2026/10/20/fifty-three-degrees.html)", the warning was on the table and nobody plotted it properly. Here the warning was photographed and filed as the fault of the camera.

**A cheap check beats an expensive repair.** A week's extra test was refused. A full test for 10 million dollars was never required. The repair cost tens of millions of dollars, a shuttle flight, and three and a half years of a crippled telescope. The expensive test is the one people argue about. The cheap one is the one that gets dropped in a bad year.

**A locked door is a decision.** The null corrector was built behind a cipher lock, by a few engineers, with no paperwork, where the company's own designers and inspectors could not see. The man who designed it never once went up the tower to watch it work. If he had seen the curved stripes, the board said, he would probably have known what they meant.

So the lesson is simple. Before you trust a measurement, find out what the measuring instrument was checked against. And when another instrument disagrees with it, do not ask which instrument is better. Ask which one is wrong, and find out.
