## The company

A submersible is a small submarine that is carried out to sea by a bigger ship and lowered into the water for one dive at a time. The company that built the *Titan* was called OceanGate. Stockton Rush started it in 2009 in Everett, Washington, with a partner, Guillermo Söhnlein, who left in 2013. Rush had studied aerospace engineering. He liked to say that he had flown jets at nineteen, that he had wanted to be an astronaut, and that he had turned to the ocean because it was the last frontier left.

OceanGate's first submersibles were ordinary ones, built of steel, and they went down a few hundred metres. From 2016 the company wanted something much harder: to take five people at a time down to the wreck of the *Titanic*, which lies 3,800 metres under the North Atlantic. At that depth the water presses on every square inch of the hull with more than two tonnes of force.

## The idea

Almost everything about the *Titan* came from one decision. Deep submersibles normally have a pressure hull, the part that keeps the water out, shaped like a ball and made of steel or titanium. A ball spreads the squeeze evenly, and engineers have measured for a century how metal behaves when it is squeezed. But a ball big enough for five people is heavy and expensive to carry around. Rush wanted a tube instead, so that five people could sit in a row, and he wanted it made of carbon fibre, a light material of glued threads of carbon. Carbon fibre is very strong when you pull on it. When the deep sea squeezes it, it has a short and bad record, and no one had ever approved a hull like it for that depth.

Ships and submersibles are normally checked by an outside organisation called a classification society, which inspects the design, watches the building and tests the result. OceanGate chose not to do this. In 2019 the company published an essay on its website called "Why Isn't Titan Classed?" It said that most accidents at sea are caused by people, not machines, and that bringing in outsiders would slow down new ideas. Rush said in interviews that he had broken some rules to build the craft, and once that "safety just is pure waste."

In place of classing, OceanGate had an experiment. The idea was that the hull would warn you before it broke. Sensors glued to the carbon fibre listened for the threads cracking and measured how much the hull squeezed, and a screen in the cabin showed the readings. If the hull began to fail, the pilot would see it in time to drop the weights and float up. Rush patented the system in 2021. The people who paid $250,000 for a seat were not called passengers but "mission specialists", and they signed a form that said the craft was experimental and could cause injury or death. One of them said the word "death" appeared three times on the first page.

## The warnings

Plenty of people said this was dangerous, in writing, before anyone was hurt. In March 2018 a committee of the Marine Technology Society, the professional body for people who build underwater vehicles, drafted a letter to Rush saying that its members were unanimously worried and urging him to have the craft classed before he put people in it. Thirty-eight experts agreed to sign it. Rush was not receptive, and the letter was never formally sent.

Inside the company the warning came from David Lochridge, the director of marine operations, a submersible pilot. In January 2018 he inspected the hull as it was being built, found flaws, and wrote a report refusing to sign it off. He made two sharp points. The window in the front had been approved by its maker only for 1,300 metres, a third of the way to the *Titanic*, and the company had designed its own instead of buying a deeper one. And the listening sensors, he said, would only hear the hull failing milliseconds before it imploded. An implosion is when the water crushes a hull inward in an instant. A warning that comes milliseconds before is not a warning. It is a recording.

Lochridge was called into a meeting and fired. The company sued him, he sued back, and they settled. He also complained to the government agency for safety at work, which had eleven cases ahead of his and never got to it.

## What they built

The hull was a carbon-fibre tube 2.4 metres long and about 1.4 metres across on the inside, with walls 12.7 centimetres thick. A titanium ring was glued to each end, and a titanium dome was bolted to each ring. The front dome held the window. The whole craft was 6.7 metres long and weighed about nine and a half tonnes. Inside, five people sat on the floor with their backs against the curved wall. There were no seats, no heating, one small toilet behind a curtain, a screen for the outside camera, and a video-game controller to steer with. OceanGate's website said that NASA, Boeing and the University of Washington had helped design it. All three later said they had not.

The first hull went to sea in 2018. In April 2019, on a dive with four people aboard, an experienced submersible pilot named Karl Stanley heard what he described as the sound of something breaking. A month later a crack was found in the hull. It was retired after about fifty dives, and the company lowered the craft's depth limit to 3,000 metres because of fatigue, the way a material weakens a little every time it is loaded. Then it had a second hull made, in 2020 and 2021, and the limit went back up to 4,000 metres.

The second hull went to the *Titanic* in the summers of 2021 and 2022. By one count the craft had 118 things go wrong in those two seasons. Once it was stuck on the bottom for twenty hours with people inside. Once the ship lost track of it for five hours. Once a thruster was wired backwards.

## Dive 80

On July 15th 2022, on the eightieth dive of the programme, the crew heard a loud bang on the way up. They were told it was probably the hull shifting in its metal cradle. But the sensors heard it too. Both the listening sensors and the strain gauges recorded it, and afterwards the strain readings never went back to where they had been. On the next three dives the line on the graph that showed how much the hull squeezed as it went deeper was no longer straight. Three years later government engineers worked out that the layers of carbon fibre had most likely come apart on dive 80, and that the hull was damaged again on dive 82.

Nobody did anything. The data was recorded and filed. No one at the company read it carefully enough to see what it meant, and there was no rule that said who had to look at it or what number meant stop. The craft was stored for the winter without proper protection and brought back out for 2023. The instrument that was the whole safety case had done its job. The experiment had no procedure for believing it.

## The last trip

In 2023 the *Titan* was towed out to the wreck site on a platform behind a ship called the *Polar Prince*. A travel-magazine editor named Arnie Weissmann spent eight days aboard that May, waiting for weather that never came, and wrote about it afterwards. What he saw looked careful. There was a briefing every morning at seven with a checklist on a screen. Everyone wore helmets and steel-toed boots on deck. Rush kept a book about checklists on the lounge table, and told his crew to stop for five minutes after any complicated repair and think through everything they had just done. There was a list of things that would cancel a dive, such as wind over ten knots or a journalist aboard.

One evening on the back deck Rush told Weissmann that he had bought the hull's carbon fibre cheaply from Boeing because it was past its use-by date for aircraft. Boeing later said it had no record of any such sale. Weissmann wrote that he had thought about that conversation a great deal.

The weather finally opened on June 18th 2023. Five people climbed into the *Titan* that morning: Rush, who was piloting; Paul-Henri Nargeolet, a 77-year-old French diver who had been to the *Titanic* thirty-seven times; Hamish Harding, a British explorer who had been to the deepest point in the ocean and to the edge of space; and Shahzada Dawood, a Pakistani-British businessman, with his 19-year-old son Suleman, who had brought a Rubik's cube to solve at the wreck. It was Father's Day. Suleman's mother had given him her seat.

The craft let go of the platform at 9:18 and began to sink, which takes about two and a half hours. At 10:14 it sent a text message to the ship: "all good here." At 10:47 it sent another: "dropped two wts.", meaning it had released two weights. Six seconds later came the last position signal, from 3,346 metres. Then nothing.

The investigators later worked out that the hull failed at about 3,880 metres. The carbon tube buckled and the water crushed it in milliseconds, faster than anyone inside could have noticed. People on the ship heard a bang around then and did not know what it was. A US Navy underwater listening system recorded a sound like an implosion in the right place at the right time, but it matched no known submarine and was logged as unidentified.

The *Titan* was due back at half past four in the afternoon. The Coast Guard was told at ten past seven that evening. For four days ships and aircraft from three countries searched, while the world counted down the ninety-six hours of air the craft was said to carry. On June 22nd a remote-controlled underwater robot found the tail cone on the sea floor, 500 metres from the *Titanic*'s bow, and then the two titanium domes, lying apart.

## After

OceanGate stopped operating in July 2023. The US Coast Guard held two weeks of public hearings in September 2024, and in August 2025 it published its report, 327 pages long. It said the five deaths were preventable. It found that the company's design, testing, maintenance and inspection were all inadequate, that it had put money and schedules ahead of safety, and that Rush had kept nearly every decision in his own hands. It made seventeen recommendations. The National Transportation Safety Board followed in October 2025. It found that the hull had been built with wrinkles, holes in the glue and gaps in the fibre, that the company had never established how strong it really was, and that it had misread the one instrument that could have told it. Canada's investigators reported in June 2026 and said the same. The families sued.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A warning only works if someone has to act on it.** The hull did warn. It cracked out loud in 2019. It banged on dive 80, and the sensors recorded that something had changed. Then it gave seven more dives of warning, all written down in the data. But nobody had agreed in advance what a changed reading meant, or whose job it was to stop the dives when it appeared. So the warnings sat in a file. A sensor that nobody has to listen to is just a recorder.

**Being careful about small things does not make the big thing safe.** The helmets, the morning briefings, the five-minute pause after a repair: all of these were real, and they protected people on the deck and in the waves. But none of them had anything to say about the hull, because the hull was the thing everyone had already assumed was fine. You can look very careful and still have skipped the one question that matters.

**People who agree to a risk need to know the whole risk.** The passengers signed a form that said the craft was experimental and could kill them. But they were not told what the sensors had recorded on dive 80, or that the window was only approved for a third of the depth. Saying yes to a danger you have been told about is not the same as saying yes to one that was kept from you.

**Brave people in the next seat are not proof that it is safe.** The travel editor wanted to dive because an expert with thirty-seven dives was going. The explorer went because the company seemed to know what it was doing. Rush piloted the craft himself, which people took as proof. Everyone's confidence came from someone else's, and nobody at the bottom of the chain had a measurement.

**Safety rules are slow for a reason.** The rules Rush called a waste exist because, in the past, hulls were tested until they broke, in tanks, with nobody inside. That is how you find out how strong something really is. Rush may have been right that carbon fibre could one day work. He was wrong to find out with passengers aboard.

So the lesson is simple. When you build something new, decide before you start which reading means stop, and who has to stop. Then test it to breaking with nobody inside before you ask anyone to trust it.
