## The horse in the courtyard

In the north of Berlin, in the summer of 1904, there was a horse that could do sums. Or so everyone said. His name was Hans, and he belonged to an old man called Wilhelm von Osten, who had been a mathematics teacher and now owned the house whose courtyard the horse lived in. Von Osten was about sixty-five. He wore a black floppy hat and a long coat that was too big for him, and he had an idea that nobody else had: that an animal could be taught the way a child is taught at school.

So he taught Hans. Every day, for four years, he stood in the courtyard with a blackboard, a counting frame of beads, wooden pins to count, a little organ, a row of coloured cloths and a pocket full of bread and carrots, and he gave the horse lessons. Hans could not talk, so he answered by tapping with his right front hoof. Three taps meant three. For letters, von Osten wrote the alphabet on the board in rows, and Hans spelled a word by tapping the row and then the place in the row for each letter.

By 1904 Hans could count the people in the yard and the men wearing straw hats. He added and subtracted and multiplied. He said what day of the week any date fell on. He told the time from a watch. He spelled the names of visitors. Schoolteachers said he was about as clever as a child of thirteen. And von Osten never charged anyone a penny to see it.

In July 1904 a retired general wrote about the horse in a newspaper. A famous explorer called Schillings, who was sure it was all nonsense, went to the courtyard to find out, got answers from the horse himself, and became its biggest supporter. Then everyone came. The Prussian Minister of Education came and stayed two hours. The Emperor's adjutant came. Police had to line the street. There were songs about Hans, and postcards, and a toy. One newspaper said that Berlin, in those August weeks, would have been a city with nothing to talk about "if the 'learned' horse had not taken pity on us". Another newspaper said it was all a trick, and that the secret was in von Osten's hat.

## The thirteen

The man who had to sort it out was Carl Stumpf, a professor at the University of Berlin who ran its Psychological Institute: the place where people studied how minds work. In September 1904 he got together a commission of thirteen men to answer one question: was anyone giving the horse secret signals on purpose, the way circus trainers do? The thirteen included the director of the Berlin zoo, a circus owner called Busch who knew every trick there was, two retired officers, a vet, a teacher and Stumpf himself.

On September 11th and 12th 1904 they watched von Osten work the horse for four hours a day. They split him up between them: one man watched his head, one his eyes, one his right hand, one his left hand, and Busch watched all of him. The horse got everything right. Nobody saw a signal. Busch then had von Osten work the horse with nobody else in the yard, and still saw nothing. On the second day they tried something cleverer. Schillings gave the horse a number while von Osten was indoors, then left; von Osten came back and asked the horse to do a sum with the number, which he did not know. They did this five times. The horse mostly just tapped the number again instead of doing the sum. But Schillings had, by mistake, told the horse to "repeat this number for Mr von Osten", so they thought that might explain it.

On September 12th the thirteen signed a statement. It said there was no trick: "nothing in the way of movements or other forms of expression which might have served as a sign, could be discovered". It said that the horse was not trained like a circus horse, and that the case deserved "a serious and incisive investigation". The newspapers read that as "the horse is real". The New York Times said the commission had found the horse's feats were done "under circumstances excluding the possibility of a trick".

Stumpf himself did not expect to find any hidden signals. If there were any, he thought, they might be some kind of whisper. "I could not believe," he wrote later, "that a horse could perceive movements which escaped the sharp eyes of the circus-manager."

## The tent

In October Stumpf started again, with two helpers from his institute: Erich von Hornbostel, who wrote everything down, and a student of medicine and philosophy named Oskar Pfungst, who did the experiments. They put up a big canvas tent in the courtyard so that the crowds could not watch and the horse could not be distracted. Hans did not like it when the wind flapped the canvas. Von Osten kept interrupting. Hans also bit, more than once.

Pfungst wrote down, before he started, the test and what each result would mean. The test was simple. Ask the horse a question when the person asking does not know the answer. If the horse still gets it right, then the horse really can think for itself, and von Osten is right about everything. If the horse gets it wrong, then it is not thinking; it is getting help from somewhere, and the next job is to find out where.

He also decided to mix the two kinds of test together, one where the questioner knew the answer and one where he did not, so that nobody could say the horse had simply been tired or in a bad mood when it failed. And he decided to write down every single result, including the ones that did not fit.

From October 13th to November 29th 1904 the three men went to the courtyard about four times a week, in all weathers. Hans would only work properly for people he liked, which meant von Osten and Schillings, so Pfungst spent time making friends with him, and soon the horse would answer him as well as anyone.

## Eight percent

The first test was with cards. A card with a number on it was held up so that the horse could see it but nobody else could, and the horse was asked to tap the number. With von Osten asking, the card said 8 and the horse tapped 14. Then the card was turned so that von Osten could see it too, and the horse tapped 8. The next card said 4; the horse tapped 8. Von Osten looked; the horse tapped 4. They did this 91 times altogether. When the questioner knew the number, the horse was right 98 times out of 100. When the questioner did not know, the horse was right 8 times out of 100, which is about what you would get by guessing.

Everything else went the same way. When the questioner knew where a word was hanging, Hans found it every time; when he did not, Hans never did. Von Osten whispered one number in the horse's ear and Pfungst whispered another, and they asked Hans to add them: 3 right out of 31. When they both knew the answer: 29 out of 31. The music, the calendar, the clock: all gone. "Hans can neither read, count nor make calculations," Pfungst wrote. "Finally, he has not a trace of musical ability."

So the horse was getting help. From which sense? Von Osten believed that Hans had amazing hearing and could even hear the questioner thinking. Pfungst found that he could get right answers without asking the question at all, out loud or in his head. So it was not hearing. Then they put blinders on the horse, big ones, fifteen centimetres square, so that Hans could not see the questioner standing beside him. In 102 tests, when the questioner was definitely hidden, the horse got 6 percent right, which was two answers. When the questioner could be seen, 89 percent. Hans hated the blinders. He twisted and strained to see the man beside him. When von Osten stood on the other side of the tent canvas, every test failed. When the questioner stood in the feed room with the door open wide enough to be seen, every test worked. And in the evening, as the light went, the horse got worse.

Now Pfungst knew that Hans was watching the questioner. He still had to find what Hans was watching for. He found it by staring at von Osten, which was hard because the old man never kept still, and then at Schillings, who was easier. And there it was. When the questioner asked a question, he leaned forward a tiny bit, to watch the horse's foot. That was when Hans started tapping. When the horse reached the right number, the questioner's head gave a tiny jerk upwards, because he was relieved. That was when Hans stopped. Nobody meant to do it. Nobody knew they were doing it. The jerk was so small that Pfungst later measured it in his laboratory at about one millimetre, and in von Osten it was no more than a fifth of a millimetre, a fifth of the thickness of a coin. But a horse's eye is very good at seeing things move, and Hans had been watching that head every day for four years.

Then Pfungst proved it. He stood up straight beside the horse and asked a question, and nothing happened, no matter what he said. He leaned forward a little without saying anything, and Hans started tapping. He told the horse to tap 13, then kept leaning until he had counted 20, and Hans tapped 20. He leaned further forward, and Hans tapped faster. He raised his head and Hans stopped. He raised just his eyebrows, and Hans stopped. He asked Hans where his head was, and looked down, and the horse bowed to the ground; he asked where his legs were, and looked up, and the horse looked at the sky. He had two men with stopwatches time it: the questioner's head moved first, and the horse's foot followed, about a third of a second later. The horse could do all of its tricks, and Pfungst could make it do any of them, just by moving his head.

## What it felt like

Pfungst also wrote down what it was like to be the questioner. The question did not matter at all. What mattered was holding the number in your mind and knowing the moment the horse reached it. He asked Hans how many corners a hexagon has, and thought of 6, and the horse tapped 6; then he thought of 2, and the horse tapped 2; then 27, and the horse tapped 27. "The animal always followed the ideas which were in the questioner's mind," he wrote, "and never his words." You had to want the answer, hard, like giving an order. In the first days it gave him headaches. And he found his own head jerk last of all, after he had seen it in von Osten.

Then he played the horse himself. In the laboratory, twenty-five people, including children of five and six, stood beside him and thought of a number while he tapped with his hand. None of them knew what he was looking for. Twenty-three of them made the little jerk of the head when he reached their number, and did not know they had.

Von Osten saw all this and still saw a thinking horse. When the horse got things wrong, he always had a reason: it was being stubborn, or it was bored, or it was tired, or it did not respect a questioner who did not know the answer. Pfungst said that every one of those things was looked for in the horse when it should have been looked for in the man. When the blinders first failed, von Osten flew into a rage at the horse, and for a moment admitted that he had been wrong; the next day he believed as strongly as ever. When he found out what Stumpf had concluded, he wrote a letter forbidding any more experiments.

## What happened next

Stumpf published his report on December 9th 1904. It said that the horse failed whenever nobody present knew the answer, so it could not count, read or do sums; that it failed when it could not see the questioner, so it needed something to look at; and that the signals it looked at were given without anyone meaning to give them. It said that nobody had the right to call an old man a liar when the facts could be explained another way. And it ended with a warning: anyone who tried the same experiment with a dog or an ape should now know about "one source of error which hitherto has not been noticed."

The New York Times printed the news the next day under the headline "Horse's trainer exonerated". It also said, which tells you something, that if the explanation was true, the horse was just as amazing as before.

Von Osten went on showing Hans in the courtyard for years. People came who had been told exactly what to look for, and said they could not see it. In 1907 Pfungst's book came out. Von Osten was sent a copy. He gave it away without even cutting the pages open. A jeweller called Karl Krall, who was sure the horse really could think, did thousands more tests with it and said that Hans worked just as well with a blinder on and in the dark. Von Osten died on June 29th 1909, poor and bitter. His last words to Krall were a curse on his horse, which he blamed for ruining his life. A few days later Hans was walked to the station and sent by train to Krall, who kept him with two Arab horses he was teaching to do sums.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**The person asking the question has to not know the answer.** This is the big one. The thirteen watched the trainer as hard as they could and saw nothing, because the signal was far too small to see. Pfungst did not watch harder. He took the answer out of the questioner's head, and the horse's score fell from 98 percent to 8. Today, whenever scientists test something, they try to make sure that the person who records the result does not know what result is expected. That rule comes, more than from anywhere else, from this horse.

**Sometimes the thing you are testing is testing you.** Everyone thought the horse was the one being examined and the man was just asking questions. It was the other way round. The horse was a very good detector of one thing: what the man beside it expected. That is why it "corrected" people who had the date wrong, and why it tapped whatever number Pfungst happened to think of. A result that follows what the experimenter is thinking, instead of the facts, is a warning sign.

**Beware of a theory that has an excuse for every failure.** Stubborn, bored, tired, dark: von Osten could explain away every wrong answer, and so no wrong answer could ever change his mind. Pfungst wrote down beforehand what a failure would mean. That is why his test could actually fail.

**Looking is not proof. Making it happen is.** Pfungst did not stop at seeing the head jerk. He did it on purpose and got every one of the horse's tricks whenever he wanted, and he stood still and got nothing. The thirteen had only looked.

**Being right does not always change minds.** Von Osten never believed it. Krall did thousands more tests and decided the horse really could think. The newspapers said the explanation was as strange as the trick. What lasted was not the horse but the twenty-three people in the laboratory, moving their heads a millimetre without knowing it. Hans was one horse. The people asking the questions were everybody.

So the lesson is simple. When you test something, make sure the person doing the testing does not know the answer, because if they do, the answer can leak out of them in ways that nobody, not even they, can see.
