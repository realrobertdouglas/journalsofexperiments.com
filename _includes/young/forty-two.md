## An island nobody lives on

St. Matthew Island is in the middle of the Bering Sea, between Alaska and Russia. It is about 32 miles long and three miles wide, with no trees, no harbour and no airstrip. The nearest village is more than 200 miles away. In 1916 a naturalist who spent six days there called it "barren, treeless, uninhabited and surrounded by dangerous and poorly charted waters."

One thing grew there very well: lichen. Lichen is a slow, grey, crunchy plant that reindeer eat in winter, when the grass is under the snow. It grows only a few millimetres a year. On St. Matthew, with nothing to eat it, the lichen had built up over hundreds of years into a mat three or four inches deep across the valleys at the southern end of the island. No reindeer had ever seen it.

## The radio station

In 1943, during the Second World War, the United States Coast Guard built a small radio station on the island. It was a loran station: it sent out radio signals that ships and aeroplanes could use to work out where they were in the fog. The station was six metal huts on a bluff above the sea, with one officer and eighteen men. The winter was terrible. Snowdrifts buried the huts up to 25 feet deep, and the blizzards were so bad that men got lost walking between huts fifty feet apart, so ropes and tunnels were put in to guide them. In September 1943 five men took a small boat along the shore on a calm day and were never seen again.

The men worried about food. So in June 1944 the station asked permission to buy thirty reindeer from a herd on Nunivak Island, 170 miles away, as an emergency food supply. On August 20th 1944 a Coast Guard ship called the *Clover* landed twenty-four female reindeer and five males on the beach. They were young animals, about a year old. The men were not allowed to shoot them.

They never did. The war ended, and on September 13th 1946 the last of the men sailed away. They left the huts, the oil drums and the reindeer. For the next eleven years, no one lived on the island at all.

## Nobody was watching

Think about what the reindeer had. An island full of food, with nothing to stop them eating it. No wolves, no bears, no hunters. No herders to move them on when they had eaten one valley bare. And no way to leave: the next island, Hall Island, is three miles away across a strait full of fast currents, and no reindeer ever crossed it.

Reindeer have fawns every spring. Twenty-nine became fifty, then a hundred, then hundreds. By 1954 two scientists who camped on the island to study voles reported that the reindeer were everywhere and were already trampling the lichen.

The government did a sum. The island belonged to the Fish and Wildlife Service, as a bird reserve, but the reindeer did not belong to anyone. In July 1957 the Service announced that it was sending a biologist to count them. Its announcement said something remarkable, before anyone had counted anything: that the herd had grown so fast, with no one to hunt it, that it "will eventually destroy the range and itself too."

## The biologist

The biologist was David Klein. He was thirty years old. He had driven to Alaska from the east coast in an old Model A Ford in 1947, fallen in love with the place, and come back to study wildlife. In July 1957 a Coast Guard ship dropped him and a student assistant named Jim Whisenhant on the beach with a tent, a radio in a wooden box, a fishing net, and rolls of fencing wire.

They walked the whole island, from ridge to ridge, with binoculars, counting. It took four days. They counted 1,226 reindeer and worked out that fog had hidden about a tenth of them, so the real number was about 1,350. The animals were enormous: fat, with huge antlers, much bigger than reindeer in the herds they had come from. One bull weighed 404 pounds.

But the lichen was going. On the flats where the herd spent the winter, the mat that should have been three or four inches deep was less than an inch, and most of it was broken into pieces that the wind was blowing away. Klein built three fenced plots to keep the reindeer out, so that he could compare what grew inside the fence with what grew outside. Then the ship took him to Hall Island for a few hours, so that he could see the lichen there, untouched. Hall Island became the control: the thing you compare against.

Klein wrote a report that winter. He said the herd could not be managed. It was too far away to harvest, and too hard to remove. So he recommended that the government do nothing, and watch, because "the expected crash decline" would teach biologists something about how animal populations work. He warned that the crash might be fast, and asked for the herd to be counted every year.

Nobody could get there for six years.

## Six thousand

In July 1963 a Coast Guard icebreaker with two helicopters took Klein back. "There were reindeer all over the place," he remembered later. But the island was buried in fog, and from the ridges he could see nothing. On the last day, with the fog lifted, he radioed the captain and asked to borrow the helicopters. He got both. He flew one half of the island and a colleague flew the other, counting, and that night on the ship they added it up.

"We counted 6,000 of them," Klein said. "They were really hammering the lichens."

Six thousand reindeer on 128 square miles is 47 to every square mile. The animals were much smaller than in 1957: the adult females weighed about 40 percent less, down to the size of ordinary reindeer. There were fewer fawns for every mother. And the lichen was gone. On the winter range it was less than a centimetre deep. On Hall Island, across the strait, it was still eight to twelve centimetres.

Klein left on the ship. He would not see the island again for three years.

## The winter

Nobody was there for the winter of 1963 to 1964. That is the strange thing about this experiment: the most important moment happened while nobody was watching.

Here is what the weather stations on the nearest islands recorded. On Nunivak, the snow on the ground reached 42 inches in February, 47 in March and 48 in April, the deepest in twenty years of records, and February was the coldest February ever recorded there. On St. Paul, 140 inches of snow fell that season. In February and March 1964 there were almost constant windstorms, one of them as strong as a hurricane, with a week of wind chills below minus forty.

On a range with no lichen left, the snow buried everything else. The reindeer starved.

On May 18th 1964 a Navy aeroplane flew along the island with Klein on board, hoping to photograph the herd. He saw no reindeer at all. He decided the plane had been going too fast and too high to see them. "We were not aware, of course, that a die-off had already taken place," he wrote later.

## Forty-two

The first people to find out were sailors from a Coast Guard ship who went ashore in August 1965 to hunt. They found bleached white skeletons scattered across the tundra, and one small group of about forty living reindeer at the southern end of the island, and no others.

Klein got there in June 1966 with two colleagues, a botanist and a German scientist named Detlef Eisfeld. The bones were everywhere. Moss had started to grow on them. Klein studied 241 skeletons. He broke open the leg bones to look at the marrow: when an animal starves, it uses up the fat in its marrow, and every bone he opened was hollow. The skeletons lay in groups of ten or more, as if a band had lain down together in a blizzard and not got up. Inside four of the female skeletons were the tiny bones of unborn fawns, and from their size Klein could tell that their mothers had died in late February or March.

It took a long time to find any living reindeer. At last, in a valley near Big Lake, Klein and Eisfeld found them grazing in a meadow. They counted forty-two. All but one were female. The one male had tiny antlers and could not breed. There were no fawns and no yearlings. The reindeer were in good condition now, with the whole island to themselves, but they could never have young again.

Then the ship that was supposed to collect them was called away to search for a crashed aircraft, and said it might be a month before it came back. The three men were running out of food. They were rescued, in the end, by a young pilot who landed his small floatplane on their lake because he had heard there were reindeer to see, and who radioed for a bigger plane to come for them. On the way out, Klein asked the pilot to fly over Big Lake. There were the thirty-two reindeer that were left, where they had been.

## What happened after

Klein published everything in 1968. The graph of the herd, rising from 29 to 6,000 and dropping to 42, became famous. It is in textbooks as the picture of what happens to a population when nothing stops it growing.

The last reindeer, a lame female, is believed to have died in 1982. She was at least twenty-one years old and had never had a fawn.

The lichen did not come back. Klein returned in 1985 and measured it: the island had 6 percent of the lichen that Hall Island had. He returned again in 2005, when he was seventy-eight, and it was 12 percent. Forty-one years after the reindeer died, the island still had not recovered. Part of the reason was that the reindeer had destroyed the living lichen so completely that it had to start again from nothing. Part of the reason was new: the climate had warmed by almost two degrees since 1949, the summers were drier, and there was less fog. Lichen only grows when it is wet.

Scientists still argue about what killed the reindeer. Klein said it was the food running out, made deadly by the worst winter in the records. Three Canadian scientists looked at his numbers again in 2004 and said the herd had still been healthy and growing, and that the winter alone had killed it. Both agree the lichen was gone. Nobody can say for certain what would have happened if the winter had been ordinary, because nobody was there.

Klein went to the island for the sixth and last time in 2012, when he was eighty-five. He died in 2020, aged ninety-three. The Coast Guard station is still on its bluff: rusted oil drums, the foundations of the huts, and a white porcelain toilet standing alone on the tundra.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Nobody planned this experiment, but it had everything a good experiment needs.** A known number of animals, counted onto the island on a known day. A closed system that nothing could get into or out of. Nobody interfering. And a control: Hall Island, three miles away, where the reindeer never went. Because of Hall Island, scientists could measure exactly how much lichen the reindeer had destroyed. Without it, the story would just be a story.

**If you only measure now and then, you can miss the most important moment.** Klein asked for the reindeer to be counted every year. They were counted in 1957, 1963 and 1966. The whole crash happened in one winter in between, when nobody was there. The graph has four points on it and a line joining them. The line looks certain, but nobody saw what happened along it.

**When you look at a result, you have to be ready to believe it.** In May 1964 Klein flew over the island and saw no reindeer. He had counted 6,000 ten months before, so he decided the plane must have been too fast to see them. The answer was right there under him, and he explained it away. It is easy to blame the instrument when the result is not what you expect.

**Food that took centuries to grow is not the same as food that grows every year.** The lichen mat was hundreds of years old. Once the reindeer had eaten it, it did not grow back the next summer, or the next decade. Sixty years later it still has not. A herd that eats its savings instead of its income looks rich until the day it is not.

**Writing down what you expect turns a mess into knowledge.** The government could not manage the herd, so in 1957 it wrote down what it thought would happen, built the fenced plots, and came back when it could. Almost everything we know about St. Matthew Island exists because someone decided to count instead of pretending.

So the lesson is simple. Nature will run the experiment whether or not you are watching. If you want to learn from it, write down what you expect, measure against something you have left alone, and come back often enough to see the answer when it comes.
