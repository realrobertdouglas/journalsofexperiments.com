## A tube in the heart

On November 5th 1929 a newspaper in the German town of Bonn printed a story with the headline "With the probe in the heart." A probe is a thin tube that doctors push into the body to reach somewhere they cannot see. The story said that a young doctor called Forssmann, at a hospital in Eberswalde, a small town near Berlin, had pushed a soft rubber tube into a vein at his own elbow and kept pushing until the end of it was inside his own heart. The tube was 65 centimetres long. Then he had walked to the X-ray room with the tube still in his heart, up a flight of stairs, and had a picture taken to prove it.

The same day, a medical journal printed Forssmann's own three-page report. Within two weeks the story was in newspapers all over Germany and in America.

Here is the strange thing. The experiment proved exactly one fact: that a tube could sit in the heart of a healthy young man without killing him. Nothing was measured through the tube. For eleven years almost nobody used it. Then two doctors in New York picked it up, and in 1956 all three of them shared the Nobel Prize, the biggest prize in medicine. This story is about the gap between the photograph and the prize.

## Who he was

Werner Forssmann was born in Berlin on August 29th 1904. His father was a lawyer who was killed in the First World War, so Werner was brought up by his mother and grandmother. He studied medicine in Berlin and qualified as a doctor in 1929, when he was twenty-four. He wanted to be a physician, a doctor who treats illness with medicines, but there were no paid jobs, so he took a job as a junior surgeon at the Auguste-Viktoria-Heim, a Red Cross hospital in Eberswalde.

His boss there was a surgeon called Richard Schneider, who was a friend of the family. Schneider is the one senior doctor in this story who behaves well. The other people in the story are Peter Romeis, another young doctor at the hospital; Gerda Ditzen, the nurse who kept the key to the cupboard where the sterile instruments were locked; and, far away in Berlin, three famous professors who would soon be very angry.

## The idea

As a student, Forssmann had seen a picture in a textbook. In 1861 two French scientists, Chauveau and Marey, had pushed a long tube down a vein in the neck of a living horse until it reached the horse's heart, and used it to measure the pressure inside while the heart was beating. The horse was fine afterwards. Forssmann also knew a drawing in a book by the great French scientist Claude Bernard, which showed a dog with its chest opened and the routes a tube could take from the neck into the heart.

Forssmann's idea was simple. A man is a large animal. If a tube could go into a horse's heart without harm, why not into a man's?

What he wanted the tube for was less simple. In his report he said it was for emergencies. When a patient's heart stops, doctors in those days sometimes pushed a needle straight through the chest into the heart to inject a drug, and the needle could do terrible damage. A tube that was already lying in the heart would be a safer way in. But his daughter, who wrote about him many years later, said he really wanted the tube to study the heart from inside, to measure things, and that Schneider told him to describe it as a treatment because that would cause less of a fuss.

So there were really two questions. Could a tube reach the heart safely? That could be tested on one person, with one X-ray. And was the tube any use? That could only be tested on sick people, with instruments that the little hospital did not have.

## What he built

He needed very little. A ureteral catheter, which is a thin rubber tube that doctors normally push into the kidney, about a millimetre across and 65 centimetres long, rubbed with sterile olive oil. A local anaesthetic, which is an injection that numbs one part of the body. A scalpel, to open the vein at his elbow. The hospital's X-ray machine, which was in the basement. And a mirror, so that a man lying under the X-ray screen could see the shadow of the tube inside his own chest.

He asked Schneider to let him try it on patients. Schneider said no. He asked to try it on himself. Schneider said no again, and told him to do experiments on animals first. Forssmann did not do the animal experiments.

## The experiment

Forssmann's own report describes two attempts. In the first, his colleague Peter Romeis opened the vein in Forssmann's left elbow and pushed the tube in about 35 centimetres. Then Romeis stopped, because he thought it was too dangerous to go further. About a week later, in the summer of 1929, Forssmann did it alone.

He numbed his own elbow, cut into the vein, and pushed the whole 65 centimetres of tube in. He felt a gentle warmth as it slid along inside the vein, a warmer feeling behind his collarbone, and a tickle that made him want to cough. Then he walked out of the operating theatre, through the hospital and down to the X-ray room in the basement. Under the X-ray screen, with a nurse holding a mirror so he could see, he pushed the tube the last of the way until the tip was in the right atrium, the chamber of the heart that collects blood coming back from the veins. The tube was not long enough to go any further. The X-ray picture was taken.

That is the story he told in 1929. In 1972, when he was an old man, he told it differently in a book about his life. In that version Gerda Ditzen, the nurse with the key, had agreed to help only if she could be the first subject. She lay down on the operating table, and in a flash, he wrote, he strapped her legs and hands to the table so she could not get up. While she lay there, unable to see, he opened his own vein and pushed in the tube. Then he let her go, and they went down to the basement together. In this version Romeis burst into the X-ray room shouting "You idiot, what the hell are you doing?" and tried to pull the tube out, and Forssmann kicked him in the shins until he stopped.

Both stories were told by the same man. A historian who compared the two versions pointed out that in 1929 Forssmann also claimed to have practised on dead bodies and animals, and that there is no sign he really did.

Schneider was very angry when he found out. But he also understood that the experiment mattered, and he helped. He let Forssmann use the tube on one patient, a woman who was dying and could not be saved any other way. Drugs were sent straight into her heart through the tube. She got a little better for a while, and then she died six hours later. When her body was examined there were no blood clots in the vein or the heart, which is what the critics would have predicted.

Forssmann finished his report on September 13th 1929. Schneider used his friends in Berlin to get him a job, without pay, at the Charité, the most famous hospital in Germany, under Ferdinand Sauerbruch, the most famous surgeon in Germany. In October Forssmann started there. On November 5th the report came out.

## What people said

The newspapers loved it. The Bonn paper's story was copied in a dozen towns within a week. A paper in Hanover called it "a foolhardy experiment," and its writer said he had read it "only with horror," but admired it anyway, and compared Forssmann to Max von Pettenkofer, the Munich professor who had drunk a glass of cholera germs in 1892 to prove a point. (That story is in this journal too, under the title [*One Cubic Centimetre*](/experiments/2026/10/19/one-cubic-centimetre.html).) The same writer guessed, correctly, that the method might turn out to be less useful than the hopeful young experimenter thought.

The surgeons hated it. Sauerbruch called Forssmann in and sacked him on the spot. According to Forssmann, he said: "With tricks like that you qualify yourself for a circus, not for a decent German clinic." By January 1930 Forssmann was back in Eberswalde.

And a Berlin professor called Ernst Unger wrote to the journal to say it had all been done before. In 1912 Unger and two colleagues had pushed the same kind of tube up a vein in a friend's arm as far as the armpit, and later up a vein in the thigh into the big vein that leads to the heart. But they had never taken an X-ray, they had never been aiming at the heart, and their paper did not even mention it. Forssmann printed a short note admitting the earlier work. Years later he said the difference plainly: in 1912, "no X-ray checks were used." Nobody had looked to see where the tube was. The full list of people who got there first, from a Berlin surgeon in 1831 to a doctor in Prague in 1929, is in the companion piece [*The letter from Unger*](/deeper/sixty-five-centimetres/the-letter-from-unger/#young).

## What happened next

The first person to do something useful with the tube was not Forssmann. In Prague, a doctor called Otto Klein read the report and saw at once what it was good for. For forty years scientists had wanted to measure how much blood the heart pumps in a minute, and the way to do it needed a sample of blood from inside the right side of the heart, which nobody could get. Klein got it. He passed tubes into the hearts of eleven patients, took the blood, and measured the first heart outputs ever taken from living people: about five or six litres a minute. Then his boss told him to stop, and he did.

Forssmann tried something else: pictures. He kept dogs at his mother's flat, carried them to a Berlin hospital in a sack on his motorcycle, put tubes into their hearts and injected a dye that shows up on X-rays, trying to photograph the dye as it passed through. He tried it on himself too, about nine times in all. He got a bad taste in his mouth and a dizzy feeling, and no good pictures, because the X-ray plates could not be changed fast enough. In April 1931 he read a paper about it to the German surgeons' congress. Nobody clapped and nobody asked a question.

After that he gave up on the heart. He went back to the Charité for a year and was sacked again. In 1932 he joined the Nazi party, before it came to power, a thing he left out of his book. He married a doctor called Elsbet Engel, and became a urologist, a doctor who treats the kidneys and bladder, which is the kind of doctor who uses that thin rubber tube every day. He was an army surgeon in the Second World War, in 1945 he was a prisoner of the Americans, and after the war he was banned from working as a doctor for three years because of the party. He cut timber in the Black Forest and helped in his wife's village practice. In 1950 he got a job at a small hospital in Bad Kreuznach.

Meanwhile, in New York, two doctors called Dickinson Richards and André Cournand had spent ten years at Bellevue Hospital trying to measure the blood going through the lungs, which needs the same sample from the heart that Klein had taken. They had tried every indirect way and, as Richards said later, "failed completely." They knew about Forssmann's report. In late 1940 they passed a tube into a patient's right atrium, took the sample, and found that the tube could stay in for hours without harm. Then the United States went to war, and doctors badly needed to understand what happens to the blood of wounded soldiers. The tube was the way to measure it. By 1945 the thing Forssmann had done once, to see if it could be done, was a standard test, and soon it was being used to find holes in the hearts of children before surgeons operated. Cournand said later: "For us in 1943, the cardiac catheter was only the key in the lock." Their story is in the companion piece [*The key in the lock*](/deeper/sixty-five-centimetres/the-key-in-the-lock/#young).

## The prize

On October 18th 1956 the news came from Stockholm: the Nobel Prize for medicine would be shared by Cournand, Richards and Forssmann. He said he felt "like a village parson who just learned that he had been made bishop." He took all six of his children to Stockholm. The professor who presented the prize said that Forssmann had done his experiment "with the intrepidity of youth," that the criticism he got afterwards had been so severe "that it robbed him of any inclination to continue," and that the criticism was based on "an unsubstantiated belief in the danger of the intervention." In other words, the senior doctors had been wrong, and they had been wrong for nothing.

Forssmann never went back to heart research. He became chief surgeon of a hospital in Düsseldorf and retired in 1969. He died on June 1st 1979. The hospital in Eberswalde is now named after him, and the operating theatre and the basement X-ray room were still in use when his daughter visited. She walked from one to the other and found it was a long way.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**An experiment on yourself can answer one question: the one your own body can answer.** Can a tube sit in the heart of a healthy young man without harm? Yes. The X-ray proved it. But whether the tube was useful could only be found out on sick people, with instruments, by people who had a question to ask. Pettenkofer, in the other story, asked his own body a question it could not answer. Forssmann asked it the one question it could.

**The proof was the photograph, not the man.** Forssmann could have pulled the tube out and told everyone where it had been. Instead he walked downstairs with it in his heart and had the picture taken, because he knew that nobody would believe an unknown junior doctor and everybody would believe an X-ray. That is the whole difference between him and Unger, who had a tube in a man's big vein in 1912 and never looked to see where it went. A result that nobody records is just a story.

**A tool waits for the question it answers.** For eleven years the tube sat in a journal with no use attached. Klein found a use at once and was stopped. Forssmann tried the wrong use, pictures, before the cameras were fast enough. Richards and Cournand had spent ten years failing to get one number, and the tube was the only way to get it. "The key in the lock," Cournand called it. A key is no good without a door.

**The story a person tells later is not the same as the record.** In 1929 Forssmann wrote that a colleague helped and then he did it alone. In 1972 he wrote that he tied a nurse to a table. The report written a few weeks after the event, with the X-rays on the desk, is the one to trust.

**The people who called it a circus trick were not completely wrong.** Forssmann had no plan for what to do next, and no training in the kind of science that would have needed. He tricked a nurse. He claimed practice runs he probably never did. The next step, into the hearts of sick people, needed things he did not have, and it was taken by other people.

So the lesson is simple. Showing that a tool works is not the same as doing an experiment with it, and the distance between the two can be eleven years and an ocean.
