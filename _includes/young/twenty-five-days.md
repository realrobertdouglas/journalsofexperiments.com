## The telephone call

On the morning of September 14th 1962, a young man woke up in a red tent. The tent stood on a glacier, a river of old ice, deep inside a mountain on the border between France and Italy, about 130 metres below the ground. It was always completely dark outside the tent. The man had no watch, no clock and no radio, and nobody had told him the time or the date for two months.

He did what he did at every waking. He wound the handle of a field telephone, a telephone that worked on a wire running up through the rock to a small camp on the mountain above. He told the man who answered his pulse. He counted out loud from one to 120, trying to say one number every second. And he said what day he thought it was. He thought it was about August 20th.

The man on the surface told him it was September 14th, and that the experiment was over.

The young man did not believe him. He thought his friends were lying to get him out of the cave early. He was sure he had about a month still to go. But it was true. He had been underground for about sixty days, and he had lived them, by his own count, as about thirty-five. He was twenty-five days behind the calendar.

The young man was called Michel Siffre. He was twenty-three, and he was a geologist from the city of Nice, in the south of France. He had planned the whole experiment himself. He was also the one person in it who could not be allowed to see the results.

## Who he was

Michel Siffre went into his first cave when he was ten years old. By the time he was seventeen, scientists in Paris were presenting his notes about rocks and caves to the French Academy of Sciences. He was a caver: someone who explores caves.

In August 1961, Siffre led a group of cavers from Nice into the Marguareis mountains, where the caves open at 2,000 metres above the sea. At the bottom of a shaft called the Scarasson, more than a hundred metres down, they found a big cave with a floor of ice: a small glacier, hidden underground. It was so cold and so wet in there that they stayed only an hour.

Siffre decided to go back and camp on the ice to study it, for two months. And then he had an idea that he said later became the idea of his life. He had been reading about how animals have a kind of clock inside them: rats kept in the dark still sleep and wake in a daily pattern. Did people have a clock like that too? Nobody knew, because nobody had ever lived for weeks with no way of telling the time. Siffre decided to find out, using himself. He would live, he said, "like an animal, without a watch, in the dark, without knowing the time."

## What he wanted to find out

Siffre was asking two questions at once.

The first was about survival. In 1962 people were afraid of nuclear war and shelters under the ground, and the first astronauts had just flown into space. Siffre wanted to know how long a person could stand being alone, cold and in the dark.

The second question was the one that mattered in the end. If a person is cut off from the sun, from clocks and from other people, will his body still keep to a day? And if it does, how long will that day be?

His way of answering it was simple. He would call the surface every time he woke up, every time he ate, and every time he was about to go to sleep. The people on the surface were never allowed to call him, so that nothing from the world of clocks could reach him. At every call he gave his pulse, counted to 120, and said what time and date he thought it was. The people above wrote down what he guessed, and next to it they wrote down the real time. That list, kept on the mountain where Siffre could not see it, was the real instrument of the experiment.

## What he built

The camp was a small tent on the ice. The air in the cave was always at or below freezing, and it was so damp that water formed on everything. The groundsheet, the camp bed and the sleeping bag were wet from the first day. He had a little stove, but he was afraid to run it at night in case it poisoned the air. He had books, a tape recorder and a pile of records. He had no watch: on July 16th 1962, at the top of the shaft, he handed it to a policeman named Canova, and then he climbed down. At his own request, the ladder was pulled up after him so that he could not change his mind.

There was no big organisation behind him, and he went into debt to pay for the trip. His helpers were his caving friends and a team of French mountain-rescue police, who took turns to sit by the telephone in a tent at 2,000 metres and write down his calls.

## What his body did

Siffre's body kept time. From the first days, his calls came in a pattern: a waking, meals, a sleep, a waking. When the list was studied afterwards, the pattern ran on a day of twenty-four hours and thirty-one minutes. A man with no sun was living a day half an hour longer than the real one, and keeping it almost as steadily as a clock. Because his day was a little too long, he slowly drifted round. By the tenth day he was sleeping while it was daytime on the surface and awake while it was night, and he had no idea.

Siffre's mind did not keep time. He sat up for what felt like a few hours and had been awake fourteen or eighteen. Sometimes he lay down for what he thought was a nap of an hour or two, got up, spent an "afternoon" and ate "dinner", and had really gone through a whole extra day of sleeping and waking. Each of those extra days was a day to his body and only half a day to him.

He tried to count his days in his diary and could not make it come out. Near the end he wrote that he had woken forty-one times, so he must have been there about forty-two days; but it felt more like twenty-seven. So he added the two numbers and divided by two, and got thirty-five. Thirty-five days after July 16th is August 20th. The real answer was about sixty. The counting test told the same story: every time he counted from one to 120, trying to say a number a second, it took him five minutes.

The people above could see all this happening. One of the policemen drew the calls on a chart, and the chart showed a very regular rhythm, and a gap between Siffre's guesses and the real date that grew bigger every day. They wrote it down and said nothing. That was the rule, and the rule was what made the experiment work.

## What it was like inside

Siffre kept a diary by the light of a small lamp, with one arm out of his wet sleeping bag. He wrote that he felt as if he were standing still while time flowed past him. Falling asleep became the one pleasure. Waking up was the worst moment, lying in the total dark wondering whether he was asleep or awake.

His memory began to fail. He put the same record on three times in a row, each time thinking he had just chosen it; the policemen listening upstairs said he once played the same song ten times running. Twice, while he was on the telephone, the tape recorder caught the crash of rock and ice falling somewhere in the cave, and one day a huge fall came down a few metres from his tent. "I begin to be really terrified," he wrote. He was also very cold. Early on he took his temperature, read a number that seemed far too low, decided the thermometer was broken, and stopped using it. It was not broken. His body had cooled so far that he was in a kind of half-hibernation, like an animal in winter.

## Coming out

When the call came on September 14th, a team climbed down to him at the weekend. On the morning of September 17th he began the climb up the shaft. He fainted twice on the way. At the top the police put a hood over his head to protect his eyes from the daylight, and two friends carried him on their backs across the mountain to a helicopter. He told a reporter that it was good to see the sky again, and that alone at the bottom he had cried.

The newspapers called it a world record: about sixty-two days alone underground. Some of them said he had broken down, and for a while cavers in other countries believed that two months was the most a person could stand alone in a cave. A Yorkshire caver named Geoffrey Workman stayed down for 105 days the next year, knowing the time, and came up fit.

## The others

Siffre did not go into a cave alone again for ten years. Instead he sent other people, with the French army paying, because the army wanted to know how long a soldier could be kept awake. In the winter of 1964 he put a furniture maker named Antoine Senni and a midwife named Josiane Laures into two caves near Nice. When Siffre phoned Laures on March 11th 1965 to say she could come up, she thought it was February 25th and said: "It is not true. You must be lying." She had been down for 88 days. Senni stayed 126.

Then something strange appeared. In 1968 a caver named Philippe Englender settled into an almost perfectly regular day of forty-eight hours: thirty-six hours awake, twelve asleep, and he had no idea he was doing it. Nearly everyone Siffre sent down seemed to fall into this two-day day sooner or later, except Siffre himself. You can read about them in [The speleonauts](/deeper/twenty-five-days/the-speleonauts/#young).

## Midnight Cave

So in 1972, when he was thirty-three, Siffre went down again, this time to catch the forty-eight-hour day himself, and for six months. The cave was Midnight Cave, on a ranch about fifty miles from the town of Del Rio in Texas, near the Mexican border. NASA, the American space agency, was interested, because it wanted to know how astronauts would manage on very long flights. So was the French Ministry of Defence.

This cave was warm, about 20 degrees, and the camp was almost a laboratory: a wooden platform about a hundred feet down with a nylon tent on it, electric light, a freezer and frozen meals exactly like the ones the Apollo 16 astronauts ate. Siffre was wired up: a probe recorded his body temperature every five minutes, electrodes on his chest recorded his heartbeat, and electrodes glued to his head recorded his brain waves while he slept. The wires ran up to a tent on the surface where four French friends kept watch day and night. His only link to them, as before, was a one-way telephone. He went down on February 14th 1972, Valentine's Day, after kissing his wife Nathalie goodbye.

For the first five weeks his body kept a steady day of about twenty-six hours. Then, on the thirty-seventh day, without knowing it, he skipped his usual bedtime and stayed up for hours, and after that his days went wild: sometimes short, sometimes very long. Some lasted fifty hours. He slept two hours or eighteen and could not tell the difference. Twice he fell into the forty-eight-hour day he had come to find, but only for a while, and he could not feel it happening.

His mind suffered badly. About eleven weeks in, he wrote in his diary that his "crack-up" had begun. He was lonely and full of despair, and at one point he even thought about killing himself, though he did not act on it. He told his friend on the telephone that he had had enough; the friend just said, "Yes, yes," and kept him talking. In the fifth month a mouse began visiting his camp. He was so glad of the company that he named it Mus and tried to catch it under a dish to keep as a pet. When he finally brought the dish down, the edge hit the mouse on the head and killed it. "Desolation overwhelms me," he wrote.

On August 10th the telephone rang, and his friend Gérard told him the time-free part of the experiment was over. Siffre thought it was the middle of July. Once again he was about a month behind. He stayed down for nearly four more weeks for other tests, this time knowing the date, and came out on September 5th 1972 after 205 days, into a crowd of reporters.

## After

The Texas experiment cost Siffre a great deal. He came out 100,000 dollars in debt, he suffered a deep depression that took a year to pass, and three years later he was still having memory lapses. Much of the data was never fully studied, because he could not afford to go on with the work.

In 1999, when he was sixty, he went underground one more time, for seventy-six days, to see what growing older had done to his inner clock. He came out on February 14th 2000 believing it was February 5th: nine days behind, not twenty-five. This time he knew he had a clock, and knew not to trust his feelings.

Other scientists carried the work on in laboratories. For thirty years they agreed that the human body's natural day was about twenty-five hours. Then, in 1999, a team at Harvard worked out that the lamps the subjects switched on and off for themselves had been nudging their clocks later every day. With the light controlled, the human day came out at twenty-four hours and eleven minutes. The clock Siffre found is real. Most of the extra half hour was his lamp. You can read that story in [The length of a day](/deeper/twenty-five-days/the-length-of-a-day/#young).

Michel Siffre died in Nice on August 25th 2024, aged eighty-five.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**You cannot be your own measuring instrument.** Siffre's only sensor for time was his own feeling of how long things took. That feeling was wrong by twenty-five days, and he had no way of knowing, because the one record that could have told him was kept where he could not see it. That was good design: it kept the experiment honest. But it also meant that nobody could tell the instrument it was broken. When you measure something with yourself, keep a second record that you cannot touch, and expect to disbelieve it when you finally see it.

**Your body and your mind keep two different clocks.** Siffre's body kept a very steady day without any help. His mind lost a day in every two. If you ever have to rely on your sense of how much time has passed, in a dark place or on a long journey, know that it can be half wrong.

**A result can be right and still be read differently later.** For thirty years everyone thought the human day was about twenty-five hours long. Then scientists found that the subjects' own lamps had been pushing their clocks later. The caves were not really dark; they had a light, and a light is a time cue. That did not make the old experiments useless. It told scientists what they had really been measuring.

**Experiments on one person cost that person everything.** Siffre was his own subject, and he paid with his health, his money and his career. The people he sent down after him were cavers who wanted records, and one of them, years later, took her own life. The clock could have been found, and was found, in a laboratory with a door the subject could open.

So the lesson is simple. When you are measuring something, make sure the instrument is not you. And if it has to be you, keep a record you cannot see, and believe it when it tells you that you are wrong.
