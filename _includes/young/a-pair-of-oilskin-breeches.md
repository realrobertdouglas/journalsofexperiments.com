## A sock in the ice

On June 18th 1884, three Greenlanders were hunting seals from their kayaks in a fjord near Julianehaab, a Danish trading town at the southern tip of Greenland. They saw a raven that kept circling over a lump of floating ice, and paddled over to look. A cotton sock was sticking out of the ice. Frozen in around it were other things: half a tent, a bearskin, a boot, scraps of paper, buttons, and clothes. One piece of clothing was a pair of oilskin trousers, the waterproof trousers sailors wear, with a name written inside: Louis Noros.

Louis Noros was a sailor in the United States Navy. Three years earlier, to the very day, he had walked off an ice floe on the other side of the North Pole, north of Siberia, with thirty-two other men. Their ship, the *Jeannette*, had just sunk under them. The place where she sank was about 2,500 nautical miles from Julianehaab in a straight line, and the straight line went almost over the Pole.

That autumn a young Norwegian scientist read about the find in a newspaper and had an idea that took him twelve years to carry out.

## The man with the newspaper and the man with the theory

The *Jeannette* was paid for by James Gordon Bennett, who owned the *New York Herald*, a newspaper. In 1877 he went to Germany to see August Petermann, a famous map-maker who had spent thirty years thinking about the North Pole.

Petermann had a theory. A warm ocean current, the Kuro Siwo, flows north past Japan. Petermann believed it went on through Bering Strait, the narrow gap between Alaska and Siberia, and kept the sea open far to the north, maybe all the way to the Pole. He also believed that a coast which a whaling captain had seen in 1867, called Wrangel Land, was the edge of a huge continent that stretched across the Pole and came out the other side as Greenland. A ship, he said, could follow the warm water and then the coast, and reach the Pole in a single summer.

Bennett found a naval officer who wanted to try: George Washington De Long, a lieutenant in his thirties who had already taken a small steam launch through the ice off Greenland. De Long thought the same way. In 1878 he wrote to Bennett that of the three possible routes to the Pole, "I am in favor of Behring Strait."

So the experiment had two ideas to test. First: is there a warm current that keeps a channel open towards the Pole? Second: is Wrangel Land a continent with a coast to follow? There was a third idea underneath, which nobody thought of as an idea, because it seemed obvious: that a ship caught in the ice up there would drift north.

Petermann never saw the test. He killed himself in September 1878, a year before the ship sailed.

## The ship

Bennett bought a British ship called the *Pandora*, renamed her *Jeannette* after his sister, and gave her to the US Navy to run. The Navy's inspectors said she was lightly built, with thin frames two feet apart. The dockyard in California filled her bow with solid wood, lined her inside with thick oak and put heavy braces across her middle. The sides of the ship were now nineteen and a half inches thick.

Nobody planned a way for the ship to send news home. No carrier pigeons, no relief ship. The only way the experiment could report its result was for the ship to come back.

She sailed from San Francisco on July 8th 1879 with thirty-three men. The Navy's orders told De Long to go to Bering Strait and then "proceed on your voyage toward the North Pole." Bennett sent a telegram: "I wish this to be an American success."

## Stuck

It was already late in the year. On September 5th 1879 the *Jeannette* pushed into the ice pack near Herald Island, a small rock north of Bering Strait, and the ice closed round her. "This is a glorious country to learn patience in," De Long wrote the next day. On September 9th he could see Wrangel Land in the distance. "And this is the land which, two months ago yesterday, we sailed for from San Francisco, hoping to explore this winter. Man proposes but God disposes."

The ship had been at sea fifty-nine days. The ice never let her go.

All that first winter she drifted in circles near Herald Island, going north-west when the wind blew from the south-east and back again when it changed. In January 1880 the ice squeezed her bow and she began to leak. From then on the men pumped water out of her every day for seventeen months, first with a steam pump, then with a windmill the engineer, George Melville, built on deck, then by hand.

By the end of that winter the men had seen the far side of Wrangel Land. It was not a continent. It was an island about eighty miles long, and the ship was drifting past the top of it. That answered the second question.

De Long answered the first question himself, in his journal, on June 3rd 1880. The ship's thermometers had been measuring the sea water all along, and there was no warm current. "I pronounce a thermometric gate-way to the Pole a delusion and a snare," he wrote. In the same entry he worked out where the ice must be going. The winds from the south-east kept pushing it north-west, he reasoned, and it probably ended up in the current that flows south past Spitsbergen into the Atlantic. He was right. Nobody could read it. The journal was on the ship, and the ship was in the ice.

"One year in the ice!" he wrote on September 5th 1880, "and we are only one hundred and fifty miles to the northward and westward of where we entered it."

The second winter was harder. Some of the men were poisoned by lead from the tins their food came in. One sailor became convinced there was a plot against him. The navigator, John Danenhower, went half blind. But in the spring of 1881 the ship began to move steadily north-west, eight miles a day, and in May the men saw land for the first time in fifteen months: two small islands, which De Long named Jeannette Island and Henrietta Island.

## The last day of the ship

On June 12th 1881 the ice came in hard against the ship's side. Melville, down in the engine room, saw a crack open right across her. The men got the boats, the food, the sledges and the dogs out on to the ice. At eight in the evening De Long ordered everybody off. At four the next morning, June 13th, the *Jeannette* sank. She had been in the ice for 646 days.

That night De Long wrote: "Everybody seems bright and cheerful, with plenty to eat and plenty of clothes. Even music is not forgotten. Lauterbach serenaded us to-night with a mouth harmonica."

## Walking to Siberia

The thirty-three men now had to drag three boats on sledges across the moving ice to the coast of Siberia, hundreds of miles to the south. They set off on June 18th 1881. After a week De Long worked out from his observations that the ice was carrying them north-west faster than they were walking south. He did not tell the men. He changed course to get across the current.

In September they reached open water and took to the boats. On September 12th a storm separated them. One boat, with Lieutenant Chipp and seven men, was never seen again. Melville's boat, with eleven men, reached a river mouth and found native people who helped them. De Long's boat, with fourteen men, ran aground on a muddy shore with no people anywhere near it. They waded in with what they could carry and started walking.

The food ran out in early October. De Long sent two men, Nindemann and Noros, ahead to look for help. Noros's oilskin trousers, as he later remembered it, were probably left behind in a pile of things the party could not carry. The rest followed slowly. De Long's journal became a line or two a day, with the days counted from the sinking of the ship. "October 17th. One hundred and twenty-seventh day. Alexey dying. Doctor baptized him." "October 22d. One hundred and thirty-second day. Too weak to carry the bodies of Lee and Kaack out on the ice. Then my eye closed up."

Nindemann and Noros found native people, but could not make them understand. Melville heard about them at the end of October and went to search, but winter stopped him. In March 1882 he found the camp. De Long lay on his side, and about four feet behind him was the small notebook, where he had tossed it with his left hand. Of the thirty-three men, twenty were dead.

## What the Navy decided

The journals were sent home and De Long's widow, Emma, published them in 1883. A Navy court of inquiry looked at everything and blamed nobody. It said that any ship, however strong, would have been crushed by that ice. It also said, in a plain sentence, that the expedition had produced "a want of important results". As far as Washington was concerned, that was the end: a theory destroyed, a ship lost, twenty men dead.

Four months later, the floe came ashore in Greenland.

## What the floe said

The Danish manager at Julianehaab, Carl Lytzen, wrote down everything the kayakers had found: a list of provisions signed by De Long, a list of the *Jeannette*'s boats, a torn cheque book, a cap peak with a sailor's name on it, buttons, tent ropes, and the oilskin trousers marked Louis Noros. His letter reached New York in August 1884.

Many Americans did not believe it. Newspapers invented a story that a dead body had been found under the bearskin; Lytzen asked the kayakers, who said there had been no such thing. Some experts said the objects must have come from a different wreck, far to the north in Smith Sound, because nothing could possibly drift from Siberia right across the top of the world. The companion piece, "[The list](/deeper/a-pair-of-oilskin-breeches/the-list/#young)", tells that argument in full.

In Norway, a professor named Henrik Mohn looked at the ocean currents and said the floe could only have come one way: across the polar sea, down the east coast of Greenland, and round the bottom of it. A young scientist in Bergen, Fridtjof Nansen, read Mohn's article in the autumn of 1884. If a floe could cross the unknown sea, he thought, a ship frozen into a floe could cross it too. "My plan was laid," he wrote.

## The ship built to be frozen in

Nansen did his sums. The floe had travelled about 2,900 nautical miles in about 1,100 days: between two and three miles a day. That was almost exactly the speed the *Jeannette* had drifted in her last months. So a ship put into the ice north of Siberia should come out in the sea between Greenland and Spitsbergen in two or three years.

The *Jeannette* had been crushed because the ice could grip her straight sides and squeeze. Nansen's ship would have sides that sloped, so that when the ice pushed, it would slide underneath and lift her up instead. "Instead of nipping the ship, the ice must raise it up out of the water," he wrote. She would be small, short and very strong, with her sides nearly three feet thick. He named her *Fram*, which means "forward". The ship would stop being a ship, he said, and become a barrack that the current carried.

Nansen explained the plan to the Royal Geographical Society in London in November 1892. The most experienced polar sailors in Britain told him it would not work. One admiral called it "the most adventurous programme ever brought under the notice of the Royal Geographical Society." Another said that once a ship was frozen into the pack, "the form of the vessel goes for nothing." An American general, Adolphus Greely, who had lost most of his own men in the Arctic, called it "Dr. Nansen's illogical scheme of self-destruction." The companion piece "[The prophets](/deeper/a-pair-of-oilskin-breeches/the-prophets/#young)" gives what they said.

## Three years in the ice, on purpose

The *Fram* left Norway on June 24th 1893 with thirteen men and was frozen in north of Siberia on September 22nd. On October 9th the ice squeezed her for the first time. "A deafening noise began, and the whole ship shook," Nansen wrote. "Every one rushed on deck to look. The *Fram* behaved beautifully, as I had expected she would. On pushed the ice with steady pressure, but down under us it had to go, and we were slowly lifted up."

The drift went just as the floe had gone: north-west, then west, towards Greenland. But one thing was not as Nansen had expected. He had thought the polar sea was shallow, a few hundred feet deep. When the crew lowered a weighted line through the ice, it went down and down. They had to tie every line on board together. In 1895 they found the bottom at 3,800 metres.

In March 1895 Nansen and one companion, Hjalmar Johansen, left the ship and tried to reach the Pole on foot with dogs and sledges. They got to 86 degrees 14 minutes north, closer than anyone before, and had to turn back. They spent a winter in a stone hut on Franz Josef Land and were found by an English expedition in June 1896. The *Fram* kept drifting under her captain, Otto Sverdrup, and broke out of the ice north of Spitsbergen on August 13th 1896. "WE WERE FREE!" Sverdrup wrote.

One of the admirals who had doubted the plan wrote to Nansen to say that the *Fram*'s voyage had proved him wrong. General Greely admitted that the *Jeannette* relics had been real after all. But he still found a fault: Nansen had left his ship and his men, which De Long had never done.

In 2019 a German research icebreaker, the *Polarstern*, was frozen into the ice north of Siberia and carried for a year past the Pole, by scientists who said they were following Nansen.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**An experiment is no use if it cannot report.** The *Jeannette* found the answer to both of her questions in a few months: there was no warm current and there was no continent. De Long even worked out where the ice was really going. But the only way the result could get home was for the ship to come home, and the very thing she had discovered, the drift, made that impossible. The answer sat in a notebook for two years, and the man who wrote it died carrying it.

**A failure is data.** Almost everyone looked at the *Jeannette* and saw a disaster. Nansen looked at the same facts and saw measurements: a ship that drifted west-north-west for two years, a floe that finished the journey, a weak ship that still lasted twenty-one months. He used every one of those numbers to design the *Fram*.

**Fix the one thing that broke.** The *Jeannette* was crushed because the ice could grip her sides. Nansen did not try to make a stronger ship of the same shape. He changed the shape, so that the ice would lift her instead of squeezing her. Then the thing that had destroyed the first ship became the engine of the second.

**Being right about the big idea does not make you right about everything.** Nansen was right about the drift. He was wrong about the depth of the sea, wrong about a current along the Siberian coast, and the drift never took the *Fram* as close to the Pole as he hoped. The experiment still worked, because the ship came back with all its measurements, and the measurements could be checked.

So the lesson is simple. When you design an experiment, design the way its result will get home, or the result may arrive three years late on a pair of trousers.
