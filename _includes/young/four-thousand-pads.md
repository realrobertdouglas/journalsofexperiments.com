## The biggest airship in the world

An airship is a flying ship: a huge bag of gas lighter than air, with a metal frame inside it, engines hung on the outside, and cabins for people underneath. In 1930 the biggest airship in the world was British. She was called R101, and she was 777 feet long, longer than two football pitches end to end. Inside her were seventeen enormous gasbags full of hydrogen, a gas so light that it lifted the whole ship, and so easy to set alight that nobody was allowed to smoke anywhere except in one special room.

On the evening of October 4th 1930 she left her mooring tower at Cardington, near Bedford, to fly to India. Fifty-four people were on board, including the Air Minister, the man in the government in charge of flying. Seven and a half hours later she came down on a hillside in France and burned. Forty-eight people died.

This is the story of how she came to leave when she did, and of the trials that would have found out whether she was ready, and were never flown.

## The experiment

In 1921 a British airship called R38 had broken in two in the air. The people who looked into it found that her designers had only worked out the strains on a ship hanging still, not on a ship flying through the wind. So in 1924 the government started an experiment to do airships properly. Two new airships would be built, both much bigger than anything before. One would be built by the government itself, at Cardington. The other would be built by a private company, at Howden in Yorkshire, for a fixed price. Both had to meet the same list of requirements: five million cubic feet of gas, room for a hundred passengers, a top speed of seventy miles an hour, and a structure light enough to leave sixty tons of lift to spare for fuel and people. Then each would fly to a far part of the Empire and back.

The government's ship was R101. The company's ship was R100. A young engineer on R100 named Nevil Shute Norway, who later became a famous novelist, wrote that the gods had decided to find out "which is the better principle, capitalism or State enterprise." That was the experiment. Two ships, built to the same rules by different people, and then compared.

The man in charge of it all was Lord Thomson, the Air Minister. He believed in airships so much that when he was made a lord he took his title from Cardington, the village where R101 was being built. He wanted to be the first person to fly to India by airship.

## What they built

R101 took four years to build, and she was built with enormous care. About a hundred of her girders were tested until they broke, to make sure the rest would not. Almost everything about her was new: a stainless steel frame, gasbags hung in a clever net of wires that was meant to keep them from touching the frame, a new kind of gas valve, and five engines that ran on heavy oil instead of petrol, because petrol was thought too dangerous in hot countries.

The engines were the first warning. They were meant to give 700 horsepower each; they gave 585, and all five together weighed seventeen tons. R100's petrol engines weighed under nine. The second warning came when the gasbags were filled for the first time, in September 1929. The ship was supposed to weigh ninety tons and lift 150, leaving sixty tons to spare. She weighed nearly 114 tons. That left only thirty-five tons to spare, and a ship with thirty-five tons to spare could not carry enough fuel to reach Egypt with anything left over.

She flew for the first time on October 14th 1929. Lord Thomson flew in her four days later and told the newspapers he hoped to go to India in her at Christmas. He also said that his policy was "safety first", and that nobody would be pushed into a flight before they were ready.

That autumn she made seven test flights, about seventy hours in all, all in good weather. The longest was thirty hours. She never flew at full speed. One day in November a gale blew while she was tied to her tower, with gusts of eighty-three miles an hour, and she rode it out. But her chief coxswain, the man who steered her, wrote down what he saw inside her that day. The gasbags were swinging about and rubbing against the metal frame. One of them had been torn nine inches by a metal plate. When the gasbags were taken out that winter, every one but one had holes in it. One bag had 103.

## Four thousand pads

Because she could not lift enough, the engineers decided to take out everything she could do without, and to let out the wires round the gasbags so the bags could swell bigger and hold more gas. Then they would cut the ship in half and put in a new section with an eighteenth gasbag. Lord Thomson agreed, and said it was better to wait than to fly to India too soon. The flight was put off to the end of September 1930. Everybody agreed that a rebuilt ship would need to be tested again. The Air Ministry's senior officer wrote in December 1929 that before she could get her full Certificate of Airworthiness, the official paper that says an aircraft is fit to fly, "R.101 has yet to do a 48 hours' endurance flight."

But letting out the wires meant the gasbags now touched the frame everywhere. To stop them rubbing, the engineers wrapped cloth pads round every nut and bolt and sharp corner they could find. They made four thousand of them.

In June 1930 the ship came out again to fly at the Royal Air Force Display at Hendon. The very first day, her outer cover split open for 140 feet. They patched it and flew anyway. Over three days she flew about thirty hours, and the officers noticed that she kept getting heavier, because she was losing gas. On one day nine tons of ballast had to be thrown out to keep her up. Her captain wrote that there was "an abundance of holes in gasbags" where they had rubbed on the nuts of the girders.

Then the inspector wrote a letter. His name was Frederick McWade, and he had worked on airships for twenty-seven years. It was his job to hand the ship the permit that let her fly. On July 3rd 1930 he wrote to the Air Ministry in London that the gasbags were now "hard up against" the girders, that the places where they rubbed "amount to thousands", that the pads came loose in flight, and that there were already "many holes" in bags that had been perfect a few weeks before. He ended: "Until this matter is seriously taken in hand and remedied I cannot recommend to you the extension of the present 'Permit to Fly' or the issue of any further permit or certificate."

The man in London who got the letter did the proper thing and asked Cardington what they thought. Cardington's director wrote back that padding was a perfectly good answer and that it would all be finished by the end of the week. The man in London was satisfied, and decided he did not need to send the letter any higher. The inquiry later found that "neither the Secretary of State nor any member of the Air Council learned anything about it." The people who had to fix the problem were the same people who were asked whether it was a problem, and they said no.

## The deadline

That same July the calendar closed in. Lord Thomson wrote that he wanted to leave for India in the last weekend of September and be back by October 16th, in time for the Imperial Conference, a big meeting of leaders from all over the Empire in London. When the engineers asked for a week's delay, he wrote: "I must insist on the programme for the Indian flight being adhered to, as I have made my plans accordingly." The officer in charge warned him that the plan left "no margin" for anything going wrong. Lord Thomson replied that he was "not unduly pessimistic".

Meanwhile the other ship flew. R100 had been tested for fifty-three hours at a stretch, flown at full speed many times, and flown in bad weather. In July 1930 she flew to Canada in seventy-nine hours, got caught in a thunderstorm that threw her up 2,500 feet, and came home in August in fifty-seven hours. Lord Thomson met her at the tower and said she showed that the great experiment would succeed.

R101 was put back together on September 25th. The weather kept her in the shed until October 1st. There were now three days to the date Lord Thomson had planned. The night before she came out, her director asked the Air Ministry whether the test flight could be shorter than twenty-four hours, "So that we may have a chance, if all goes well, of starting on Friday evening." The Air Ministry said yes.

The test flight lasted sixteen hours and fifty-one minutes, on a calm, clear night. One engine broke down early, so she could not be tried at full speed. Her designer wrote in his diary that "Flying conditions were very perfect". No written report on the flight was ever found. Her captain had written down, long before, the trials he thought she needed: forty-eight hours in bad weather, with six hours at full speed, and then a complete inspection in the shed. None of that was done.

On October 2nd, the day the test flight ended, the Air Ministry issued her Certificate of Airworthiness. The form said it was issued "having regard to the Reports furnished". But the main report, by the professor whose job it was to check the rebuilt ship's sums, had not been written yet. He was still writing it on the Sunday when he heard the news.

That same evening the director met Lord Thomson to fix the start. Lord Thomson said: "You must not allow my natural impatience or anxiety to start to influence you in any way. You must use your considered judgment." His secretary's note of the meeting says, "No rush on my account." And as the meeting ended Lord Thomson said: "Well that is all settled; I can make certain of being back on the 20th."

## The last flight

On Friday October 3rd the first officer wrote in his diary that everybody was "rather keyed up", because the future of airships depended on the flight, and that "that thing called 'Luck' will figure rather conspicuously." That day Lord Thomson wrote his will.

She left at 6.36 in the evening on Saturday October 4th 1930, heavy with twenty-five tons of fuel, into rising wind and drizzle. The forecast that morning had promised winds of twenty to thirty miles an hour over France. An hour and a half after she left, a new forecast said forty to fifty, a wind no British airship had ever met over land. Over the Channel she flew so low that an officer took the wheel himself and said, "Do not let her go below 1,000 feet." At midnight, near Abbeville in France, she sent a cheerful message: the passengers had "smoked a final cigar" after "an excellent supper" and gone to bed, and "the crew have settled down to watch-keeping routine."

At two in the morning the watch changed, as usual. Nobody in charge knew anything was wrong. A few minutes later, in a storm of wind and rain near the town of Beauvais, the ship put her nose down and dived for about thirty seconds. In the smoking room a siphon and glasses slid off the table. Then she came level for a few seconds. The chief coxswain came out of the control car and said to the sleeping crew, "We're down, lads." The engine telegraphs rang "slow". Then she dived again and touched the ground, gently, and within a second she was on fire. The companion piece, "[The last watch](/deeper/four-thousand-pads/the-last-watch/#young)", tells the whole of that night from the wireless messages and the survivors.

Six men got out alive, most of them because they were in the engine cars hung outside the hull. Forty-six people died at once, and two more died in hospital. The Air Minister, the ship's designer, her captain and all her officers were among the dead.

## What the inquiry found

Britain held a public inquiry, led by a famous lawyer, Sir John Simon. It sat for thirteen days and heard forty-two witnesses, including the six survivors, the inspector McWade, and the head of the German Zeppelin company. McWade's letter only turned up in the middle of the hearings; it had been put aside for copying and forgotten.

The inquiry found that the ship was strong, that her controls had worked, and that her officers had done nothing wrong. What brought her down was gas. In the rough air she had lost so much hydrogen from the bags at her front, probably through holes worn at the padded points, through valves that opened as she rolled, and in the end through a tear in her cover, that when she dived she could not lift her nose again. On the decision to fly, the inquiry wrote: "It is impossible to avoid the conclusion that the R.101 would not have started for India on the evening of October 4th if it had not been that reasons of public policy were considered as making it highly desirable for her to do so if she could." It did not blame Lord Thomson, who had relied on his experts, or the experts, who believed in their ship. It said the whole atmosphere had pushed them: the years of waiting, the conference, the success of R100, and the minister's own seat on board.

R100 never flew again. She was broken up for scrap and sold for a few hundred pounds. Britain never built another big airship.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A test is only a test if you let it finish.** Everybody agreed that the rebuilt ship needed a long trial in bad weather. The plan said forty-eight hours, then twenty-four, then "shorter if she behaves well", and in the end she flew for seventeen hours on a perfect night with one engine out. Each time the trial got shorter, it was because the date was getting closer. A test that is cut down to fit the calendar is not telling you about the ship. It is telling you about the calendar.

**Listen to the warnings, especially the ones you cannot fix.** The ship warned everyone, over and over: heavy engines, too little lift, holes in the gasbags, nine tons of ballast gone in a day, an inspector saying he could not recommend a permit. Each warning was answered with a quick fix, like four thousand pads, instead of a proper test of whether the fix worked. When a problem is hard to fix, people often decide it is not really a problem.

**Do not ask the people who caused a problem whether it is a problem.** McWade sent his letter to London precisely so that someone outside Cardington would judge it. London sent it straight back to Cardington to ask what they thought. Of course they said it was fine. If the only people who understand something are the people who built it, somebody else still has to be able to say no.

**Saying "no rush" does not take the rush away.** Lord Thomson meant it when he said safety came first and that nobody should hurry on his account. But he had also written "I must insist", and he had fixed the date of his return, and everyone knew he was going to be on board. People hear the deadline, not the kind words that come with it.

**If you build two things to compare them, compare them.** The whole point of building two ships was to see which way of building worked better. The private ship did all her trials before she crossed the Atlantic. The government ship skipped hers. That comparison was sitting there in August 1930, and nobody made it until the inquiry did, after the crash.

So the lesson is simple. When you have written down how to find out whether something is safe, do that, all of it, before you bet lives on it. If the date will not allow the test, change the date.
