## The sickness

Yellow fever is a disease that begins with a chill and a headache, turns the skin yellow, and in the worst cases makes people vomit black blood and die. In 1900 nobody knew what caused it or how it travelled from one person to the next. It came every summer to the ports of the southern United States. In 1878 it reached 132 towns and killed about 16,000 people. In Havana, the capital of Cuba, it had been present every single month since 1762.

Because nobody knew how it spread, people guessed. The guess that everybody believed was that the sickness lived in things: the sheets a sick person had slept in, the shirt he had worn, the clothes in his trunk. The old word for such things is fomites. Every summer the ports burned mattresses, soaked clothes in poison, and fumigated every trunk that came from the south. Hundreds of years of quarantine law rested on that guess. Nobody had ever tested it.

## The four doctors

In May 1900 the United States Army, which was running Cuba after the war with Spain, sent four doctors to find out what caused yellow fever. Major Walter Reed, aged forty-eight, was in charge. James Carroll had been a private soldier and had studied medicine while in the ranks. Aristides Agramonte had been born in Cuba and was believed to be immune: a person who has had the disease once cannot catch it again. Jesse Lazear, aged thirty-four, from Baltimore, knew more about mosquitoes than any of them. They met for the first time on June 25th 1900 on a veranda at Columbia Barracks, an army camp west of Havana, and agreed that whatever they found would belong to all four of them together.

There was one other important doctor. Carlos Finlay of Havana had said in 1881 that a mosquito carried yellow fever from the sick to the well. For nineteen years he had tried to prove it by letting mosquitoes bite people, and he had never produced a case that anyone believed. Most doctors thought his idea was silly.

## The two ideas

The four doctors decided to test Finlay's idea, and they knew from the start that it was really two ideas, not one.

The first idea was that a particular mosquito, the kind that bites by day and breeds in the water jars inside houses, drank the blood of a sick person, kept the sickness inside itself for some days, and then gave it to the next person it bit. The second idea was the opposite of what everybody believed: that the sheets, the shirts and the black vomit carried nothing at all.

Nobody had tested the second idea, because the test meant asking healthy people to sleep in the bedding of people who had died. The doctors wrote that they could see no other way to settle it, and there was no animal that caught yellow fever. The test would have to be done on people.

## The first bites

Lazear began in August 1900. He bred mosquitoes and kept them in glass test tubes. He turned a tube upside down on the skin of a patient and let the mosquito drink, and some days later turned the same tube over on the arm of a volunteer. Through August nine men were bitten and nobody got sick.

Then on August 27th Carroll held a tube on his own arm. The mosquito in it had bitten a very sick man twelve days before. Four days later Carroll had yellow fever, and nearly died of it. The same morning a soldier called William Dean stopped at the laboratory door and asked, "You still fooling with mosquitoes, Doctor?" Lazear asked if he would take a bite. "Sure I ain't scared of 'em," Dean said. He had not left the army camp for two months, so there was no other way he could have caught it. Five days later he had yellow fever too.

Then, on September 13th 1900, Lazear was bitten by a mosquito in a hospital ward in Havana. He fell ill on September 18th and died on September 25th. He was thirty-four. His wife was in America with their small daughter, and he never saw their second child. His three colleagues never agreed on exactly how that bite had happened, and people have argued about it ever since.

## The camp

Reed came back from Washington in October and wrote a short report saying the mosquito carried yellow fever. But three cases would convince nobody. A proper test needed a place where no mosquito could reach a man unless the doctors put it there.

So they built a camp. General Leonard Wood, the governor of Cuba, who was a doctor himself, paid for it. It was set up on November 20th 1900 in an open field on a farm about a mile from the town of Quemados, and they named it Camp Lazear. It had seven tents and strict rules. Nobody could come in or go out except the doctors and three men who were immune. Every man's temperature and pulse were written down three times a day, so that anyone who had brought the sickness in with him would be found out before he could spoil the test. Nobody was allowed to be bitten until he had lived inside the fence for longer than the disease takes to appear. Anyone who left was never allowed back.

They needed volunteers. Two young men from Ohio came forward first: John Kissinger, a private in the army's Hospital Corps, and John Moran, a clerk at headquarters. Reed explained that the experiment might kill them, and offered them money. Both refused it; that was their one condition. Reed is said to have touched his cap and told them, "Gentlemen, I salute you."

But most of the soldiers, when it came to it, would rather sleep in the dirty bedding than be bitten. So Agramonte went to the immigration station in Havana whenever a ship came in from Spain and hired eight or ten newly arrived Spaniards to work at the camp picking stones out of the field. He sent away anyone who was too young, who had a family depending on him, or who had lived in the tropics before. Then he explained the experiment and asked them to sign a paper.

One of those papers survives. Antonio Benigno, from a village in Galicia in Spain, signed it on November 26th 1900. It says that he understands "perfectly well" that if he gets yellow fever "he endangers his life to a certain extent," but that since it was impossible to avoid the sickness in Cuba anyway, he would rather catch it on purpose where good doctors could look after him. He would be paid $100 in gold, and another $100 if he got the fever. If he died, the $200 would go to anyone he named. Reed signed it too. Papers like this one are now thought to be among the first written consents in the history of medicine.

## The two huts

Then the doctors built two wooden huts, each fourteen feet by twenty, on opposite sides of a little valley, eighty yards apart.

Building Number One was the bedding hut. It was built to give the fomites every possible chance. Its two little windows were both on one side, so that no air blew through. They had fine wire screens, glass behind them, and wooden shutters outside, so that no sunlight could get in and clean anything. You entered through a tiny porch with two screen doors, one after the other, so that no mosquito could follow you in. A stove kept it as hot as a summer day and a pan of water kept it damp.

Building Number Two was the mosquito hut. It was the same size, but its door and windows were on opposite sides so the air blew through, and a wire screen ran from floor to ceiling down the middle, dividing it into a bigger room and a smaller one. Everything in it was cleaned with steam before it went in.

## Sixty-three days

On the evening of November 30th 1900, Dr Robert Cooke and two soldiers, Levi Folk and Warren Jernegan, walked into Building Number One. Inside were three big boxes that had been locked for two weeks. They were full of sheets, pillowcases and blankets from the beds of people who had died of yellow fever, on purpose made as filthy as possible with black vomit and worse. The three men unpacked the boxes, shook every sheet out to fill the air of the room, made their beds with them, hung the rest round the walls, and went to sleep. They did this every night for twenty nights. When a fourth box arrived on December 12th the smell was so bad that they ran out of the hut, and then went back in and slept there anyway.

Meanwhile the mosquitoes were failing. For two weeks nobody who was bitten got sick, because the weather was cool and the mosquitoes were too young. Then on December 5th five older mosquitoes were put on Kissinger. Three and a half days later, near midnight on December 8th, he had a chill, and in the morning he had yellow fever. Reed sent for the best yellow fever doctors in Havana to look at him, and did not tell them how he had caught it until they had agreed what it was. He wrote to his wife that the case was "as clear as the sun at noon-day."

Within a week three Spaniards had been bitten and all three were ill. Antonio Benigno was one of them. That made four cases of yellow fever among the twelve men in the camp who could catch it, and every one of the four had been bitten. When the fourth man was carried out on a stretcher, the rest of the Spaniards ran away. Reed wrote, with a smile, that they had "suddenly appeared to lose all interest in the progress of science," and that he could not blame them. The three men in the bedding hut, on the other hand, cheered up at once. Reed said they went about looking like "schoolboys let out for a holiday."

On December 21st, at a quarter to twelve, the doctors let fifteen mosquitoes loose in the big room of Building Number Two. At noon John Moran walked in wearing only his nightshirt, straight from a bath, and lay down on the bed. Within two minutes they were biting his face and hands. He stayed half an hour, came back that afternoon, and came back again the next day. On the other side of the wire screen, breathing the same air, sat two men who could catch the disease and had agreed to sleep there every night. On Christmas morning Moran had yellow fever. The two men behind the screen slept in that hut for eighteen nights and never got sick.

Building Number One went on. From December 21st two more soldiers slept there, not just in the dead men's sheets but in the actual nightshirts the patients had worn through their whole illness. From January 11th 1901 two more did the same, with towels soaked in a patient's blood on their pillows. After sixty-three days and seven men, the doctors wrote one sentence: the attempt to infect Building Number One "has proved an absolute failure."

There was one more check, and it was the clever one. Perhaps the seven men just could not catch yellow fever? So the doctors bit two of them with mosquitoes afterwards. Folk, who had slept twenty-one nights in the bedding, was bitten on January 19th and was ill on January 23rd. Another bedding man, James Hanberry, was bitten in February and nearly died. They could catch it. The bedding had simply never had it to give.

## What happened next

On February 6th 1901 Reed read the results to a hall full of doctors in Havana, "crowded and even the doors packed." Two of his conclusions did all the work. Yellow fever is not carried by fomites, so disinfecting clothes, bedding and goods "is unnecessary." And a house has yellow fever in it only when it has infected mosquitoes in it.

Major William Gorgas, who was in charge of keeping Havana clean, had spent two years scrubbing the city, and yellow fever had not gone down at all. So on February 4th 1901 he started on the mosquitoes instead: screens over every sick room and fumes in every room near it. In January there had been seven deaths from yellow fever. In February there were five, in March one, and from April to June none. From September 1901 there was not a single case for nine months, in a city that had never once been free of it in a hundred and fifty years.

The ports stopped burning mattresses. A few years later Gorgas cleared the mosquitoes from Panama, and the canal was dug. Reed died of appendicitis in 1902, aged fifty-one. Kissinger lost the use of his legs and lived for years on a pension of twelve dollars a month. The Spaniards took their money and disappeared. The little hut was still standing in its field forty years later.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A rule can be very old and still never have been tested.** For a hundred years every port in America burned bedding because of stories nobody could check. A doctor had written in 1855 that the stories did not hold up, but nobody did the test, because it meant asking healthy people to sleep in the sheets of the dead.

**The hut where nothing happened was the important one.** The mosquito hut proved that mosquitoes carry yellow fever, and that is the half everybody remembers. But the bedding hut is the half that changed what governments did, because it took the old rule away. Finding a new cause is not enough. You also have to show that the old cause was not a cause at all.

**A result of "nothing happened" is only worth as much as its checks.** Seven men staying well proves nothing unless they could have got sick, so the doctors bit two of them afterwards and both fell ill. The two men behind the wire screen proved it was the insects and not the air. The rule that nobody could be bitten until he had lived inside the fence for weeks is what made Moran's Christmas morning mean something.

**Even a fair agreement rests on what people know at the time.** Benigno signed because, as his paper said, it was impossible to avoid yellow fever in Cuba anyway. That was true in November 1900. By the next September it was false, because of the very experiment he had signed up for. Nobody lied to him. But what he agreed to was built on a world that the experiment was about to end.

So the lesson is simple. When you test an idea, test the old idea at the same time, and give the old idea every chance to win. If it still loses, you have learned something that a hundred years of believing could never have told you.
