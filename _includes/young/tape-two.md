## The tape

On Saturday August 14th 1971, at eleven in the morning, a tape recorder was running in a room at Stanford University in California. A professor named Philip Zimbardo was talking to about a dozen young men. He had hired them to be the guards in a pretend prison, and he was telling them what he wanted.

He said there were things they could not do. In real prisons, he said, people get beaten up and sometimes killed; that could not happen here. But, he said, "we can create boredom; we can create a sense of frustration; we can create a sense of fear in them, to some degree." The prisoners would have no privacy. "They can do nothing, or say nothing that we don't permit. We're going to take away their individuality in various ways." Nobody would call them by name. "In general what all of this leads to is a sense of powerlessness, that is, we have total power in the situation and they have none."

The prisoners would be picked up the next morning. The guards would be paid fifteen dollars a day. And once the prisoners came in, the professor said, nobody must ever call it a study or an experiment: "they are in a prison".

That recording is called Tape Two. It sat in a box for forty-seven years. In all that time, the official story of the experiment said that the guards had been given almost no instructions, and that what they did, they did on their own. This is the story of what happened in the basement, of what was said about it afterwards, and of the day someone finally listened to the tape.

## The people

Philip Zimbardo was thirty-eight. He had grown up in a poor part of New York and become a professor of psychology, the science of how people think and behave. Until the spring of 1971, by his own account, he had never been interested in prisons.

Two people changed that. One was David Jaffe, a student in his class, who in May 1971 had built a pretend prison for one weekend in the basement of his dormitory, with six prisoners and some guards. The other was Carlo Prescott, a man who had just come out of real prison after about sixteen years, and who Jaffe brought to the class. That summer Zimbardo and Prescott taught a course together about prisons. Prescott became the adviser to the experiment. Jaffe became its "warden", the person who runs a prison day to day. Two graduate students, Craig Haney and Curtis Banks, helped. The money came from the United States Navy's research office.

An advertisement went into the student newspaper, the *Stanford Daily*, on August 3rd, 6th and 10th: "MALE students needed to participate in psychological study of prison life. $15 per day for 1-2 weeks, beginning Aug. 14." About seventy-five men answered. Each one filled in a long form and was interviewed, and twenty-four of the calmest and healthiest were chosen. Then a coin was tossed for each one: heads a guard, tails a prisoner. The idea was that the two groups would be exactly alike to begin with, so that anything different about them afterwards must have come from the prison.

## The idea

The experiment was built to test one idea. Real prisons are cruel places. Most people assumed that was because of the people in them: that guards were bullies and prisoners were bad men. Zimbardo wanted to test the opposite idea: that the place itself does it. Put ordinary young men in a prison, give half of them uniforms and half numbers, and see whether they turn into guards and prisoners anyway. If they did, the job had made the man, and not the other way round.

There was a second idea hiding behind the first. Zimbardo and Prescott believed real prisons should be changed. A press release Zimbardo wrote on the second day of the experiment said the study would make people "aware of the prison reforms needed". In other words, before the experiment was half over, he had already written down what it would show.

## The prison

The prison was a corridor in the basement of Jordan Hall, the psychology building, about eleven metres long, with a wall built across each end. Three small laboratory rooms became cells, with bars on the doors and three beds in each. A cupboard became "the Hole", a dark space two feet square for solitary confinement, which means locking a prisoner away alone. The guards wore khaki uniforms, mirrored sunglasses and carried wooden clubs and whistles. The prisoners wore loose smocks with numbers on them, no underwear, rubber sandals, a nylon stocking on the head, and a chain locked around one ankle. The cells had no windows.

There were seventeen rules. Prisoners must be silent after lights out. They must use numbers, never names. They must call the guards "Mr. Correctional Officer". And Rule 9: "Prisoners must never refer to their condition as an 'experiment' or a 'simulation.' They are in prison until paroled." Zimbardo told people for years that the guards had made up these rules themselves. But the archive holds the rules from Jaffe's weekend prison in May, and eleven of the seventeen are the same.

On that Saturday, after Zimbardo left to arrange things with the police, Jaffe spent hours with the guards. He told them how the "count" had worked in May: line the prisoners up, make them call out their numbers over and over, and at the end make them say, "Yes we all enjoyed the count, Mr. Correctional Officer." He told them a bit of sarcasm worked well. He had a blanket full of prickly burrs that took hours to pick clean, and suggested a prisoner's blanket could be used the same way. And he told them: "A lot of this, of what happens, does depend on you. You were selected."

Jaffe later wrote down what his job had been. Zimbardo, he said, had told him "that the most difficult problem would be to get the guards to behave like guards", and so "I was given the responsibility of trying to elicit 'tough-guard' behavior." Without that, he thought, "the simulation would have appeared more like a summer camp than a prison."

On Sunday morning, August 15th, real Palo Alto police officers arrested the nine prisoners at their homes, handcuffed them, took them to the police station, blindfolded them, and handed them over. In the basement they were stripped, sprayed with a powder, dressed in the smocks, and put in the cells. A television crew filmed some of the arrests.

## The second day

Monday was the day everything turned.

In the morning the prisoners rebelled. They tore the numbers off their smocks, pushed their beds against the cell doors, and shouted at the guards. Extra guards were called in. The guards broke in, took the beds away, put the leader in the Hole, and gave the quietest cell better food and its beds back, to split the prisoners against each other. One guard quit that day, saying it was "too heavy".

That same morning, Zimbardo asked Jaffe to have a word with a guard who had stood back during the rebellion. The talk was recorded. "We really want to get you active and involved," Jaffe told him, "because the guards have to know that every guard is going to be what we call a tough guard." The guard said that if it were up to him he would do nothing and let things cool off. Jaffe kept pressing. Whether this "seems like a prison", he said, depended on the guards. The experiment mattered because it would lead to prison reform, and "so that we can get on the media and in the press with it". At the end he said: "we need you to play the part of, you know, tough guard."

The same Monday a prisoner numbered 8612 asked to leave. His name was Douglas Korpi. He was a recent graduate in his early twenties who had taken the job hoping to sit quietly and study for an exam. The staff told him no. The next day Zimbardo explained to his team, on tape, that two prisoners had asked to go and he had told them "there are only two conditions under which you can leave", a medical reason or a mental one. "I think they really believed they can't leave," he said.

That evening Korpi tore a cover off a hidden camera and was put in the Hole. Then Jaffe interviewed him. The tape has him shouting: "I want to get out! I WANT TO GET OUT NOW!" And then, more quietly: "This is an experiment. You're messing with my head." And then: "Maybe I'm inducing myself to cry so I can get out. I don't know why it is." Haney let him go home that night. He had been in the prison about thirty-six hours.

That breakdown became the most famous moment in the whole story, shown for decades as proof that the pretend prison had become real.

## What the people inside said

The experiment ran four more days. On Tuesday a rumour spread that Korpi would come back with friends to free the others, and the prisoners were moved to a cupboard upstairs for three hours. On Wednesday another prisoner was let go in tears, and a third was sent to the doctor with a rash. On Thursday a new prisoner refused to eat, and a guard told the others they could give up their blankets to get him out of the Hole; they kept their blankets. That night Zimbardo's girlfriend, Christina Maslach, also a psychologist, came to take him to dinner, saw the prisoners being marched to the toilet with bags on their heads, and told him what she thought. He decided to stop. On Friday August 20th it ended, after six days instead of fourteen.

What the people inside said is in the files too, and they all said it at the time.

Korpi came back on Thursday and spoke into a microphone. He said he could not decide "whether the prison experience had really freaked me out, or whether I induced that freaked out thing." He said that when the staff refused to let him leave, "I made up several schemes whereby I could get out. The easiest one, the one that wouldn't hurt anybody or hurt any equipment, was to just act mad, or upset, so I chose that one." He said the same thing a year later, again in the 1980s, and in 2017 to a writer: "Anybody who is a clinician would know that I was faking."

The toughest guard, a tall drama student named Dave Eshleman, whom the prisoners called "John Wayne", was interviewed on the last day. "Throughout the entire experiment I was an actor, and I was hamming it up," he said. He had put on a cruel act because "I thought it would be better for the study". Another guard said he had not been able to act tough at all: "it's almost the job makes the man, or that some people are better actors than others. I don't think that I'm a very good actor." A quieter guard said later that the guards had thought they were part of the staff, helping run the experiment, and had not known they were being studied too.

The prisoners knew where they were as well. One told a researcher, "We all know it's an experiment." And Jaffe, the warden, wrote something honest about himself: he was "startled by the ease with which I could turn off my sensitivity and concern for others for 'a good cause.'"

## What was said about it

The story travelled fast. The day after the experiment ended, a famous prisoner was killed trying to escape from San Quentin prison nearby, and three weeks later prisoners took over Attica prison in New York and many people died. Suddenly everyone wanted to know about prisons. *Life* magazine ran the story. The *Stanford Daily* ran it on its front page. And on October 25th 1971, Zimbardo gave evidence to a committee of the United States Congress in a courtroom in San Francisco. He told them the guards "were simply told that they were going to go into a situation that could be serious and have perhaps some danger" and that "They made up their own rules". He told them that putting ordinary people in a prison was "guaranteed" to make them cruel or broken.

In 1973 the scientific paper came out. It said: "Neither group received any specific training in these roles." It said the guards were given "only minimal guidelines". It said the guards' cruelty grew by itself, as a "natural" result of wearing the uniform. The experiment went into almost every psychology textbook, into a documentary film in 1992 and into a bestselling book in 2007.

Some scientists doubted the story from the beginning: most of the guards had not been cruel at all, and students who simply read a description of the experiment could guess what the professor wanted to find. But all of them were arguing from the published report, because the tapes and papers belonged to Zimbardo, and nobody asked to see them.

## The boxes

Between 2011 and 2017 Zimbardo gave his papers to Stanford's library: 182 boxes. The library put the recordings, transcripts, logs and forms online for anyone to read.

In 2017 a French researcher named Thibault Le Texier read them. He listened to the orientation tape. He read Jaffe's notes and Korpi's interview and the warden's talk with the reluctant guard. He found that less than fifteen per cent of the week had been recorded at all, that nothing was recorded on the third day, and that the researchers had filmed the dramatic moments on purpose.

Le Texier published a book in France in April 2018. In June the writer Ben Blum published his interviews with Korpi and Eshleman. Zimbardo answered that he had given the guards no "detailed instructions", that the warden's talk was mild compared with real prisons, and that Korpi's story had "changed several times over the past 47 years". He also admitted something: that he himself had turned into a prison boss during the week, and that the experiment had "no independent scientific observer". In 2019 the main journal of American psychology printed Le Texier's paper. Zimbardo died in October 2024, aged ninety-one, still defending the experiment.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**An experiment leaves two records.** One is the report that gets published, read and taught. The other is what was actually written down and recorded at the time. Here, the report said the guards were left alone to become cruel. The tape from the first morning showed the professor and the warden telling them exactly what to create. Both records were made in the same week by the same people. Only one of them travelled.

**If something can be checked, somebody has to check it.** For almost fifty years clever people argued about this experiment, and none of them opened the boxes. The argument ended when one person went and looked.

**If people know what you want, they will often give it to you.** The guards were told the aims of the study, told they had been specially chosen, told they were part of the team, and paid at the end. Then they acted like tough guards and said so in writing. That does not prove what a uniform does to a person. It shows what a briefing does.

**The job did make the man, but someone wrote the job.** This is the hard part. The old story had no villains: just good people in a bad place. The tapes do not turn anyone into a villain either. They show that the "place" was designed, hour by hour, by people who wanted it to come out a certain way, and that those people were caught up in their own creation too. Jaffe said it best about himself: he had been "a classic subject" in a study of cruelty "that was originally my own creation."

So the lesson is simple. When you hear what an experiment proved, ask to see what it recorded. The two are not always the same, and the difference can sit in a box for forty-seven years.
