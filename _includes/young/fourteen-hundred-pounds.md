## The rig

The Deepwater Horizon was a drilling rig: a floating platform, as big as a block of flats, that drilled for oil under the sea. It belonged to a company called Transocean and was hired by the oil company BP for about a million dollars a day. In the spring of 2010 it was floating about forty miles off the coast of Louisiana, in water nearly a mile deep, over a well that BP had named Macondo. The well went down more than three miles below the surface of the sea, into rock that held oil and gas under enormous pressure. About 125 people lived and worked on the rig.

Macondo had been a hard well. The job was six weeks late and $58 million over its budget. Some of the crew called it the well from hell.

Two BP men, called company men, were in charge of the well, one by day and one by night. The day man was Bob Kaluza, who had been on the rig only four days. The night man was Don Vidrine. Transocean's men ran the rig itself: Jimmy Harrell, the rig manager; Randy Ezell, the senior toolpusher (a toolpusher is the boss of the drilling floor); Wyman Wheeler, the toolpusher by day; and Jason Anderson, the toolpusher by night. Anderson had been on the rig since it was built. He was thought to be the man aboard who understood best how to keep a well under control, and he had just been given a job teaching that to others. April 20th 2010 was his last shift. The driller that night, the man who sat at the controls, was Dewey Revette.

## The question

A well is a steel pipe, called casing, that runs from the sea bed down into the rock. At the bottom, cement is pumped round the pipe to seal the oil and gas out. If the cement is bad, the oil and gas push their way into the pipe and up towards the surface. That is called a blowout.

BP had found its oil and was about to leave. The plan was to plug the well, unhook the rig, and come back months later with a cheaper rig to start pumping. While the rig was away, the cement at the bottom would be the main thing holding the oil down. So the question on April 20th was simple: had the cement sealed the well?

There were two tests. The positive test pushed pressure into the well from the top and watched whether it leaked out. That passed in the morning. The negative test did the opposite. Normally the well is full of heavy mud, a thick fluid whose weight holds the oil down. For the negative test, the crew replaced some of the mud with seawater, which is much lighter, so that the inside of the well pushed down less hard than the rock pushed in. Then they let off whatever pressure was left, until the gauge read zero, and watched. If the gauge stayed at zero and nothing flowed out for thirty minutes, the cement was good. If the pressure climbed back, the oil and gas were getting in, and the cement had failed. An expert put it to the judge years later: "This isn't baseball, the tie doesn't go to the runner, the tie goes to the failed test."

There was a third check that could have been made. A team was on the rig to run a cement log, a sort of sound scan of the cement. BP sent them home that morning to save $128,000 and a few hours. So the negative test was now the only thing checking the cement, and that cement had a lot against it: BP had used six centralisers, the clips that hold the pipe in the middle of the hole, where the cement company had asked for twenty-one, and when one engineer argued about it, another wrote, "But, who cares, it's done, end of story, [we] will probably be fine."

Nobody had written down how to do the test. BP's engineers changed the plan four times in eight days. The sheet the crew worked from said only "Conduct negative test." It ended with a good line: "If you are not sure, stop and ask." Kaluza said afterwards, "We didn't talk about what if the negative test fails."

## The test

The crew started at about five o'clock in the afternoon. There were two gauges to watch. One showed the pressure in the drill pipe, the pipe running down the middle of the well. The other showed the pressure in the kill line, a thin pipe that ran down beside it. Both went to the same place, like two straws in one glass of water, so they had to read the same.

Straight away things were odd. The first reading was about 700 pounds per square inch higher than it should have been, though nobody had worked out what it should have been. The crew opened the drill pipe to let the pressure off and could not get it to zero. More fluid came out than a sealed well could give. When they shut the pipe, the pressure climbed back to about 1,260. Then someone noticed that the fluid in the big pipe up to the rig was dropping: a rubber seal at the sea bed was leaking. Harrell had it tightened. They bled the pipe again. This time it went to zero, but fifteen barrels came out when three to five would have been right, and the pressure climbed back to about 770 and kept going. They bled it a third time, through the kill line. The gauge went to zero. Then, over the next half an hour, with everything shut, it climbed to 1,400 pounds per square inch and stopped there.

Everyone agreed afterwards what that number meant. It was almost exactly the pressure the oil and gas would push against the gauge if there were no cement at the bottom of the well at all. The well had been bled to nothing three times and had come back three times.

The little room where the driller sat was packed. The night shift was arriving, and four visiting bosses from BP and Transocean were being shown round. One of them, Daun Winslow, tapped Revette on the shoulder and asked if everything was under control. "Yes, sir," Revette said. Winslow saw there was a discussion going on and asked Harrell and Ezell to stay and help.

At about six o'clock Anderson told Ezell to go and get his dinner. Ezell said he could eat and come back. Anderson said, "Man, you ain't got to do that. I've got this. Don't worry about it. If I have any problems at all with this test I'll give you a call." "He was just like a brother," Ezell said later. He went to eat and did not come back. Wheeler, whose shift was over, was sure something was wrong, but his twelve hours were up and he went off.

Then Anderson explained the 1,400. He called it a "bladder effect". The heavy mud in the big pipe above the rubber seal, he said, was pressing down on the rubber, and the rubber was passing that pressure into the well below, where the gauge picked it up. He told the two BP men, "Bob and Don, this happens all the time." Revette said he had seen it too. A BP trainee asked about it and accepted it. Nobody argued. Nobody could check it, because there was nothing to check it against. No engineer, then or since, has found that any such thing exists, and even if it did, pressure passed through a rubber ring would have gone away when the pipe was opened and would not have come back by itself.

Vidrine, the night company man, kept talking about the 1,400 for so long that the crew found it funny. Then he said they should do the test again, on the kill line, because that was what a form sent to the government had said. They opened the kill line, bled it to zero, and watched it for thirty minutes. Nothing came out. The other gauge, the drill pipe, read 1,400 the whole time. The likeliest answer, found later, is that the kill line was blocked, so that it would have read zero whatever the well was doing.

At about five to eight Vidrine declared the test a success. Nobody disagreed. Nobody phoned the engineers ashore, who had the rig's readings on their screens and later said they would have expected a call. Nobody fetched Ezell. Nobody asked the two visiting BP bosses, who had fifty years of drilling between them. Kaluza said afterwards that if Anderson had seen the thing so many times, it must be real.

## The phone call

At two minutes past eight the crew began pumping seawater down the well to push out the rest of the mud. That was the step that left the cement as the only thing holding the oil down.

At 8:52 Vidrine phoned Mark Hafle, a BP engineer in Houston, to ask about a cement plug. He mentioned the test: zero on the kill line, he said, but still pressure on the drill pipe. Hafle told him you cannot have pressure on one and zero on the other if the test is set up properly. Vidrine said he was fully satisfied that the test had passed. The call ended at 9:02. BP's own computer model later showed that the well began to flow at 8:52, the minute the call began. For the next thirty-six minutes the crew could still have stopped the pumps and shut the well. Nobody did.

## The blowout

At 9:01 the pressure on the drill pipe, which had been falling as the light seawater went in, turned and started to rise. Nobody noticed. At 9:08 the pumps were stopped for a few minutes for a test on the fluid coming out, and the pressure rose again while they were off. Nobody noticed that either. At 9:20 Ezell, in his cabin, phoned the drill floor and asked Anderson how the negative test had gone. "It went good," Anderson said. "We bled it off. We watched it for 30 minutes and we had no flow." Did Ezell need to come up? "No, man. I've got this. Go to bed. I've got it."

At half past nine Revette and Anderson saw that the two gauges disagreed again, and stopped the pumps to look. A visitor found them calm and quiet, watching the screens. The pressure climbed with the pumps off. They tried letting it off, and it came straight back. At 9:38 gas passed the sea bed and entered the mile of pipe up to the rig. Still nobody looked over the side to see whether the well was flowing, and nobody shut it.

At about twenty to ten, mud burst out onto the drilling floor and shot to the top of the derrick, 240 feet up, and rained down on the deck and on the supply ship alongside, "kind of like a black rain," the ship's captain said. Now the crew acted. Anderson closed a seal at the sea bed. A man phoned Ezell: "We have a situation. The well is blown out. We have mud going to the crown." But the gas was already in the pipe above the seal. It poured out across the deck, the alarms went off, the engines sucked it in and raced, and at 9:49 the power failed. Five seconds later the rig exploded, and ten seconds after that it exploded again.

Anderson, Revette and another man were in the driller's room. Eleven men died that night, and none of them was found. A hundred and fifteen got off, in two lifeboats, a raft and the water. Ezell dug Wheeler out of the wreckage and would not leave him; they went down on the raft together into sea that was on fire. The survivors sat on the supply ship all night and watched the rig burn. "Those guys that were on there were our family," Ezell said.

The rig burned for a day and a half and sank on April 22nd. The well went on pouring oil into the sea for eighty-seven days, until it was capped in July. About four million barrels came out. It was the biggest oil spill in American waters.

## Afterwards

BP's own report, that September, said plainly that there was no such thing as a bladder effect. It also said nobody on the rig had asked anyone outside for help, and left out the phone call at 8:52. When the case came to court, the judge said that was false and that BP had left the call out to avoid blame. Kaluza went on insisting for days that the bladder effect was real and that the test had been "rock solid."

The investigations all said the same thing. The test had worked: it had shown three times in three hours that the well was not sealed. The crew, one report said, had "kept running tests and coming up with various explanations until they had convinced themselves their assumption was correct."

BP pleaded guilty to eleven counts of manslaughter and paid $4 billion in fines, admitting that the test "provided multiple indications that the wellbore was not secure." Transocean paid $1.4 billion. In 2014 the judge ruled that the 1,400 reading had made it impossible to call the test a pass, that BP had been grossly negligent, and that the blame was two thirds BP's and most of the rest Transocean's. In 2015 BP settled with the government for about $20 billion more. The two company men were charged with manslaughter; the charges were dropped. Vidrine admitted a minor offence and was given ten months' probation. A jury found Kaluza not guilty. Nobody went to prison for the eleven men.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A test has to say what failing looks like.** The crew knew what a pass looked like: zero for thirty minutes. Nobody had written down what a fail looked like, what the gauge should read along the way, or who had to stop the job if it went wrong. So when the gauge said 1,400, there was no rule to follow, and they talked until they found a reason to carry on. If the only answer a test can give is "pass", it is not a test.

**"This happens all the time" is not an explanation.** The bladder effect sounded like science. It was a name, from the most experienced man in the room, and nobody could check it against anything. The whole point of a test is to find out things that experience cannot tell you. Once the number had a name, nobody looked at the number any more.

**When two gauges disagree, believe the one that says danger.** The drill pipe said 1,400. The kill line said zero. One of them was wrong, and a thin pipe that reads zero is the one more likely to be blocked. Instead the crew moved the test to the gauge that gave the nicer answer. The tie goes to the failed test.

**If you are not sure, stop and ask, and the person you ask has to act.** The engineers ashore had the readings on their screens and nobody rang them. The senior toolpusher was sent to dinner. When a call was finally made, at 8:52, the engineer gave the right answer and then let himself be told that everything was fine. There were thirty-six minutes left.

**A passed test is not the end of watching.** Once the crew had told themselves the well was safe, they stopped looking. The signs of the blowout at half past nine were the same as the signs of the failed test four hours earlier: pressure rising with the pumps off, and two gauges that did not agree.

So the lesson is simple. Before you run a test, write down what a fail looks like and who has to stop. And when the instrument gives you a number you do not like, the number is the thing to believe, not the story that explains it away.
