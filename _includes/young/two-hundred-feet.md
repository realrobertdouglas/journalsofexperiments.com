## The man who wrote the warning

In 1923 a plant scientist called James Weir spent four months in the Amazon forest of Brazil, looking at rubber trees. He was a plant pathologist: someone who studies the diseases of plants. The United States government had sent him, because America bought nearly all its rubber from British and Dutch plantations in Asia, and wanted to know whether rubber could be grown in the Americas instead.

Rubber comes from a tree called *Hevea brasiliensis*. Cut its bark and a white juice called latex runs out, and latex can be made into rubber. The tree comes from the Amazon. But in the Amazon nobody grew it in fields. Men called seringueiros, rubber tappers, walked through the forest from one wild tree to the next along a path called an estrada, cutting the bark and collecting the latex in cups. The trees stood far apart, about 200 feet from each other, with hundreds of other kinds of tree in between.

Weir found out why. There is a fungus in the Amazon that lives on the young leaves of the rubber tree. It turns them black and makes them fall off. A tree can grow new leaves, but if the fungus attacks every new set of leaves, the branches die back one by one until the tree is just bare sticks. In the forest, with the trees far apart, the fungus cannot jump easily from one to the next, so the trees live. When the British and the Dutch had planted rubber trees close together in rows in the Guianas, on the north coast of South America, the fungus had run through the rows and destroyed the plantations.

In May 1926 the government printed Weir's report. It said, in plain words, that putting one kind of tree together in a pure stand makes a disease much worse, that this was well known to foresters, and that in the wild nothing like the damage seen on plantations ever happened. Another scientist on the same trip, Carl LaRue, printed a report of his own. It said the lower Amazon was not a good place for rubber planting, that there were not enough workers there, and that anyone planning a plantation must first study how to control the leaf disease.

Both reports could be bought from the government printing office in Washington. The story that follows is about a plan that did not read them.

## Henry Ford buys a forest

Henry Ford made more cars than anyone in the world, and every car needed rubber for its tyres. In the 1920s the British, who owned most of the rubber plantations in Asia, had pushed the price up. Ford did not like depending on anyone. He owned his own mines, forests and ships, and he decided to grow his own rubber.

On October 11th 1927 his son Edsel Ford announced that the company had a concession, a grant of land from the government, in the state of Pará in Brazil. It was a million hectares, about 2,500,000 acres, on the east bank of the Tapajós River, a big river that flows into the Amazon. Half of it was bought from a Brazilian businessman for 125,000 dollars; the other half the state gave for nothing. In return, the company promised to plant rubber on a certain amount of land every year, to build schools and clinics, and after twelve years to pay the state a share of its profits. The place was named Fordlandia.

Henry Ford never went there. He said he would. "I certainly intend to visit Para," he told reporters in 1928, "though I cannot now say how soon." He also said: "The plantation is bound to succeed." He would be eighty-two when the company gave the land back, and he still had not gone.

## A town from Michigan

In the summer of 1928 two ships left Ford's factory at Dearborn, Michigan, carrying a sawmill, a power plant, tractors, railway lines, an ice plant and everything needed to build a town. They sailed up the Amazon and up the Tapajós. The site was called Boa Vista, 110 miles above the mouth of the river. The company's launch took sixteen hours to get there from the nearest town, Santarém.

The first clearing was done at the wrong time of year, in the rainy season, when the felled forest would not burn. The first planting was done in the dry season by a sea captain who had never planted anything, with seeds picked up in a hurry, because the contract said 400 hectares had to be planted in the first two years. Most of those trees died and were ploughed under. In 1930 the company planted again, with better seed brought by canoe from far up the river. By 1934 it had about 8,300 acres of rubber trees in rows, planted ten to twenty feet apart as they were in Asia. In the forest they stood 200 feet apart.

Around the fields the company built an American town. There were wooden houses with shingled roofs like those on Cape Cod, concrete pavements, electric street lights, red fire hydrants, a water tower, a hospital, a school, a cinema, a golf course, and a band of twenty-five. Alcohol was banned, although Brazil had no such law. On weekends there were square dances. The workers were paid more than anyone else in the region, 35 cents a day at first and later 65 cents, and they were fed oatmeal, tinned peaches, brown bread and brown rice, chosen in Michigan for their health.

The workers were Brazilian, nearly all of them. The company wanted 5,000 men and could only find about 1,600. Most of them worked for three months and then went home with their money. LaRue's report had said in 1926 that the people of Pará did not like going up river to work. Nobody at Fordlandia had read it.

## The time clocks

Everyone at Fordlandia had a badge and punched a time clock, as at a Ford factory. In December 1930 the company opened a new concrete dining hall, hot as an oven. The skilled men, the mechanics, masons and carpenters, had always sat at tables and been served by waiters. On Monday December 22nd 1930 a foreman just arrived from Michigan replaced the waiters with a queue, like a factory canteen, and clerks stood at the door writing down badge numbers to take the cost of the meals out of the men's pay.

A mason called Manuel Caetano de Jesus took off his badge and handed it to the paymaster. The paymaster laughed. Within an hour the dining hall was wrecked. The crowd went on to the office, the power house, the sawmill and the radio station, pushed trucks into ditches and one into the river, and smashed every time clock on the estate to pieces. The Americans spent the night on a tugboat in the middle of the river. When the manager asked the men what they wanted, they told him: "We are mechanics, masons, and carpenters, not table waiters."

Soldiers arrived on Christmas Eve. The company paid every worker up to December 22nd and sacked almost all of them. Nobody in Dearborn asked what the clocks had meant. The whole week is told in the companion piece "[The time clocks](/deeper/two-hundred-feet/the-time-clocks/#young)".

## The man who wrote the warning arrives

In March 1933 the company hired a plant pathologist to look after its trees. It was James Weir, the man who had written the government report of 1926. He had been working on a plantation in Sumatra, in Asia, where rubber grows in rows without any trouble, because the fungus is not there.

Weir brought Asian methods. He wrote a book of standard practices, as a factory would. He taught the staff to graft: to cut a bud from a good tree and grow it on the roots of another, so that every tree in a row would be a copy of the best one. He went to Asia and brought back about 2,000 young grafted trees of the best kinds. He even told the manager to plant the rows closer together.

Then he changed his mind about the place. The land at Fordlandia was hilly, with steep slopes that washed away when the forest was cut, and swampy hollows where trees would not grow. In 1934 Weir told Edsel Ford to give up Fordlandia and start again on a flat plateau seventy miles down the river, called Belterra. The company had already spent about seven million dollars. It did what the expert said. In May 1934 it swapped the far part of its land for new land at Belterra, and began again, with the trees in straight rows on a grid of forty-acre squares.

## Naked stems

The trees at Fordlandia were left standing. In 1935 they were six years old, and their tops had grown together into one roof of leaves, so that the fungus could go straight from one tree to the next. That year the disease ran through the whole estate. Weir wrote to Dearborn that nearly every branch now ended in what he called "naked stems", each new shoot smaller than the last. The disease, he said, could not be fought at Fordlandia. The Asian trees he had brought were no help: nobody in Asia had ever needed a tree that could resist a fungus Asia did not have.

The government's own report of 1926 had said exactly this would happen. Its author was now standing in the rows, watching it happen.

Belterra seemed to escape, for four or five years. Then its trees grew old enough to do what rubber trees do: drop all their leaves once a year and grow a new set all at once. In a plantation every tree does this in the same two weeks, so for a fortnight the whole estate is nothing but soft new leaves, which is what the fungus eats. From about 1938 the disease swept Belterra too, three years in a row. Then came the caterpillars. In 1942 the manager saw "the greatest swarm of caterpillars that has ever been seen in this area". They ate the leaves, the trees grew new ones, and the fungus took those.

There was one thing left to try, and it was the thing Weir's report had suggested in 1926. A few scattered trees at Fordlandia had kept their leaves through the epidemic. They were resistant. Weir had cut buds from them and grafted them onto the tops of other trees, so that a tree had a good trunk from Asia and a resistant head from the Amazon. Now the company did this to the whole of Belterra. More than two million trees were sawn off at seven feet and given new heads. Six hundred men did it, and by the end of 1945 it was finished. Then the trees needed two or three years to grow new crowns before they could be tapped.

## What happened

By 1941 the company had spent about nine million dollars and was shipping only a few tons of latex a month to Michigan, at best. It needed tens of thousands of tons a year. Edsel Ford died in 1943. His son, Henry Ford II, became president of the company in September 1945, and on November 5th 1945 he handed Fordlandia and Belterra to the Government of Brazil for 244,200 dollars, which was about what the company owed its workers in leaving pay. A Brazilian law of December 24th 1945 set the price at five million cruzeiros and the handover for New Year's Eve. The company had spent about twenty million dollars. Henry Ford died in 1947 without having seen the place.

The Brazilians kept Belterra going. With its two million re-headed trees it was tapped from 1948. The method of giving a tree three parts, roots, a high-yielding trunk and a disease-resistant top, became the way rubber was planted all over Latin America. The Brazilian scientist who took over called Fordlandia an "utter failure" caused by "blank ignorance", and also "the true cradle" of that method. Both things were true.

Almost all the world's rubber is still grown in Asia, in rows, because the fungus has never got there. What the fungus is and how it works is told in the companion piece "[The leaf](/deeper/two-hundred-feet/the-leaf/#young)". Fordlandia is still there on its hills. People live in the old houses. The water tower still stands, and the sawmill with its windows broken.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Read what has already been written down.** The answer to the whole experiment was printed by the government in 1926, with photographs of the diseased leaves, and it cost about as much as a stamp. The company spent twenty million dollars and seventeen years finding out the same thing. Before you start something big, look for the people who tried it before you, and read what they said.

**Local knowledge is knowledge.** The rubber tappers of the Amazon kept their trees 200 feet apart. They did not write a report about it. They just knew, from walking their paths every morning, that rubber trees grow alone. The men from Michigan did not think of that as information, because it did not come from an expert. It was the most important fact in the whole valley.

**A method that works in one place can fail in another, for one reason.** Everything the company did was done well somewhere else. Rows of rubber trees worked in Asia. Time clocks and canteens worked in Michigan. The one thing Asia had that the Amazon did not was an ocean between the tree and its fungus. The company copied the method without asking what made it work.

**If your plan can only measure itself, it will tell you it is succeeding.** The company counted hectares cleared, trees planted, houses built and hours punched, and all those numbers went up. The only instrument that mattered was a leaf, and the leaf said no, every season, for years.

**Listen to the people who break the clocks.** The workers who smashed the time clocks were saying something true: that a rule made in Michigan did not fit the Tapajós. The company heard a riot and sacked them. The trees were saying the same thing, and the company could not sack the trees.

So the lesson is simple. Before you build something, find out what the place already knows, from the people who live there and from the books that have already been written, and measure the thing you are actually trying to grow, not the things you find easy to count.
