## The aeroplane

In 1952 a British company called de Havilland built the first jet airliner in the world. It was called the Comet. Before the Comet, airliners had propellers and flew at about 20,000 feet. The Comet had four jet engines hidden in its wings and flew at 35,000 to 40,000 feet, twice as high, where the air is thin and smooth and a jet can go fast without burning too much fuel.

Up there the air is far too thin to breathe. So the Comet's cabin, the tube where the passengers sat, was sealed and pumped full of air, like a long metal balloon. Inside, the air was kept at the pressure you would feel at 8,000 feet. Outside, at 40,000 feet, there was almost nothing. The difference pushed outwards on every square inch of the cabin's thin aluminium skin with a force of about eight and a quarter pounds. That was half as much again as any airliner before it.

Every time the Comet climbed, the cabin was pumped up. Every time it came down, the cabin was let down. Pumped up, let down, once a flight, for the whole life of the aeroplane.

The first paying passengers flew on May 2nd 1952, from London to Johannesburg in South Africa, in just under a day.

## The test before it flew

The people who designed the Comet knew the cabin was the dangerous part. Ronald Bishop, the chief designer, said later that it was "one of the biggest problems that we had to face". If the cabin burst at 40,000 feet, everyone inside would die.

So they tested it, more than anyone had tested a cabin before. They built two lengths of cabin, one twenty-six feet long from the nose back, with windows, hatches and a door in it. They filled it with water and pumped it up to twice the working pressure, 16½ pounds on every square inch, and it held. They pumped it up and down 2,000 times to check it did not leak. The rules of the day said a cabin should stand twice its working pressure. The Comet was designed for two and a half times.

Behind all this was a belief that almost everyone in aviation shared. If a cabin could stand twice its working pressure once, without damage, then it would stand its working pressure once a flight for ten thousand flights. The makers thought the cabin was good for ten years.

There is a thing called metal fatigue. If you bend a paper clip back and forth, it does not break the first time, or the tenth. It breaks on the fortieth, under a force it could easily bear when it was new. Metal that is loaded and unloaded over and over can break the same way. In 1952 engineers were just beginning to worry about fatigue in airliners, but they worried about the wings, which are bent up and down by every gust of wind, thousands of times a flight. A cabin was only pumped up once a flight. Once a flight seemed too seldom to matter.

In 1953 new rules were being written that would ask makers to pump a cabin up and down 15,000 times. So in July 1953 de Havilland went back to their front section of cabin, the same one that had already been pumped to twice the working pressure, and pumped it up and down at the working pressure about 16,000 more times. By September it had stood 18,000 pumps in all. Then the skin cracked at the corner of a window. But 18,000 was a huge number. Nobody had flown a Comet more than about 800 times. By the makers' sums, 18,000 pumps was worth 54,000 hours of flying. The cabin was safe "with an ample margin".

That number, eighteen thousand, is the number this story is about.

## Yoke Peter

On Sunday January 10th 1954 a Comet called G-ALYP took off from Rome for London. Pilots said the letters out loud as words, so the aeroplane was "Yoke Peter". It had twenty-nine passengers and six crew. At 9.50 in the morning it reported that it was over a radio beacon at Orbetello, climbing through 26,500 feet. A slower BOAC aeroplane on the same route had asked its captain how high the clouds went. At about 9.51 the other captain heard: "George How Jig from George Yoke Peter. Did you get my—" and then nothing.

On the island of Elba a sailor heard "a heavy roaring noise like thunder" and saw "a globe of fire rotating as it came down into the sea". A farmer saw two pieces of aeroplane fall, the smaller one on fire. Everyone on board died. Yoke Peter had flown 3,681 hours and had been pumped up 1,290 times.

Nobody knew why. The airline stopped its Comets flying and took them to pieces at London Airport to look. A committee of the airline, the makers and the Air Registration Board, which is the body that says whether an aeroplane is fit to fly, made a list of six things that might have gone wrong: the controls, the structure, the engines, fire, a fault in the wing, and the cabin bursting. They thought fire the most likely, and they changed many things on the aeroplane to guard against fire. They did not think the cabin could have failed from fatigue. The report of the inquiry said why: "They still regarded the 18,000 repeated loadings as removing any doubt about the fatigue life of the cabin."

## The peas in the field

Meanwhile the Royal Navy was looking for the aeroplane. It lay in 500 feet of water off Elba. Ships with grabs and an underwater television camera, the first ever used for this, searched the sea bed. The camera found the first piece on February 12th. The admiral in charge, Earl Mountbatten, said it was "like searching for a handful of peas in a one-acre field from a helicopter on a misty day".

In March the Minister asked his advisers, the Air Safety Board, whether the Comets should fly again. The Board wrote that "no cause has yet been found that would satisfactorily account for the Elba disaster", but that "everything humanly possible has been done" and the Comets should go back into service once the changes were made. The Minister agreed. On March 23rd 1954 the Comets started flying passengers again. The front of Yoke Peter's cabin was still on the sea bed. It came up on April 3rd.

On Thursday April 8th a Comet called Yoke Yoke, flying for South African Airways, took off from Rome for Cairo in the evening with fourteen passengers and seven crew. It reported near Naples, climbing to 35,000 feet, and sent one more message, and was not heard again. Bodies were found the next day in water more than half a mile deep, too deep for anyone to bring the aeroplane up. Yoke Yoke had flown 2,704 hours and had been pumped up about 900 times. It had been back in service for sixteen days.

That night the Comets stopped flying, and this time the Government took away their certificate of airworthiness, the paper that says an aeroplane may carry passengers. The Air Registration Board wrote that there might be "some factor influencing the airworthiness of Comet aircraft which the most diligent investigations have failed to discover."

## The tank

The Government handed the whole problem to the Royal Aircraft Establishment at Farnborough, the country's research station for aircraft, and its director, Arnold Hall. Hall's scientists noticed that both aeroplanes had broken up at about the same height, at about the same time after take-off, near the top of their climb, which is when the cabin is being pumped hardest. On April 18th 1954 Hall decided to pump a whole Comet up and down until something happened.

You cannot do that with air. A cabin full of air bursts like a bomb and destroys the evidence, and a cabin simply filled with water is squashed by the weight in a way no flight ever does. So Farnborough built a tank big enough to hold a Comet with its wings sticking out through the sides, filled the tank and the cabin with water together, and pumped water into the cabin from the tank outside. Each pump stood for one flight. Hydraulic rams bent the wings up and down at the same time, to stand for the gusts. The aeroplane was a Comet called Yoke Uncle, which had already made 1,230 real flights for the airline.

The tests began in early June. On June 24th 1954, after 1,830 pumps in the tank on top of the 1,230 real flights, 3,060 in all, the cabin split open. The split began at the corner of a hatch on the left side, near the front. Tiny green algae in the water had stained the faces of the crack, which proved the crack had been growing for several pumps before it let go. It was fatigue.

Bishop, the designer, said later: "it was a very great surprise to me personally when the fuselage blew up in the tank at 9,000 hr. We thought we had a very much longer life than that."

## The piece from the roof

Now Farnborough had a prediction. If Yoke Peter's cabin had burst the same way, the wreckage should show it. The scientists had been putting Yoke Peter back together on a wooden frame, thousands of pieces, like a jigsaw. Everything said the cabin had burst somewhere over the wing while the aeroplane was flying normally. But the hatch that had failed in the tank was fine on the wreckage. The piece that mattered was missing.

So they worked out where it would have fallen, blown clear and further back towards Rome, and the search moved there, where the water was too deep for grabs and fishing boats had to trawl. On August 12th 1954 an Italian fishing boat brought up a piece of the cabin roof with two small windows in it, for the radio aerials. Every crack in the roof ran away from one corner of one of those windows, and none ran into it. At that corner, at the edge of a hole where a bolt went through, the experts found the fine, smooth grain of a fatigue crack.

Then they measured something nobody had measured before. They fixed strain gauges, which are tiny instruments that read how hard metal is being stretched, right at the corners of Yoke Uncle's windows. The makers had believed the stress there was about half of what the metal could bear. The gauges said it was 70 per cent. The corners of the windows were working much harder than anyone had thought.

## Twice two is four

That raised a puzzle. If the corners were so highly stressed, how had the makers' cabin section stood twice the working pressure without breaking, and then 18,000 pumps on top? By the new numbers, twice the working pressure should have torn it apart.

Hall gave the answer at the public inquiry that autumn. "Within the yield point of the material twice two is four," he said; "above the yield point it is not." Metal is elastic up to a point: stretch it and it springs back. Push past that point and it stretches and stays stretched, like a paper clip you have opened out. When the test section was pumped to twice the working pressure in 1948 and 1949, the metal at the corners of its windows had been pushed past that point. It had stretched a tiny bit, too little to see, and stayed stretched. And a corner that has stretched and settled no longer carries its load the same way. The worst of the stress had been smoothed out.

So the section that was pumped 18,000 times in 1953 was not the same as a new cabin. It had been toughened at exactly the corners that were in danger, by the earlier test that was meant to prove it strong. A real Comet, like Yoke Uncle, had never been above one and a third times the working pressure. Its corners had never been eased. Hall was asked whether the makers had been right to trust the 18,000. "I cannot entirely agree with that," he said. If a specimen had been pre-loaded like that, "I do not know of any basis on which one could then predict that the fatigue life of an unpre-loaded specimen would be X at all."

The inquiry's report put it in one sentence: "The pre-loaded cabin is therefore physically different from a new one."

## The inquiry

The public inquiry sat in London for twenty-two days in October and November 1954, under a judge, Lord Cohen. Sixty-eight witnesses spoke. Walter Tye, the Board's chief engineer, was the one expert who was not quite sure the tiny fracture proved fatigue, though he could think of nothing else. The lawyer for de Havilland said: "We believed the static-loading test would cover fatigue. We know now that that was wrong, but not only did we believe that, the Royal Aircraft Establishment believed it, the Air Registration Board believed it, the International Civil Aviation Organisation believed it and the Civil Aeronautics Authority of America believed it." The companion pieces, "[The Crown's case](/deeper/eighteen-thousand/the-crowns-case/#young)" and "[The makers' case](/deeper/eighteen-thousand/the-makers-case/#young)", tell what each of them said.

The report came out in February 1955. The cause of the Elba accident was "the structural failure of the pressure cabin brought about by fatigue". Naples was probably the same, though without wreckage nobody could be certain. Nobody was blamed. The makers had done what everyone then thought was right, and more. On the decision to trust the 18,000: "In the then state of knowledge I think this conclusion was reasonable."

## What changed

The Comet 1s never carried passengers again. New Comets were built with thicker skin and round windows instead of square ones. And a new rule was made: every new type of pressurised airliner would have to be put in a tank and pumped up and down until it failed, and makers would have to build two aeroplanes for testing, one to be broken by a single huge load and one to be broken slowly by thousands of small ones. Within weeks other makers in Britain and Holland said they would put their own airliners in tanks too.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A test only tells you about the thing you tested.** The makers tested a piece of cabin that had already been pumped to twice the working pressure. That pumping changed the metal at the corners of the windows, in a way nobody could see, and made it stronger against fatigue. So the 18,000 was a true number about that piece of cabin, and a false number about the aeroplanes that flew. Before you trust a test, ask whether the thing in the test has had the same life as the thing in the real world.

**When everyone agrees, it is still a guess until it is measured.** Everyone believed that if a cabin could stand twice its pressure once, it could stand its pressure ten thousand times. The makers believed it, the research station believed it, the Board believed it, the Americans believed it. It was wrong. Nobody had measured the stress at the corners of the windows until Farnborough put gauges there in 1954. A belief that lots of clever people share is still only a belief.

**"Everything possible" is only everything you can think of.** After Elba, the people in charge changed many things on the Comet and said everything humanly possible had been done. But the cause was still unknown, and the piece of wreckage that would have explained it was still on the bottom of the sea. Changing everything you can think of does not fix the thing you have not thought of.

**The aeroplane is the specimen.** The thing that finally gave the right answer was a whole aeroplane, Yoke Uncle, treated exactly like a working aeroplane, pumped up and down until it broke. Not a section, not a panel, not a calculation. After the Comet, the rule became: break a whole one first.

So the lesson is simple. A number from a test is only as good as the match between the thing tested and the thing used. Eighteen thousand was a real result. It just belonged to a different cabin.
