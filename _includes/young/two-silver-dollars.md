## The doctor and the scale

Duncan MacDougall was a doctor in Haverhill, a town in Massachusetts that made shoes, about thirty miles north of Boston. He was born in 1866 and trained at the Boston University School of Medicine. He saw patients in his house on Main Street in the morning, in the afternoon and again at night, like most doctors then. A newspaper said, when he died, that he also wrote poems for magazines.

In the winter of 1896 he had an idea. Most people believed that something of a person goes on living after the body dies. MacDougall thought that if that something was real, it had to take up room, like everything else that is real. And if it took up room, perhaps it had weight. If it had weight, then a dying person should get lighter at the moment of death, by exactly the weight of whatever left. Nobody had ever tried to measure it.

To measure it he needed a very good scale. He used a Fairbanks platform scale: a big weighing machine with a flat platform near the floor, a tall post, and a beam at the top with a small sliding weight along it. Shops used them to weigh sacks and bales. His was the kind made for weighing silk, which could take 300 pounds and could notice a change of about a fifth of an ounce, which is about the weight of a letter. On the platform he built a light wooden frame, and on the frame he put a bed.

## What he was looking for

MacDougall wrote down what he expected before he began. The soul, he said, must be a "space-occupying body". It could not be made of the "ether", the invisible stuff that scientists then thought filled all of space, because the ether was supposed to be one continuous thing, and a person is separate from everything else. So the soul had to be ordinary matter of some kind, and ordinary matter can be weighed.

He also wrote down what it would mean if the scale showed nothing. It would mean nothing, he said. The soul might simply be too light to weigh, or made of ether after all. So if the beam moved, that proved the soul had weight; and if it did not move, that proved nothing. Keep that in mind. It is the most important thing about the whole experiment, and MacDougall thought it was just being fair.

## The first patient

He waited four years for the right patient. He wanted someone dying of tuberculosis, which people then called consumption. A person dying of consumption dies slowly, after months of wasting away, with hardly any movement at the end, and the doctors can tell hours ahead that death is coming. That mattered, because any movement would shake the beam.

On April 10th 1901, at half past five in the evening, a man dying of consumption was lifted onto the bed on the scale. MacDougall and another doctor, named Sproull, watched the beam. For three hours and forty minutes the man slowly lost weight, about an ounce an hour, which is what happens to a dying body as moisture leaves it in the breath and through the skin. Every ten or fifteen minutes MacDougall slid the little weight back along the beam so that the end of the beam stayed pressed up against its top stop. That way, if the bed suddenly got lighter, the beam would drop, and he would see it.

At ten past nine the man died. MacDougall wrote: "exactly simultaneously with the last movement of the respiratory muscles and coincident with the last movement of the facial muscles the beam end dropped to the lower limiting bar and remained there without rebound as though a weight had been lifted off the bed."

Then he did a sensible thing. He put two silver dollars on the bed to bring the beam back up to balance, and then he weighed the two coins. "They were found together to weigh three-fourths of an ounce," he wrote.

Three-quarters of an ounce is about 21 grams. That is the number that everyone remembers. But look at the coins. A silver dollar in 1901 weighed a fixed amount by law, and two of them together weighed almost two ounces, two and a half times what MacDougall said his coins weighed. He never explained that, and when he published the experiment six years later he left the coins out. Nobody seems to have noticed in all the years since.

## The other five

MacDougall wrote everything down in letters to Richard Hodgson, a man in Boston who investigated ghosts and mediums for a living. "Have I really weighed the soul substance?" MacDougall asked him. "I think so, and I mean to verify and re-verify and re-re-verify, if I live long enough." He asked Hodgson to keep it secret until he had more cases.

The next five patients were nothing like the first.

The second man died so slowly that the doctors could not tell when he was dead. He stopped breathing at ten past four in the morning. For fifteen minutes his eyelids and lips twitched and the beam did not move. Then the beam began to sink, slowly, over another fifteen minutes. In the end the bed had lost about an ounce and a half, twice as much as the first man, but spread over half an hour, and nobody could say at what moment he had died.

The third man lost half an ounce when he died and then another whole ounce a few minutes later. In between, a doctor had leaned on the bed with a stethoscope to listen to the heart, and the scale had been jolted.

The fourth patient was a woman dying of diabetes. The scale had not been set up properly, and people at the hospital who did not like the experiment kept interfering. MacDougall threw that test out.

The fifth man's beam dropped suddenly, by about three-eighths of an ounce, half as much as the first. But when MacDougall put weights on to bring the beam back and then took them off again, the beam would not settle for a quarter of an hour.

The sixth man died within five minutes of being put on the bed, while MacDougall was still adjusting the beam. He wrote to Hodgson that there had been no loss of weight. Five years later he said there had been more than two ounces. In the published paper it was an ounce and a half, which might have been the sliding weight slipping. He threw that test out too.

So out of six patients, two were thrown out by MacDougall himself, and of the other four, only the first gave a clean, sudden drop at a moment everyone agreed was death.

## The fifteen dogs

MacDougall also tested dogs: twelve by 1902, fifteen by 1907. He put each dog on a scale that could notice a sixteenth of an ounce, and none of them lost any weight when it died. He took this to mean that dogs have no soul and people do.

But there was a problem, and he wrote it down himself. The dogs were healthy, and a healthy dog will not lie still on a scale. So he gave them two drugs to keep them quiet while they died. He said the dog tests were "vitiated", which means spoiled, by the drugs. He had wanted dogs that were dying slowly of an illness, like the people, and he could not get any. So the one comparison he had was a spoiled one, and he reported it as a discovery anyway.

## The newspapers

MacDougall did not publish anything for five years. He wanted more cases, and he was afraid that he had found what he called "a mare's nest", which means a great discovery that turns out to be nothing. Hodgson died in 1905. Then, in March 1907, somebody told a newspaper.

On March 10th 1907 a Boston paper printed the story, and on March 11th the *New York Times* printed it under the headline "SOUL HAS WEIGHT, PHYSICIAN THINKS". Within ten days it was in small-town papers as far away as South Carolina. The newspaper version said that MacDougall was the head of a "Research Society" with four other doctors, which was not true: there was only Dr. Sproull. It said the scales could notice a tenth of an ounce, when they could notice two-tenths. It said the first man had lost "a full ounce", when he had lost three-quarters. It said that one patient, "a phlegmatic man slow of thought and action", had kept his soul inside him for a full minute after death before it "came to the consciousness of its freedom". MacDougall never wrote anything like that. And it said that all the other cases, including the woman's, had shown a loss, when the woman's had been thrown out.

MacDougall was upset. "The cat is out of the bag," he wrote, "by the least desirable method—newspaper publication". He had just started working with a new hospital and had hoped to begin weighing patients again. The newspaper story, he said, "ends that hope". He never weighed anyone again.

## The argument

He published his paper in April 1907, in a medical magazine called *American Medicine*. He was careful in it. He said that "a large number of experiments would require to be made before the matter can be proved", and he admitted that two of his six cases were no good.

Other doctors wrote in. One, Augustus Clarke, said that a dying body gets hotter at the end, and a hotter body sweats, and the sweat evaporating could explain the missing three-quarters of an ounce. MacDougall answered that his patients had died cold and clammy under blankets, and that anyway "a loss of weight implies a loss of substance". Another doctor, Austin O'Malley, counted the cases and said only one was worth anything. He also pointed out that nobody can say exactly when a person dies. And he said that if a man's sweetheart leaves him and he gets thin from grief, that does not mean the sweetheart weighed what he lost. It was a joke, but it was also the best answer anyone gave.

Clarke wrote again and asked for the things that were missing from the paper: how much each patient weighed, how warm the room was, what month it was, whether the air was damp. MacDougall said he had the patients' weights at home but had not thought them worth printing, because the experiment was about the rate of loss, not the weight. He said that if anyone thought temperature mattered, they should repeat the experiment with a thermometer and find out. Nobody did. The companion piece "[The argument about heat](/deeper/two-silver-dollars/the-argument-about-heat/#young)" tells the whole argument.

## What happened to the number

MacDougall died at home in Haverhill on October 15th 1920, aged fifty-four. The *New York Times* headed its notice "He Weighed Human Soul".

The three-quarters of an ounce turned into 21 grams, and the 21 grams took on a life of its own. In 2003 there was a film called *21 Grams*. People still say that the soul weighs 21 grams, and most of them do not know where the number came from. Almost nobody mentions the other five patients, or the fifteen dogs, or the two silver dollars.

In 2010 a Japanese researcher named Masayoshi Ishida went through all the objections with modern physics. He found that MacDougall's scale really was good enough to see 21 grams, that sweat and draughts could not explain a sudden drop, and that Clarke was wrong about dying bodies getting hotter. But he also found that nothing in MacDougall's paper lets anyone check his reading of the beam, and that "four cases are certainly small".

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**One clean result is not the data.** MacDougall had six patients. One gave a neat, sudden drop. The others gave slow sinks, double losses, a jolted scale and two spoiled tests. The one neat result is the only one anyone remembers. When you do an experiment, the result is all of it, including the messy parts. If you only keep the tidy one, you are not reporting the experiment. You are telling a story.

**A test that cannot fail cannot succeed either.** MacDougall said before he started that if the scale moved, the soul had weight, and if it did not move, that proved nothing. That sounds fair. But a test where one answer counts and the other does not is not really a test. It can only ever agree with you.

**Write down everything, not just the thing you are looking for.** MacDougall recorded the beam, because the beam was what his idea predicted. He did not record the patients' weights, the temperature, the month or the room, because his idea did not need them. So when other people asked questions, there was nothing to check. And the one time he checked his own reading, with two silver dollars, the coins did not agree with it, and he left them out.

**A spoiled control is not a control.** The fifteen dogs were supposed to show what happens when a soul is not there. But the dogs were drugged, and MacDougall said himself that the drugs spoiled the test. A comparison that you know is broken cannot tell you anything, even when it gives the answer you wanted.

**The first story the public hears is the one it keeps.** The newspapers printed a version of the experiment that MacDougall never wrote, five weeks before his real paper came out. The real paper was read by doctors. The newspaper version was read by everyone, and it is still the version people tell.

So the lesson is simple. Before you weigh anything, decide what would prove you wrong, and write that down too. Then keep every reading, including the ones that spoil the story.
