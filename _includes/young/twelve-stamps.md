## The town

Wörgl is a small town in the Austrian Alps, in the valley of the river Inn, in the province called the Tyrol. It grew up around a railway junction, where the line from Innsbruck to Vienna crosses the line from Italy to Munich. By 1910 it had more than four thousand people, a paper-pulp factory, a cement works, a brickworks, sawmills and a locomotive depot.

In 1929 the world's economy crashed, and over the next three years almost everything in Wörgl closed. The railway changed to electric trains and shut the depot. The cement works closed in 1931 and never opened again. The pulp factory, which had employed about four hundred people, kept four men to watch the machines. By the summer of 1932 about four hundred people in the town had no work, and about 1,500 in the villages around it. Two hundred of the town's unemployed had used up their insurance and were living on what the town could give them, which was almost nothing. The town owed a bank in Innsbruck 1,300,000 schillings (the schilling was Austria's money) and could not even pay the interest. People owed the town 118,000 schillings in unpaid taxes, and nobody could pay. In the first six months of 1932 the town collected 3,000 schillings in taxes. The streets were so bad that someone had painted a rhyme on a wall about them.

## The mayor

The mayor was Michael Unterguggenberger. He was born on August 15th 1884 in a village up a side valley, left school at twelve to work in a sawmill, trained as a mechanic, walked across half of Europe as a journeyman, and in 1905 came home to drive locomotives for the railway at Wörgl. He was a trade unionist and a Social Democrat. He played several instruments and led the workers' youth band. He sat on the town council from 1919, and in December 1931, when the council was split exactly in half between the two political sides, the choice of mayor was decided by drawing lots. It fell to him.

He had read a book by a man called Silvio Gesell. Gesell had been a businessman in Argentina and had an idea about money. Everything money buys gets worse with time: grain rots, iron rusts, cloth wears out. Money does not. So when times are bad, people with money hold on to it and wait for prices to fall, and the waiting is what keeps times bad. Gesell said that money should be made to go bad too, a little each month, so that nobody would want to keep it and everybody would spend it. He called it free money. In 1931 a coal mine in Bavaria had tried it, paying the miners in a private money with stamps on it, and the mayor went to see.

## The idea

On July 5th 1932 the mayor read a paper to the town's welfare committee. "Slow circulation of money is the main cause of the present economic paralysis," it began. Money was seeping away into the hands of a few people who were not spending it. "Every damming-up of money causes a damming-up of goods and unemployment." He did not claim Wörgl could save the world. "Since the world cannot be freed from here, we want at least to give a sign."

The sign was this. The town would print its own notes, called labour certificates, worth 1, 5 and 10 schillings. Every note had twelve small boxes on it, one for each month. At the start of every month, whoever was holding the note had to buy a stamp worth one per cent of the note's value and stick it in that month's box. A 1 schilling note needed a one-groschen stamp every month (there are a hundred groschen in a schilling). If the stamp was missing, the note was worth that much less. So nobody wanted to be holding a note when the month ended. They spent it.

There were two things to find out. The first was whether money that cost something to hold really would move faster than ordinary money. That could be tested by counting how often the notes came back. The second was whether faster money would give the town's unemployed work and bring in the unpaid taxes. That was much harder to test, because nobody could know what would have happened in Wörgl without the notes.

There was also a question nobody on the committee wrote down. Austria's central bank, the National Bank, had the only right to print money in Austria, under Article 122 of its rules. Before the notes were even delivered from the printer, the bank wrote to object. The mayor replied that the notes were not money. They were labour certificates.

## What they built

The committee agreed the rules on July 5th 1932 and the council agreed them on July 8th. Every vote was unanimous, which in Austria in 1932 almost never happened. Taking part was voluntary. The town printed 2,000 notes of each value, 32,000 schillings in all: the 1 schilling yellow, the 5 blue, the 10 red. Each one was pressed with the town's seal.

The clever part was the cover. The town did not give the notes away. It bought them from the committee with real schillings, and the committee put those schillings in the town's savings bank. So every note out in the town was matched by a real schilling in the bank, and anyone who wanted could change a note back into real money at the bank, paying 2 per cent for the privilege. The money from the stamps, from the 2 per cent, and from the interest the bank earned on the deposit all went to help the poor.

The town paid its own staff half their wages in the notes, the mayor included, and later three-quarters. The men on the relief works were paid entirely in notes. The first 1,000 schillings of notes went out as wages on July 31st 1932. By the middle of September the town had taken 8,500 schillings of notes from the committee, and it never needed more. The most that was ever out at one time was 7,443 schillings. On average, about 5,500 schillings of notes were out, which is about one and a half schillings for every person in the town. The rest of the 32,000 stayed in the safe.

The works had started three weeks before the first note. Men dug sewers and rebuilt and asphalted the main streets, then built a ski jump, a soup kitchen, a wash-house, new paths in the forest, and the road up from the station with new lamps. In 1933 they built a bridge of reinforced concrete and put a plaque on it saying that it was built with free money. All the wages were paid in notes. The town spent about 100,000 schillings on the works in all. Some of that money did not come from the notes: the province gave a grant of 12,000 schillings for materials, and a national fund paid 3.50 schillings a day towards each man's wages.

## What it looked like

The notes came back fast. When the first 1,000 schillings went out, within three days 5,100 schillings of unpaid taxes had been paid in notes. A clerk ran to the mayor to say the notes must have been forged, because there were only 1,000 of them. They had not been forged. The same note had come back, gone out again as wages, and come back again. The mayor told Irving Fisher, a famous economist at Yale in America, that the first wages paid had come back to the town twenty times in a month. An official from Innsbruck who went through the books later worked out that the notes passed through the town treasury about twice a week.

A Swiss writer called Alex von Muralt came in April 1933 and went from shop to shop. Every shop took the notes. The shopkeepers did not love them, because they lost 1 per cent if they were still holding one at the end of the month, but most of them said business was a little better, or at least not as bad as they had feared. One grocer complained that the wholesaler who supplied him would only take half of each bill in notes. The wholesaler admitted it, and said he supported the scheme all the same. Prices had not gone up. In nine months, people had changed 34,500 schillings of notes back into real money at the bank.

Another Swiss visitor went round with a list of twelve questions. The police inspector told him that at a burglary, the thief had taken only the Austrian money and thrown the Wörgl money away, "so it must have been a stranger; a local would have taken it." The cinema owner said a quarter of his takings were now in notes, from people who would not otherwise have come. Ten of the twelve people had no complaints. Three said the stamps ought to stick better. One said the paper was a bit thin. The head of the shopkeepers' association said that if the government banned the notes, he would "rather be locked up than stop".

Letters came from all over the world, and the mayor, who read neither French nor English, had to find people to translate them. Economists came to look. Fisher wrote a book that called Wörgl the best example in the world and told American towns to copy it; twenty-two of them did, and a bill was put to the United States Congress for a billion dollars of stamped money. A French magazine, *L'Illustration*, sent a young engineer named Claude Bourdet in August 1933. "The streets, so sadly famous, now look like motorways," he wrote. The next village, Kirchbichl, printed notes of its own on January 1st 1933, and the two towns took each other's. Other villages were waiting to see what the government would do.

## The ban

The National Bank never stopped objecting. In January 1933 the government told the town to stop issuing the notes. The town appealed, and while the appeal went up from court to court, the notes kept circulating. In June 1933 the mayor spoke to a meeting of about 170 mayors who wanted to copy Wörgl. But the appeal did not stop the order, and in September 1933, after thirteen and a half months, the town took the notes out of circulation. It took eight days. Nobody lost any money, because every note was matched by a schilling in the bank. In October the local newspaper printed the town's closing account of the whole experiment: a profit of 980 schillings, against 950 schillings of costs for printing and the lawyer.

On November 18th 1933 the Administrative Court in Vienna heard the case. So many people wanted to watch that, for the first time in its history, the court had to limit who could come in. The court decided that the certificates had done the job of coins or banknotes, and that only the National Bank was allowed to issue those. The experiment was over.

The mayor wrote one last report. "Here are the facts," he began. The notes had done everything money does, and faster. Nobody had complained about the stamps. The two kinds of money had lived side by side. Taxes that had been owed for years had been paid: of 83,000 schillings owed at the end of 1931, 77,327 had come in during 1932. Unemployment in the town had fallen by about a quarter while unemployment in the rest of Austria went up. The winter's works, he wrote, could not now be paid for, and "the workers will have their tools struck out of their hands."

In February 1934 there was a short civil war in Austria, the Social Democrats were banned, and the mayor was removed from office. He died on December 19th 1936, aged fifty-two.

## The number that grew

One number from Wörgl is repeated everywhere: that the notes changed hands 416 times a year, many times faster than ordinary money. Nobody ever counted that. The mayor guessed twenty times a month. The Innsbruck official counted the notes coming back to the treasury twice a week. In 1951 a Swiss writer named Fritz Schwarz took the official's twice a week, guessed that each note also changed hands three more times in between each visit, got eight a week, multiplied by fifty-two weeks, and wrote down 416. He said it "can be right". That guess has been repeated ever since as if someone had measured it.

Nobody ever measured the other half of the comparison either: how fast ordinary schillings were moving in Wörgl at the same time. Fisher himself admitted he did not know. So the one thing the experiment was built to test was never properly counted.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**If you build an experiment to measure something, measure it.** The whole idea was that stamped money would move faster than ordinary money. The town could have counted every note that came back and every stamp it sold, and compared that with ordinary money. It counted only what arrived at the treasury, and nobody counted the ordinary schillings at all. So the most famous number from Wörgl, 416, was never measured. It was a guess built on another guess.

**A good result in a bad year is hard to read.** Unemployment in Wörgl fell while unemployment in Austria rose, and the streets got asphalted. But the wages were partly paid by a national fund, the materials by a grant from the province, and the works had started before the first note was printed. Was it the fast money, or was it the grant? Nobody can say, because there was no second town, with the same grant and no notes, to compare it with.

**Different people read different instruments.** The mayor read the tax receipts and saw proof. A professor added up the town's total income and called it bluff. An official read the unpaid taxes and said the notes were just a clever way to collect them. A court read Article 122 and said the town was not allowed to print notes at all. They were all looking at the same town, and each of them answered a different question.

**Stopping an experiment is not a result.** The court gave a clear answer, but it was an answer about the law, not about whether the money had worked. When an experiment is stopped before its instrument has been read, what is left behind is not a result. It is a legend, and legends grow.

So the lesson is simple. Before you start, decide what you are measuring and how you will compare it with what would have happened anyway. If you do not, the number people remember afterwards will be the one that somebody made up.
