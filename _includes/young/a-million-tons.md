## The morning the news came

On the morning of April 12th 1955, at the University of Michigan in Ann Arbor, a scientist named Thomas Francis stood up in front of about four hundred doctors and read out the result of the biggest medical experiment anyone had ever run. Down the corridor, more than two hundred reporters had been waiting for hours. When men came to the door with boxes of the printed report, the reporters grabbed the copies out of their hands, and in the end the men just threw the reports into the air.

The result was in one sentence. The new polio vaccine, Francis said, had been "80 to 90 per cent effective against paralytic poliomyelitis." That means it stopped eight or nine out of every ten cases of the kind of polio that leaves children unable to move their arms or legs.

That same afternoon, in Washington, the government gave six companies a licence (official permission) to make the vaccine and sell it. By the next day thirteen batches of vaccine had been approved for use. Six of the thirteen came from a company in Berkeley, California, called the Cutter Laboratories. Remember that number. It matters later.

## What polio was

Polio is a disease caused by a virus. Most people who caught it had nothing worse than a fever, but in some children the virus got into the spinal cord and destroyed the nerves that move the muscles. Those children were left paralysed: unable to move a leg, or an arm, or, in the worst cases, unable to breathe without a machine. Polio came in summer waves that nobody could predict, and in the early 1950s it was the disease American parents feared most.

The virus comes in three types, and Jonas Salk, a scientist in Pittsburgh who was forty years old in 1955, had made a vaccine out of all three. He grew the virus in cells from monkey kidneys, then killed it with a chemical called formaldehyde. The idea was that dead virus could not make anyone ill, but the body would still learn to fight it. Salk had tested it on a few thousand volunteers. Now it had to be tested on a lot more.

## The experiment

An experiment is a way of finding something out so that you can be sure of the answer. The question here was simple: does the vaccine stop polio? Answering it was not simple at all.

Polio went up and down from year to year for no reason anyone understood. If you vaccinated every child in the country and polio went down the next summer, you still would not know whether the vaccine had done it. So you needed to compare children who got the vaccine with children who did not, in the same towns, in the same summer.

The charity that paid for everything, the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis, wanted to do it the easy way: give the vaccine to every child in the second grade, and watch the first and third grades, who would get nothing. Several state health departments said no. They saw two problems. A doctor who knew a child had been vaccinated might be a little slower to call a fever polio. And the families who agreed to let their children be vaccinated would not be the same kind of families as the ones who refused. The health departments said they would only join in if the experiment was done properly.

Properly meant this. In about half the areas, every child in the first three grades whose parents agreed got three injections from a little bottle that had only a number on it. Half the bottles held vaccine. The other half held salt water, which does nothing. Which child got which was decided by chance. The nurse did not know. The parents did not know. The doctor who examined a feverish child in August did not know. Only at the very end, when every case had been examined and written down, was the code broken.

Altogether 1,829,916 children took part. About 200,000 of them got the vaccine and about 200,000 got salt water. In the other half of the trial, about 220,000 second-graders got the vaccine and about 725,000 first- and third-graders were watched.

The answer was clear. Among the salt-water children, 115 had been paralysed by polio. Among the vaccinated children, 33. Four of the salt-water children had died. None of the vaccinated children had. The salt water was what made the answer believable: it meant the two groups of children were exactly alike, except for the vaccine.

## The other experiment, the one nobody called an experiment

That was the first experiment. There was a second one hidden inside it, and nobody treated it as an experiment at all.

The vaccine had to be made in factories, in huge amounts, and it had to be made safe. Salk's method for killing the virus rested on a theory. If you measured how fast the virus died in the formaldehyde for the first two or three days and drew the line on a graph, the line was straight. Salk said you could keep drawing the line past the point where you could measure anything, and the line told you how little live virus was left. After nine days, by the line, there would be about one living virus particle in every million tons of vaccine.

Because the theory said the process itself made the vaccine safe, the safety tests were designed only to catch big accidents. Each batch was tested by putting a small sample, one thousandth of the batch, into dishes of living cells and seeing whether anything grew, and by injecting some of it into monkeys. If nothing grew and no monkey got ill, the batch passed.

The first batches made in 1954 did not follow the line. In four of the first six batches tested, live virus was found. The trial was put back by a month, the factories tightened up their methods, and after that the batches tested clean. Everyone took that to mean the problem had been solved. The trial went ahead with vaccine from two companies, Parke, Davis and Eli Lilly. Cutter had made batches too, and they had passed the tests, but they were not used.

A year later, on April 12th 1955, the government licensed six companies, including Cutter, on the strength of the trial. The report the government wrote afterwards said it plainly: "The field trial evidence itself was interpreted to indicate that the commercial product was safe." But the trial had tested vaccine made by two companies a year earlier. It had not tested what came out of six factories in the spring of 1955. Nobody had.

## Six cases

On April 25th 1955 a baby was brought into a hospital in Chicago with both legs paralysed. The baby had been given a Cutter injection nine days earlier. The next day, five more children were reported in California, all paralysed, all given Cutter vaccine ten or eleven days before. And in every one of them the paralysis had started in the arm or leg where the needle had gone in.

That evening the government's scientists held a telephone conference. The number of cases was far more than chance would give. The next morning, April 27th, the Surgeon General, the country's chief doctor, asked Cutter to take back all its vaccine, and the company did, within the hour. The day after that, the government set up a team in Atlanta, under a doctor named Alexander Langmuir, to collect every report of polio in a vaccinated child anywhere in the country.

Then the government went into the factories, all of them, and read their records, including the records of batches that had failed and had never been reported. It found that live virus had been showing up at every company. One batch in nine, across the whole industry, had failed its first test. At one company a third of the finished batches had failed. Batches that failed were simply heated for a few more days and tested again until they passed. And the test itself was weak: because the sample was only one thousandth of the batch, a company with small batches tested only a tiny amount, and might miss live virus three times out of ten.

On May 7th the Surgeon General asked the whole country to stop vaccinating until the factories had been checked one by one. The stop lasted six days. New rules were written: bigger samples, two clean tests in a row, every batch reported. Two weeks later a monkey in a laboratory in Utah, injected with one of the Cutter batches, was paralysed, and live virus was found in its spinal cord.

## What it looked like from inside

In Idaho, 32,000 children had been given Cutter vaccine, nearly eight out of ten of the first- and second-graders in the state. Idaho usually had almost no polio until July. In the spring of 1955 it had 88 cases, and 84 of them were vaccinated children, their brothers and sisters, their parents, and their friends.

Near Baltimore, a baby girl got one Cutter injection on April 16th. She never became ill. But the live virus was growing inside her, and she passed it on. Twenty-three days later her mother got polio. Two families nearby caught it from them, and two more families caught it from those. Six people in one street got polio in three weeks, from one injection that had made nobody sick.

The paralysis gave the game away. In natural polio, the legs are hit far more often than the arms. In the Cutter children it was the opposite: the paralysis began in the arm that had been injected. Of 50 injected limbs, 41 were the first to go. That is the sign that the virus went in with the needle.

In Washington the programme fell apart in public. The President was asked at his press conference whether the Secretary in charge, Mrs Oveta Culp Hobby, should be fired. She resigned in July. The government's own report, in June, took "part of the blame itself," as a Washington newspaper put it, and said that the safety the theory had promised had never been there: "the process of inactivation did not always follow the predicted course."

## What happened

When everything had been counted, years later, two of Cutter's eight batches of raw vaccine had carried the live virus. Those two batches had been used for about 120,000 injections. About 60 of the children who got them had come down with polio, 51 of them paralysed, and 89 of their family members, 74 of them paralysed. Five children and five family members died. In the ordinary safety tests before the vaccine was released, 74 monkeys had been injected with vaccine from those batches and not one had become ill. Afterwards, using every trick they knew, scientists needed 461 monkeys to find the virus in 46 of them.

Under the new rules, more than 400 million doses of the vaccine were given in the United States over the next six years without a single case of polio caused by the vaccine.

## After

Anne Gottsdanker was five years old and lived in Santa Barbara, California. James Phipps was a small boy in Monrovia. Each got a Cutter injection from a doctor and each got polio. Anne lost the use of one leg and part of the other. Their parents sued the Cutter company, and the trial was held in Oakland in the winter of 1957.

The jury had a hard time. They decided that Cutter had not been careless: its vaccine had passed every test the government asked for. But the law said that a company that sold something to be put into a person's body promised, whether it said so or not, that it would not do harm. On January 17th 1958 they found for the children, and awarded Anne $131,500 and James $15,800. They wrote a note explaining that they felt they had "no alternative." One of them, Mrs Anita Steiner, said: "We felt obligated to follow the law, whether we agreed with it or not." Cutter appealed and lost. By 1962 it had paid more than three million dollars to the families, which was more than its insurance.

The vaccine itself was as good as Francis had said. The children who had been given salt water in 1954 were among the first to be offered the real thing.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**An experiment only answers the question it was built for.** The field trial was built to find out whether the vaccine worked. It answered that question so well that nobody has doubted it since. But it said nothing about whether six factories could make the vaccine safely a year later, because nobody had put that question to a test. People read the first answer as if it were the second.

**If your safety depends on a theory, check the theory.** Salk's straight line promised one live virus particle in a million tons. The tests were designed as if that were true, so they only looked for big accidents. When the line bent, and it bent at every company, the weak test was all that was left. Nobody had asked what the test would miss until after children were paralysed. Then the answer took one page of arithmetic.

**Look at all the results, not just the ones that passed.** The government saw only the batches the companies chose to send in. The batches that had failed, been reheated and passed on the second try were in the companies' own files, and the rules did not ask for them. Every company had such batches.

**The thing that caught the problem was counting.** No test in any factory found the two bad batches. What found them was a hospital in Chicago, a health department in California, and people who wrote down which arm the paralysis started in and how many days after the injection. Within two days the government knew that six cases were too many. Within ten days it had a team counting every case in the country. That team is the ancestor of the one that stopped the swine flu programme in 1976, in the story "[One in a Hundred Thousand](/experiments/2026/10/21/one-in-a-hundred-thousand.html)".

So the lesson is simple. When something is called safe, ask who tested that, and how, and what the test would have missed. A number that proves one thing is not proof of the next thing along.
