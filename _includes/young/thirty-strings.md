## The night the lights went out

At eleven o'clock at night on July 8th 1962, the street lights on Ferdinand Street in Honolulu went out. So did the lights on a street in Kailua, on the other side of the island of Oahu, in Hawaii. People telephoned the police to say so. The lights had gone out, they said, at the very moment the bomb went off.

The bomb was a hydrogen bomb, the most powerful kind of nuclear weapon. It had been carried up by a rocket from Johnston Island, a tiny island in the middle of the Pacific Ocean, about nine hundred miles from Honolulu. It exploded 250 miles above the Earth, about as high as the space station flies today. Its power was the same as about one and a half million tons of explosive.

For a few seconds the night sky over Hawaii turned white, as if the sun were coming up. Then it turned green, pink, red and deep orange, and stayed bright for about six minutes. People had gone out to the beaches and hills to watch. "You just can't put anything like this into words after you've seen it," one man said.

The test was called Starfish Prime. The people who ran it were pleased. "Certainly!" one of them said, when a reporter asked whether it had been a success.

But the street lights were not the only things the bomb reached. In the weeks that followed, satellites high above the Earth began to fail, one after another.

## Two experiments in the sky

This story is about two experiments. Both used the sky above everyone as their equipment.

The first was Starfish Prime. It was run by the United States military and the Atomic Energy Commission in 1962, as part of a long series of nuclear tests in the Pacific. They wanted to know what a nuclear bomb exploded very high up would do to radio, to radar, and to the space around the Earth.

The second was called Project West Ford. It was run by the United States Air Force and a laboratory belonging to MIT, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. In 1961 and again in 1963 they sent hundreds of millions of tiny copper needles into space. Each needle was less than an inch long and thinner than a hair. The idea was that the needles would spread out into a thin ring around the whole Earth. Radio signals could then be bounced off the ring, from one side of America to the other. An enemy could not cut it, the way you could cut a cable under the sea.

Both experiments had two questions. The first question was the one they were built to answer: would the bomb make a belt of radiation? Would the needles carry radio? The second question was just as important. Would the effect stay where it was meant to stay? Would it go away again?

For both experiments, the people in charge said yes. They said it before anyone had checked.

## A belt of radiation

Around the Earth there are two great invisible rings, called the Van Allen belts. They are made of tiny charged particles caught by the Earth's magnetism, the same force that makes a compass point north. They had been discovered only four years before, in 1958.

Scientists knew that a nuclear bomb exploded high up would add more particles to the belts. Small tests in 1958 had shown it. What they did not know was how many. Before the test, the Atomic Energy Commission said the effects would be "minor and temporary." That means small, and not lasting long.

They were wrong. The bomb made a new belt of radiation, far stronger than anyone had expected. It lasted for years.

## Thirty strings

The street lights in Honolulu were connected in long loops, like old Christmas lights. On each small loop there were about twenty lamps. Each loop had a little fuse the size of a coin, made of two lead disks with a thin plastic washer between them. A fuse is a part that is meant to break, to protect the rest of the circuit.

When the bomb went off, it sent out a pulse of energy called an electromagnetic pulse, or EMP for short. It is a sudden push of electricity through the air, too fast to see or feel. The pulse swept across Oahu. On about thirty loops of street lights, it pushed enough electricity into the wires to make the little fuse go off. Twenty lamps went dark on each loop. That was about 300 lamps in all.

Nothing else was broken. The workmen filed the lead disks clean, put them back, and the lights came on again. Each repair took about five minutes. A telephone link on another island, Kauai, probably stopped working too.

Those thirty loops became famous. For years afterwards, books about nuclear weapons used them as the best example of what an EMP can do to a city. But hardly anyone had written them down at the time. The city kept its records for ten years or less, and then they were gone. In 1989 a scientist named Charles Vittitoe tried to work out exactly what had happened. He had to ask the old foreman of the street-lighting crew what he remembered. The foreman said there had been many more broken loops than the 23 the workmen wrote down. They had been too busy that night to fill in all the forms.

The street lights were the experiment's measuring instrument for what a bomb does to a city. Nobody had planned it that way. They were just there, in the way.

## The satellites

There were only a few satellites in space in 1962. None of them had been put there to measure a bomb.

One was Ariel 1, the first British satellite. Four days after the test, its solar panels, which turn sunlight into electricity, were too damaged to power it properly. Another, called Transit 4B, stopped working on August 2nd. A third, called Traac, stopped on August 14th. The new radiation belt was wearing them out.

Then there was Telstar. Telstar was a round satellite about the size of a beach ball, built by the American telephone company. It was launched from Florida on July 10th 1962, a day after the bomb. It carried the first live television pictures across the Atlantic Ocean, from England to America. People were amazed.

Telstar's builders had written down six goals for it. The very first was: "To look for the unexpected." They had put special instruments on it to measure the radiation belts. For most of the next four months, Telstar was the only satellite at its height measuring the radiation. It found far more than anyone had expected.

And the radiation slowly damaged Telstar too. Inside it were tiny electronic switches called transistors. They were part of the system that let people on the ground send it commands. The radiation built up on them until they stopped working properly. In November 1962 Telstar stopped answering. The engineers did not give up. They sent commands from a station in South Africa, which could reach the satellite while it was in the part of its path where the radiation was weakest, and they invented special signals the damaged switches could still understand. In January 1963 Telstar woke up and worked again. But on February 21st 1963 it failed for good.

Meanwhile scientists were trying to understand the new belt. At first they guessed it would last much longer than it really did. A scientist named James Van Allen, who had discovered the natural belts, measured it for a year. He found the early guesses of how long it would last were about a hundred times too long. He asked the government to publish proper numbers, so that the people building satellites would know what to design for.

In 1963 the United States, the Soviet Union and Britain signed a treaty. They promised to stop testing nuclear weapons in the air, under the sea, and in space. Starfish Prime was the last time America set off a bomb that big above the atmosphere.

## The needles

Now back to the needles.

Astronomers are scientists who study the stars and planets with telescopes. Some telescopes see light. Others, called radio telescopes, listen to radio waves coming from space. When astronomers heard about the copper needles, many of them were worried. A ring of metal around the Earth might reflect radio signals into their telescopes and drown out the faint signals from the stars. And if it stayed there for ever, it could spoil astronomy for everyone.

The scientists at MIT took this seriously. They told the whole world about the plan in detail. They also designed the needles to leave. Each needle was so light for its size that sunlight itself would push on it, little by little, until it dropped low enough to burn up in the air. They said the ring would last a few years at most.

In 1961 the International Astronomical Union, which brings together astronomers from all over the world, met in California. It said that no group has the right to change the Earth's surroundings without everyone studying it and agreeing first. It said it was "completely opposed" to the needles until scientists could check for themselves that the ring would not last. One English astronomer, Hermann Bondi, put it in a sentence: "If we could be reassured the band would not be permanent, it would be all right."

The first launch was on October 21st 1961. It went wrong. The machine that was supposed to spin and throw out the needles did not spin, and the needles stayed stuck together in lumps. The second launch, in May 1963, worked. The needles spread out into a thin ring around the Earth, and for a while the two big radio dishes, one in California and one in Massachusetts, could talk to each other through it.

As the needles spread out further, the ring stopped working for radio. That was the plan. By the end of 1965 it had been swept out of the sky, just as the scientists had said. The astronomers could check, because the results were published for everyone to read.

## What was left behind

But not all the needles went away.

The machine that threw them out was supposed to send them off one by one. Many of them came out stuck together instead, in clumps. A clump is heavier for its size than a single needle, so sunlight cannot push it down so easily. The single needles fell, as promised. The clumps did not.

Today, more than sixty years later, about forty clumps of West Ford needles are still going round the Earth. The lumps from the 1961 launch were still there in 2013 too. The part of the experiment that the scientists had planned for went exactly as they said. The part they had not planned for is still up there.

## What happened next

The astronomers did get something in the end. In 1967 many countries signed a treaty about space. One part of it says that if a country plans an experiment in space that might get in the way of other countries, it must talk to them first. That rule is there partly because of the needles.

In 1963 a newspaper in Washington noticed that the two experiments were really the same story. Its writers remembered that the experts had said, before the bomb, that a big radiation belt was almost impossible. Then the bomb made one. If the experts could be wrong about that, the paper said, people were right to worry about the needles too.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**When your experiment is the sky, everyone's equipment becomes your measuring instrument.** Nobody at the test had set out to measure what the bomb would do to a city or to the satellites. The answers came from Honolulu's street-light fuses, from Telstar's switches, and from Ariel's solar panels. Those things belonged to other people, and nobody had asked them first.

**Saying "it will be fine" is not the same as checking.** Before the bomb, the people in charge said the effects would be small and would not last. They did not say exactly what they expected, and they did not plan a way to find out if they were wrong. So when they were wrong, the correction came late and from somebody else.

**Write your promise down so other people can test it.** The scientists with the needles said exactly what would happen and when. They let the worried astronomers watch and check. Their promise came true, and the astronomers could see that it had.

**Look out for the thing you did not plan.** The needles were designed to fall out of the sky, and they did. The clumps were a mistake nobody had planned for. That mistake is the part that is still in space today.

**Write things down at the time.** The street-light workmen were too busy to record every broken loop, and the city's records were not kept. Years later, scientists had to rely on one man's memory to work out what had happened.

So the lesson is simple. If you do an experiment that could reach other people, say exactly what you expect, and plan how you will check it. Then ask the people it might reach, before you begin.
