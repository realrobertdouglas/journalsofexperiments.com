## The farmyard

On June 2nd 1881, at two in the afternoon, a famous French scientist named Louis Pasteur walked into a farmyard at Pouilly-le-Fort, a village about forty kilometres from Paris. Several hundred people were waiting for him: farmers, animal doctors, government officials, army officers and newspaper reporters, including the Paris reporter of *The Times* of London. They clapped.

In a shed were two groups of sheep. One group had a hole punched in each sheep's ear. Those sheep were standing up and eating. The other group had no holes in their ears, and they were dead or dying of a disease called anthrax. Men were carrying the dead ones out to graves that had been dug in advance. Five weeks earlier, Pasteur had printed a programme saying which sheep would die and on which day. This was the day.

Each sheep with a hole in its ear had been given, on May 5th, five drops of a liquid from a syringe. On May 17th it had been given five drops of a second, stronger liquid. Then on May 31st every sheep in both groups had been given a dose of anthrax so strong that it killed every untreated sheep it was given to. Two days later, the sheep with the five drops in them were alive and the others were dead.

What was in the five drops was the one thing nobody in the yard could see. Pasteur's report to the French Academy of Sciences said when the drops were given and how many, and then said that the vaccine had been made by the same method that had made his earlier vaccine for a disease of chickens. Everyone who knew his work knew what that method was: leave the germ in contact with the air, and the oxygen in the air weakens it. The notebook in which Pasteur wrote down what the five drops really were was kept shut by his family for more than ninety years. This story is about an experiment that worked, a report that did not say how, and what the notebook was for.

## The man and the disease

Louis Pasteur was fifty-eight. He had started out as a chemist and had become famous for showing that tiny living things, microbes, cause fermentation and disease. A stroke had left him lame on one side. He had two young assistants, Charles Chamberland, who was thirty, and Émile Roux, who was twenty-seven and did the actual injecting. Pasteur directed.

Anthrax was a disease that killed sheep and cattle, sometimes whole flocks. By 1881 scientists knew it was caused by a rod-shaped microbe, and Pasteur believed its spores (the microbe's tough resting form) lay in the ground where dead animals had been buried, and were brought up to the grass by earthworms. France lost more than twenty million francs' worth of animals to it every year.

The farm belonged to Hippolyte Rossignol, an animal doctor who had written in the veterinary newspapers that Pasteur's ideas were "utopias," which is a polite word for dreams. The local farming society, the Agricultural Society of Melun, asked Pasteur to prove his vaccine in public, on Rossignol's farm, and he said yes.

There was also a man who was not at the farm. Henri Toussaint, a thirty-three-year-old teacher at the veterinary school in Toulouse, in the south of France, had announced an anthrax vaccine of his own in July 1880, seven months before Pasteur announced his. Remember him.

## Two ideas in one experiment

Pasteur's programme made one prediction, but it really rested on two ideas, and the sheep could only test one of them.

The first idea was that if you gave an animal a weakened anthrax germ, and then a slightly stronger one, it would afterwards survive a dose that killed an untreated animal. That is what a vaccine is: a mild form of a disease that protects you from the deadly form. In February 1881 Pasteur had told the Academy that he had done this with sheep in his laboratory, and that he would soon try it on a large scale. The farmyard could test that idea by counting sheep.

The second idea was about *how* the germ was weakened, and this was the idea Pasteur cared most about, because it was his own discovery. In October 1880 he had explained that cultures of the chicken cholera germ left standing in contact with air slowly lost their power to kill, while cultures sealed away from air did not. "It is the oxygen of the air that weakens and extinguishes virulence," he wrote. For anthrax it was harder, because the germ made spores, and spores did not weaken in air. His answer, in February 1881, was to grow the germ at 42 to 43 degrees, a temperature at which it made no spores, and leave it in the air. He called the method "of a marvellous simplicity." He also attacked Toussaint, whose vaccine was anthrax blood warmed at 55 degrees: Toussaint's method, he said, was uncertain, and his own was sure.

So the sheep could show that a weakened germ protects. They could not show how the germ had been weakened. But because Pasteur had just told everyone that his method was air, everyone took the sheep as proof of the oxygen method too.

## The programme

The programme was agreed on April 28th 1881. The society would give Pasteur sixty sheep. Ten would be left alone. Twenty-five would be vaccinated twice, about twelve days apart, with two weakened germs, the second stronger than the first. Then those twenty-five and twenty-five untreated sheep would all be given deadly anthrax. "The twenty-five unvaccinated sheep will all die," Pasteur wrote; "the twenty-five vaccinated will resist." The society asked for cattle too, so Pasteur added ten cows, six vaccinated and four not.

Pasteur admitted later that this was "a boldness of prophecy that only a striking success could excuse." Friends told him it was scientifically imprudent. He answered with a saying that has been quoted ever since: "Chance favours prepared minds." To his assistants he said that what had worked on fourteen sheep in the laboratory would work on fifty at Melun.

On May 5th a crowd came out by train, most of them animal doctors who did not believe a word of it. Twenty-four sheep, a goat and six cattle each got five drops under the skin. For the next five days their temperatures were taken. Nothing happened. "On the whole, excellent health," Rossignol wrote.

On May 17th they were given the second, stronger vaccine. This one, Rossignol noted, usually killed about half the sheep it was given to if they had not had the first. Again, almost nothing happened: one lamb ran a fever for a day.

## The three days

Pasteur fixed May 31st for the deadly dose. On May 28th and 29th his assistants quietly gave it early to two vaccinated and two unvaccinated sheep, so that there would be a dead sheep to show the crowd. Both unvaccinated sheep died within a day. Both vaccinated ones lived.

On May 31st the yard was full again. One of the sceptics, an animal doctor named Biot, had been warned by Pasteur's most stubborn opponent that the germs settled to the bottom of the bottle, and that the vaccinated sheep would be given the weak liquid from the top. So Biot shook the bottle hard, and asked for a triple dose, and Rossignol asked for the two groups of sheep to be mixed up before anyone was injected. Pasteur agreed to all of it. Roux injected forty-four sheep and two goats in whatever order they were brought to him, and then the cattle. By half past three it was done.

On June 1st Rossignol stayed at the shed until dark. The unvaccinated sheep stood apart and would not eat; every one had a hot, painful swelling where the needle had gone in. A few of the vaccinated sheep had fevers too, and one ewe, number 3, looked very ill. At twenty to seven the first unvaccinated sheep fell down and died. Ten minutes later another died, and then another. Rossignol went home at eight, expecting many more to die in the night.

In Paris that evening Pasteur, who had written a programme that left nothing to chance, lost his nerve. His assistants had come back with the fevers of the vaccinated sheep, and a telegram from Rossignol said the ewe was probably lost. "For a few moments," Roux wrote later, "his faith wavered, as if the experimental method could betray him." Nobody in the house slept.

At four the next morning the farm men found eleven unvaccinated sheep dead, and the goat. At nine a telegram reached the laboratory. Pasteur wrote to his children before catching the train: "This morning eighteen were already dead and the others dying. As for the vaccinated, all are on their feet. The telegram ends with these words: Stunning success." Then he added that it was still too early to be sure.

At two o'clock he walked into the yard. The people who were there do not quite agree on the count. Rossignol said fourteen unvaccinated sheep were dead when Pasteur arrived and the rest were dying. Pasteur told the Academy twenty-one were dead and the last three died that afternoon. The man from *The Times* telegraphed to London: "At two o'clock twenty-three of the sheep which had not been inoculated were dead. At three o'clock died the 24th, and the 25th an hour later. The twenty-five inoculated animals were sound, and frolicked and gave signs of perfect health." Everyone agrees on the end: all twenty-four unvaccinated sheep and the goat died, and all the vaccinated animals lived, except the ewe, number 3, who died two days later. When they opened her up she was found to be carrying a lamb that had died inside her two weeks before, and the animal doctors blamed her death on that. The vaccinated cows were fine; the unvaccinated cows got huge swellings but did not die.

Rossignol, who had made fun of Pasteur in print, announced in the yard that the farm would from now on be called the Clos Pasteur. "Pouilly was my road to Damascus," he wrote, meaning that it had converted him.

## Inside the notebook

Now the part nobody in the yard could see.

Pasteur kept laboratory notebooks all his life, and in 1878 he told his family never to show them to anyone. They obeyed. In 1964 his grandson gave them to the national library in Paris, but they stayed closed until he died in 1971. In the 1980s and 1990s two historians, Antonio Cadeddu and Gerald Geison, read the notebook for 1881, and what they found was this.

On April 13th 1881, two weeks before Pasteur signed the programme, he tested two vaccines side by side. One was his own, weakened by air. The other had been made by Chamberland, who had weakened the germ by growing it in broth with a chemical in it, a salt called potassium bichromate. Two sheep got each vaccine, and then all four got deadly anthrax. One of the sheep on Pasteur's air vaccine died. Both sheep on Chamberland's chemical vaccine lived. Pasteur wrote that Chamberland's was the surer.

On April 26th he wrote a page headed "Anthrax. Vaccination at Melun," and in a note at the bottom he set down his plan: the sheep would be given a germ weakened with potassium bichromate. And on another page he recorded what was done on May 5th: the first vaccine was a germ "attenuated by Chamberland with bichromate," made so weak it could not kill a mouse, and then strengthened a little by being passed through three mice in turn. The second vaccine, of May 17th, was also made with bichromate.

So the five drops were not made by the oxygen of the air at all. They were made with a chemical. And a chemical was exactly the kind of method Toussaint, the rival in Toulouse, had turned to the summer before, when he started weakening anthrax blood with carbolic acid. Pasteur's assistants had heard about that from a friend in August 1880 and had gone looking for a chemical vaccine of their own. Pasteur's published papers attacked Toussaint's warmed blood and never mentioned Toussaint's carbolic acid, or Chamberland's bichromate. The crowd went home believing they had watched the oxygen of the air at work.

## What happened next

The vaccine worked, and it spread fast. By the following February more than ninety thousand animals had been vaccinated. In one department, Eure-et-Loir, nearly eighty thousand sheep were vaccinated in 1882; before, about nine in every hundred had died of anthrax each year, and afterwards fewer than one in a hundred did. Not every trial went well, and the German scientist Robert Koch attacked Pasteur's methods and his figures. But by and large the sheep lived.

Two years after the farmyard, in April 1883, Chamberland and Roux published a short paper on weakening the anthrax germ with chemicals, including potassium bichromate. They described a bichromate germ that killed about half the sheep it was given to, which was exactly the second vaccine of Pouilly-le-Fort. They wrote as if it were new, and they did not mention the farm. Pasteur's nephew said years later that Pasteur had forbidden them to publish it until the oxygen method had been made to work. Roux himself, in 1896, still wrote that the farmyard vaccine had been weakened by air.

Toussaint fell ill in 1881, lost his mind, and died in 1890 at forty-three.

When Geison published the notebook pages in 1995 he said Pasteur had "deliberately deceived the public." A famous scientist, Max Perutz, answered that Geison was the one behaving badly, and that bichromate is a chemical that works on germs the way oxygen does, so Pasteur had not really lied. Both men had a point. The vaccine was real; the air method soon worked too, and it was the one the farmers were sold. But for a hundred and fourteen years every account of the most famous experiment in the history of vaccines gave the wrong recipe for what was in the syringe. The companion pieces tell more: "[The race](/deeper/five-drops/the-race/#young)" is about Toussaint, "[The ledger](/deeper/five-drops/the-ledger/#young)" is about the sheep vaccinated afterwards, and "[The tenth notebook](/deeper/five-drops/the-tenth-notebook/#young)" is about the notebook and the argument over it.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A public experiment only proves what people can see.** The crowd could see dead sheep and live sheep. That proved that a weakened germ protects. It could not prove how the germ had been weakened, because nobody could see inside the syringe. But Pasteur had just told everyone his method was air, so they believed the sheep had proved that too. Before you show people an experiment, be clear about which question it can answer.

**How you describe an experiment is part of the experiment.** The sheep really did live. But because the report hinted at the wrong method, the wrong method travelled with the true result into every textbook for a century, and the man whose idea it resembled was forgotten. A true result with a misleading story attached does more lasting damage than a false result, because nobody checks a success.

**Keep an honest notebook, even if you keep it shut.** Pasteur did not tell the public what was in the five drops, but he wrote it down truthfully in his notebook and never destroyed it. That is why, ninety years later, the truth could be found. Write down what you actually did. The paper that says what you wanted it to look like will be kept for you by everyone else.

**Rivalry changes what people say.** Pasteur chose the chemical vaccine because it was the surer one, which was the right reason. He could not say so because saying so would have put his rival's name next to his own. When you are racing someone, you read your own results for what they do to the race. Someone who is not racing should read them too.

So the lesson is simple. An experiment can be true and its story can still be wrong. Say exactly what you did, and keep the notebook that proves it.
