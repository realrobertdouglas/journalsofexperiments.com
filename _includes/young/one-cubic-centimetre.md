## A glass of water in Munich

On the morning of October 7th 1892, an old professor in the German city of Munich picked up a glass of water in front of a few colleagues and drank it.

The water had something in it. A small measured amount of liquid, grown in a laboratory, that was full of the germ that causes cholera. Cholera is a disease of the gut. It gives people violent diarrhoea, and in the days before doctors knew how to replace the water a body loses, it killed huge numbers of people very quickly. At that moment, in the German port of Hamburg, it was killing hundreds of people a week.

The professor was Max von Pettenkofer. He was seventy-three years old. He had worked out beforehand exactly what he thought would happen, and he had written down what he would say if he was right and what he would say if he was wrong. That turns out to be the problem with the whole experiment, and it is why this story is here.

## The man who cleaned up cities

Pettenkofer was born in 1818 in a small place on the river Danube. His family put him into a chemist's shop as a boy. He hated it, ran off to be an actor for a few months, then went back and studied medicine instead. He became a chemist and a very good one.

Then he invented a subject. He is the reason there is a thing called hygiene: the science of how the places people live in make them ill or keep them well. A university chair of hygiene was created for him in Munich, the first anywhere, and a whole institute was built for him. Cities across Germany listened to him about drains, fresh air, clean streets and clean water, and German cities became much healthier because of it.

So when he drank that glass, he was not a crank. He was one of the most respected scientists in Europe, and most of what he had spent his life saying was right.

## The argument he was in

In 1883 and 1884 another German, Robert Koch, had gone to Egypt and India to study cholera. He came back with something new: a tiny curved bacterium, a germ shaped like a comma, which he found in the guts of cholera patients and nowhere else. People called it the comma bacillus. Koch said it was the cause of cholera.

If that was true, certain things followed. Keep the germ out of people's mouths and you stop the disease. Filter the drinking water. Isolate the sick. Disinfect their houses. Stop ships and travellers at the border.

Pettenkofer did not believe it was that simple, and he had an argument that sounds surprisingly modern. He said the cause of cholera was an equation with three unknowns, and he called them x, y and z.

- **x** was the germ, carried about by people travelling.
- **y** was something about the place: the soil, the ground, the time of year. He called it the "local disposition".
- **z** was something about the person: whether that particular body was the kind that catches it.

His point was that the germ-hunters had thrown away the y. He said there are not only people who never catch cholera, but whole towns that never catch it, even when sick travellers keep arriving. He had a list of such towns, and he wanted to know why.

It was a real question, and nobody in 1892 could answer it well.

## Why he believed in the ground

The strange thing is where his theory came from. It came from an experiment that went against him, and he accepted the result.

When cholera struck Munich in 1854, Pettenkofer believed the drinking water carried it. He set out to prove it. Munich then got its water from lots of different supplies, so he worked out, house by house, from the water rate books and by walking round and looking, which house drank which water. Then he compared that with who had got cholera.

There was no pattern at all. He tried the same thing with typhoid fever, another disease people blamed on water, and again found nothing. He concluded that in Munich the drinking water had nothing to do with it.

So he looked at the ground instead. From 1856 the level of water in the soil under Munich, which is called the ground water, was measured regularly. When he drew the ground water level and the number of typhoid deaths on the same chart, the two lines moved together. When the ground water fell, the fever rose.

That chart is the heart of his whole career. From it he built a theory: the germ leaves a sick person unable to cause an epidemic, falls on ground of the right dampness, ripens there, and only then becomes dangerous. Water could carry the germ about, he said, but that was the only way water mattered. Filtering it would not save a town.

He was wrong about that, and the cost of being wrong was about to be counted in a city about four hundred miles to the north.

## Hamburg, that summer

Hamburg is Germany's great port. In the middle of August 1892 cholera broke out there.

Hamburg took its drinking water out of the river Elbe just upstream of the city, and barely cleaned it. The waterworks were about forty years old, and the filters were so poor that a visitor reported that water weeds, shells and even eels had been found in the pipes.

Next door was Altona, a separate town pressed right up against Hamburg, so close that in places one street belonged to both. Altona took its water from the Elbe too, but from downstream of both towns, where the river was dirtier, and then put it through sand filters that were properly looked after. A third neighbour, Wandsbeck, used lake water and filtered that.

Same ground. Same weather. Same kind of people, in the same kinds of houses. One difference: the water.

## October 7th

In Munich, hundreds of miles away, Pettenkofer asked for some of the germ. A professor named Georg Gaffky sent it down from Hamburg, and two younger colleagues in Munich, Richard Pfeiffer and Carl Eisenlohr, grew it.

Then Pettenkofer made sure the germs would survive the journey through his stomach. Koch had shown that the acid in a stomach kills them, which is why swallowing them does not reliably make anyone ill. Pettenkofer deliberately took that defence away before he drank.

Several of his students begged to take it instead of him. He refused. He said that at his age, with diabetes and not one tooth left in his head, he was the least valuable person in the room, and if it killed him he would die "in the cause of science, like a soldier on the field of honour."

Then he drank it. He said it tasted "like very pure water."

Two days later the diarrhoea started. It went on for about a week, with stomach cramps. His colleagues looked at what came out of him under a microscope: it was swarming with the germ, so the experiment had certainly worked in the sense that the germs had got in and multiplied. But he was not really ill. He kept eating, kept working, refused medicine, and got better.

Ten days after Pettenkofer, a colleague of his named Rudolf Emmerich did the same thing. Emmerich got much sicker. On the worst two days he had fifteen to twenty watery bowel movements, which is what the start of real cholera looks like, and he was given medicine to stop it.

## What he said it proved

In November Pettenkofer stood up in front of the Munich Medical Society and said the question was settled: the comma bacillus does not cause cholera. The room was delighted. His paper was printed days later and translated in London within the week.

Now look at one sentence in that paper. He wrote that if he had done the same thing in Hamburg, the experiment "might have ended fatally", because in Hamburg both his x and his y were present.

Read that again. If he lived, it proved the germ was not enough. If he had died, it proved that the place matters. He had arranged things so that he could not possibly lose. An experiment like that is not a test at all. It is a way of saying what you already believe, with extra steps.

And when Emmerich got badly ill, Pettenkofer did not treat that as a result. He treated it as something to explain away.

## The experiment nobody designed

Meanwhile, Hamburg and Altona had been running the real test.

By the middle of November, Hamburg, with about 620,000 people, had had nearly 18,000 cases of cholera and more than 7,600 deaths. Altona, with about 143,000 people, had 572 cases and 328 deaths, and most of its early cases were people who worked over in Hamburg. Wandsbeck had 64 cases.

You can see it best in one street. A straight road runs from Hamburg into Altona on level ground. The Hamburg half is called Schmuckstrasse and the Altona half is called Ferdinandstrasse. In the Hamburg half there were cholera patients in every single house. In the Altona half there was not one case.

On another street, one side of the road got Hamburg water and the other side got Altona water. The Hamburg side was hit hard. The Altona side was spared.

Robert Koch went and looked, and then wrote the sentence that ends the argument. The epidemic, he said, was "a kind of experiment, which performed itself on more than a hundred thousand human beings", and it did everything a laboratory experiment is supposed to do: "In two large groups of population all the factors are the same except one, namely, the water-supply."

The cholera, he noticed, had not stopped at the boundary between the two towns. There was one block of flats that belonged to Hamburg but bought its water from Altona, and the cholera went round it. The disease "found out with sharp precision not the political frontier but the frontier of the water-supplies of the two cities."

Koch also said what weather can and cannot explain. "Sky, sun, wind, rain, etc., were distributed with absolute equality on both sides of the frontier."

## Why one glass could not settle anything

Doctors in London admired Pettenkofer's courage and then said, politely, that his experiment proved nothing. Here is why they were right.

**He was only one person.** Koch reckoned that more than half of all people can swallow this germ and not get cholera. If most people would be fine, then one person being fine tells you about that person, not about the germ.

**There was nothing to compare him with.** A proper experiment needs two groups that are the same except for the one thing you are testing. He had one old man, and no second man who drank the same glass without the germs in it.

**Things he could not see were changing the answer.** Later it came out that the germs had come from a patient with a mild case. Emmerich had taken a drug that masked how ill he was. Another doctor in Vienna swallowed the germ twice and was fine the second time, probably because the first dose had made him immune. Every one of these would change the result, and his experiment could not detect any of them.

**Two men is not two experiments.** It is two stories that disagree with each other. When they disagreed, he counted the mild one and discounted the bad one.

## Afterwards

Pettenkofer never gave up the y, though in the next year he did admit that the germ was real and specific, and his opponents admitted that his z, the question of who catches it, mattered too. That part of the argument he won.

Hamburg built proper filters. The following winter Altona's filters worked badly in the frost and let cholera through, which Koch investigated, and which showed that filtering only protects you if you do it properly.

Pettenkofer retired in 1894. His wife had died, and he left Munich for the countryside. On February 10th 1901, old, in pain and depressed, he shot himself. Thousands of people came to his funeral.

A week later a London medical journal printed a kind obituary. It said that he had swallowed cholera germs to prove they were harmless, that he had been lucky, and that he "must have been too much a man of science to have attached any significance to his solitary experiment".

That is the saddest line in the story, because it is not true. He had attached every significance to it. The writer simply could not believe that a great scientist would stake so much on a single case.

One more thing is worth saying. Modern scientists think Pettenkofer's way of thinking was better than his theory. Whether a germ makes you ill really does depend on you and on where you live: on how much of it you swallow, on what you have eaten, on how crowded your home is, on whether your water is clean. He was asking the right question. He just went looking for the answer in the wrong place, in the wrong way, with a sample of one.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**An experiment you cannot lose is not an experiment.** Before he drank, Pettenkofer had already decided what both possible outcomes would mean. Staying well proved him right, and getting dangerously ill would also have proved him right. When every result supports your idea, the results are not testing it. Before you start, write down what would make you change your mind. If you cannot think of anything, stop.

**One person is not enough to answer a question about everyone.** Most people who swallow the cholera germ do not get cholera. So one old man swallowing it and feeling nearly fine tells you about that one man on that one day. If you want to know what something does in general, you need many cases, and you need something to compare them with.

**When two results disagree, you do not get to pick the one you like.** Pettenkofer had a mild illness and Emmerich had a bad one. That is a disagreement, and it was the most interesting thing in the whole experiment. He kept his own result and explained his colleague's away.

**Courage is not evidence.** What he did took real nerve, and everybody said so at the time. Being brave about an experiment does not make the experiment any good, and nobody should ever copy this one.

**The best test of his idea was already happening, and it was not his.** Two towns, side by side, on the same ground, under the same sky, with one difference between them: the water. One street with cholera in every house on the Hamburg side and none on the Altona side. Nobody designed that, and it answered the question better than any single person ever could.

So the lesson is simple. The point of an experiment is to give the world a chance to tell you that you are wrong. If you build it so that cannot happen, you have not run an experiment; you have only proved that you are stubborn.
