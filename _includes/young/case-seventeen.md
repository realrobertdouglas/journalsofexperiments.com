## The disease

Smallpox was a disease that killed people. It gave you a fever and then covered your skin in spots that filled with pus. About one person in five or six who caught it died, and many of the rest were left with scars or went blind. In the 1790s it killed about two thousand people a year in London alone, and perhaps forty thousand a year in Britain and Ireland. Nearly everyone caught it at some time in their life.

There was one way to protect yourself, and it was dangerous. If a doctor scratched a little pus from a smallpox spot into your skin, you got a mild case of the disease, and after that you could never catch it again. This was called inoculation. It usually worked, but the mild case was still smallpox: you could pass it on to other people, and now and then the mild case was not mild, and the person died. It had been done in England since the 1720s.

Doctors who inoculated people noticed something odd. Some people could not be given the smallpox at all. The scratch went red for a few days and then faded, and nothing else happened. This was what always happened when you inoculated someone who had already had smallpox. But these people had never had it.

## The milkmaids

In the dairy country of Gloucestershire, in the west of England, the people who could not be given the smallpox turned out to have had something else. It was called the cowpox. Cows sometimes got sores on their udders, and the maids and men who milked them got sores on their hands, with a day or two of fever. It was unpleasant, but nobody died of it. And the farmers believed that anyone who had had the cowpox was safe from the smallpox for life.

Edward Jenner was a surgeon in Berkeley, a small town in that dairy country. He had been born there on May 17th 1749. As a young man he had spent two years in London learning from John Hunter, the most famous surgeon in England, who once wrote to him about a question he had asked: "but why think, — why not try the experiment?" Jenner remembered that.

He heard the farmers' belief about the cowpox when he was a young doctor, and he could not let it go. At the meetings of the local medical society he talked about it so much that the other doctors threatened to throw him out. They all knew the story, and they all knew people who had had "the cowpox" and then caught smallpox anyway. So they thought it was just a country tale.

Jenner thought there was an answer to that. There was more than one kind of sore a cow could have on its udder. Only one of them was the true cowpox. The others looked a little like it, and milkers caught them too, but they did not protect you from anything. If someone had "the cowpox" and then caught smallpox, Jenner said, they had had the wrong cowpox.

## The test

Jenner found people who had had the cowpox years before, made sure they had never had smallpox, and then tried to inoculate them with smallpox. One man, Joseph Merret, had had the cowpox in 1770. Jenner inoculated him twenty-five years later, in 1795, and "found it impracticable to infect him with it." A woman named Mary Barge had had the cowpox thirty-one years before he tried; she could not be given the smallpox either. He collected sixteen cases like this.

But those people had caught the cowpox by accident, from cows. Jenner wanted to know something more. Could you take the cowpox from a milkmaid's hand and put it into a child on purpose? Would the child be protected? And could you then take it from that child and put it into the next, and the next, so that you never needed a cow at all?

In November 1789 he had tried something like it on his own baby son, Edward, using a different mild disease called the swinepox. Afterwards he tried to inoculate the baby with smallpox several times, and it never took. But he wanted to do the same thing with the real cowpox, and for years there was none about.

## May 14th 1796

In May 1796 a dairymaid named Sarah Nelmes caught the cowpox from her master's cows. She had scratched her hand on a thorn, and the sore grew there. Jenner had her hand drawn for his book. It is the picture at the top of this page.

On May 14th 1796 he took matter from her sore and scratched it into the arm of a boy of about eight. He made two small cuts, each half an inch long, "barely penetrating the cutis", which means only just through the skin. The boy was James Phipps, the son of Jenner's gardener. Nobody wrote down whether the boy or his father was asked.

Seven days later the boy's armpit was sore. On the ninth day he was cold and shivery, lost his appetite, had a headache and slept badly. On the tenth day he was well again. That was the cowpox.

Then came the real experiment. On July 1st 1796 Jenner took fresh matter from a smallpox spot and inoculated the boy with it, in several places on both arms. If the cowpox had done nothing, the boy would now get smallpox. He did not. "No disease followed," Jenner wrote. The scratches went red for a few days and faded, exactly as they did on someone who had already had the smallpox. A few months later Jenner tried again, and again nothing happened.

Jenner wrote to his friend Edward Gardner on July 19th: "But now listen to the most delightful part of my story. The boy has since been inoculated for the small pox which, as I ventured to predict, produced no effect."

Years later he admitted that he had not been so sure. The boy's illness had been so slight that "I could scarcely persuade myself the patient was secure from the small-pox", he said. He only believed it when he saw the boy's arm on July 1st.

## One boy

Think about what Jenner had and what he did not have. He had one boy. He did not have a second boy, given the smallpox scratch without the cowpox first, to show what happened when the cowpox was not there. He did not need one, in his own mind, because he had been inoculating people for years and knew exactly what an unprotected arm looked like. But anyone reading about it had only his word for that.

He also could not try the next step, passing the cowpox from James Phipps to another child, because the cowpox had gone from the farms again. The spring of 1797 was dry, and there was none. Jenner even bought a young horse and fed it beans to make its heels swell, because he believed the cowpox started in the sore heels of horses, "but to no purpose."

So for nearly two years the whole experiment was one boy.

## The Royal Society

The Royal Society is the oldest scientific society in Britain, and Jenner belonged to it. In 1788 it had printed his study of cuckoos, and in 1789 it had made him a fellow. He wanted to publish the cowpox experiment in its journal. In 1797 the paper was shown to the President, Sir Joseph Banks. The message that came back, as Jenner's biographer wrote it down, was that Jenner "ought not to risk his reputation by presenting to the learned body anything which appeared so much at variance with established knowledge, and withal so incredible." In plain words: this is too strange, and one boy is not enough. Jenner took the paper away.

A famous doctor named John Haygarth had written the same thing the year before. The story was "very marvellous", he wrote, and "very clear and full evidence will be required to render it credible."

They were not being stupid. One boy really is not much evidence. What they could not see was the instrument Jenner had used. The smallpox scratch had been read on thousands of arms for seventy years. Jenner knew the two readings, protected and unprotected, as well as he knew his own hand.

## More children

In March 1798 the cowpox came back to the farms. Jenner inoculated a boy of five, William Summers, straight from a cow. From Summers he passed it to William Pead, who was eight. From Pead he passed it to several more, including Hannah Excell, who was seven. From Hannah he passed it to four more, one of them his own second son, Robert, who was eleven months old and did not catch it. From one of those it went to a boy called Barge. The cowpox had gone through five children in a row.

Then he tested them with smallpox. Summers could not be given it. Pead and Barge were inoculated by Jenner's nephew Henry, and nothing happened to them either. Henry did one thing more, and it was the one thing in the whole book that a reader could check: he inoculated someone who had never had the cowpox with the same smallpox matter, "and it produced the Small-pox in the usual regular manner." That showed the smallpox matter was good. The cowpox had stopped it.

Jenner printed everything in June 1798, at his own expense, in a book of seventy-five pages with four coloured pictures. It is called *An Inquiry into the Causes and Effects of the Variolæ Vaccinæ*. Variolæ vaccinæ is Latin for "the smallpox of the cow". That Latin word, vaccine, is where our word comes from. James Phipps is Case XVII, case seventeen, and in the book he has no name.

## London

Jenner went to London for three months in 1798 to show the cowpox to the doctors there, and could not find one person who would let him try it. The first London doctor to test it was Henry Cline, using matter dried on a quill. It worked.

In January 1799 the cowpox broke out among two hundred cows at a dairy in Gray's Inn Lane, in the middle of London. William Woodville, the doctor in charge of the Smallpox Hospital, began inoculating people from the cows. By May he had done about six hundred, and tested more than four hundred of them with smallpox. None caught it.

But something was wrong. Three out of five of Woodville's patients came out in spots all over their bodies, like a mild smallpox. One girl, Ann Bumpus, had 310 of them. Jenner had said the cowpox gave no spots. What had happened was this: Woodville was working in a hospital full of smallpox, and to be safe he had inoculated his first patients with smallpox in the other arm five days after the cowpox. Then he took matter from their arms to inoculate the next people. The cowpox and the smallpox had got mixed. Another doctor, George Pearson, sent threads dipped in this London matter to more than two hundred doctors all over the country. One surgeon told Parliament later that the matter he was sent "proved to be variolous", which means it was smallpox.

Jenner told Woodville that his matter had been "contaminated". Woodville did not like the word, but it was the right one. The story of those months is in the companion piece "[Gray's Inn Lane](/deeper/case-seventeen/grays-inn-lane/#young)".

## What happened next

Doctors attacked Jenner's book, but mostly for the wrong part. He had opened it with his theory that the cowpox came from the sore heels of horses. His critics showed that there were dairies with cowpox and no horses, and dairies with horses and no cowpox, and said he was talking nonsense. None of them said anything about the boy.

Others said the cowpox did not always work. A surgeon in Portsea, William Goldson, inoculated some children with smallpox in 1804 who had been vaccinated four years before, and some of their arms inflamed and one child had a fever. Jenner's answer was always the same: they had been given the wrong cowpox, or matter taken too late. He also went back to the boy. Phipps, he wrote in 1804, "has again been put to the trial with impunity."

Jenner had said that the cowpox protected you "for ever". That was wrong. By the second half of the 1800s doctors knew that the protection wore off, and that people needed vaccinating again. But the main claim was right. Vaccination spread across Europe and the world. By 1801 Jenner wrote that a hundred thousand people in Britain had been vaccinated. In 1802 Parliament voted him ten thousand pounds, by fifty-nine votes to fifty-six, and in 1807 twenty thousand more.

James Phipps grew up in Berkeley. Jenner kept testing him. In about 1818 he told a friend that Phipps had "recently" been inoculated for smallpox "I believe for the twentieth time, and all without effect." That year Jenner built him a cottage, with roses from his own garden, because Phipps lived in "a miserable place". The cottage is still there, in Church Lane. Jenner died on January 26th 1823. Phipps was at the funeral, and died in 1853.

The last person in the world to catch smallpox naturally caught it in 1977. On May 8th 1980 the World Health Organization announced that the disease was gone from the earth. It is the only human disease that has ever been wiped out. It began with one boy and two half-inch scratches.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**The experiment is the test, not the thing you do first.** A farmer named Benjamin Jesty scratched cowpox into his wife and sons in 1774, twenty-two years before Jenner, because he believed the same story. But he never tested them, so nobody learned anything from it. Jenner's real experiment was not May 14th, when he gave the boy the cowpox. It was July 1st, when he tried to give him the smallpox and could not.

**One is a very small number.** The Royal Society was right to say that one boy was not enough. Jenner knew that too, which is why he went on to do six more children, and why the doctors in London did six hundred. A result from one person is a reason to try more, not a reason to stop.

**Sometimes the proof is in the instrument, not on the page.** Jenner had no second boy to compare with, but he had seventy years of inoculated arms in his head. He knew what a protected arm looked like and what an unprotected one looked like. That was a real check. The trouble was that nobody else could see it. His nephew Henry wrote down the one check anyone could read: he put the same smallpox matter into someone unprotected, and it worked. Write down your checks.

**An explanation that can never be wrong is not a proof.** Jenner said that every failure was a case of the wrong cowpox. He was often right. But it meant that no failure could ever count against him, and so nobody found out for thirty years that the protection wore off.

**The thing you send out is not the thing you tested.** Jenner tested a dairymaid's sore on a boy in a village. London sent out threads dipped in matter from a smallpox hospital, and some of it was smallpox. His name was on it, and he had never tested it.

So the lesson is simple. When you try something on one person, test it properly, write down how you tested it, and then keep testing. Jenner tested the same boy for twenty years.
