## The box

In Moscow there is an institute that looks after the health of Russia's cosmonauts: the Institute of Biomedical Problems. In 2010 it had, inside one of its buildings, a pretend spaceship. It was four steel tubes joined by short tunnels, with wooden walls inside, and all together it had about as much room in it as a large house: 550 cubic metres. One tube had six tiny bedrooms, a kitchen and a living room. One was a sick bay. One was a store, with freezers, a greenhouse and a gym. The fourth was a lander, and above it was a big dark room with sand and rocks on the floor, which was Mars.

On June 3rd 2010 six men went into the tubes and the hatch was shut behind them. It stayed shut for 520 days, until November 4th 2011. That is about as long as it would take to fly to Mars, stay a month, and fly home. The men would not see the sun, breathe fresh air, or meet anyone else for all that time. Nothing could be sent in. All the food for 520 days was already inside.

The point was to find out whether people could stand a trip to Mars and back: whether they would stay healthy, stay sane, and keep doing their work. That had never been tested, because nobody had ever been away that long. The longest anyone had spent in space was 437 days, by a Russian doctor called Valery Polyakov, in the 1990s.

## The six

The crew were chosen from hundreds of people who applied. Three were Russian: Alexey Sitev, the commander, who had trained divers for the navy and cosmonauts for spacewalks; Sukhrob Kamolov, a surgeon, who was the crew's doctor; and Alexandr Smoleevskiy, an army doctor who would lead the landing on Mars. Two were chosen by the European Space Agency: Romain Charles, an engineer from France, and Diego Urbina, an engineer from Italy whose family came from Colombia. One, Wang Yue, came from China's astronaut centre, where he helped train astronauts. They were between twenty-seven and thirty-eight years old. They spoke to each other in English and Russian.

They were not just passengers. Nobody else could get into the box, so the crew had to run about a hundred experiments on themselves. They took each other's blood, scanned each other's arteries with ultrasound, stuck wires on each other's heads to record their brains, swabbed the walls for germs, collected every drop of their own urine for months, and filled in questionnaires every week about how they felt. Diego, asked which experiments he liked least, said the ones that hurt. Alexey said: "I do not like sleep investigations."

## Talking to Earth

The cleverest part of the pretend flight was the talking. For the first seven weeks the crew could phone the control room. Then, on July 26th 2010, the phone was switched off, because a real spaceship would be too far from Earth. From then on every message was delayed, as it would be in space, and the delay grew as the "distance" grew: up to twelve minutes each way by May 2011. So a question took twelve minutes to arrive, and the answer took twelve minutes to come back. The crew sent video messages and written notes, and waited. Their families came to the control room and recorded greetings. Schoolchildren sent videos. The delay shrank again on the way home, and on September 15th 2011 the crew could talk to Earth once more.

The control room watched the crew all the time. There were cameras in the kitchen, the living room and the corridor, though not in the bedrooms. Every morning and evening each man measured his blood pressure, his pulse, his temperature and his weight, and wrote down how he felt. Once a month there was a full medical check. And every week the institute put a bulletin on its website. The bulletins nearly always said the same thing: the equipment was working, and there were "no alterations of health state."

## The watches

One instrument asked the crew nothing at all. Each man wore a plastic watch on his wrist, day and night, for the whole 520 days. It was not really a watch. Inside it was a sensor that felt every movement of the wrist and counted it, once a minute, together with how bright the light was. A computer could later sort each minute into three kinds: awake and moving, awake and resting, or asleep. These watches had been supplied by a team of sleep scientists at the University of Pennsylvania, in America.

Once a week each man copied his watch's data onto a memory card, and the card went out of the box through the rubbish hatch, the only thing that ever left. In America the cards were checked to make sure nothing was missing, and put away. Nobody read them as a report on the crew while the crew was still inside. By the end the watches had recorded 4,396,333 minutes, which is where this story gets its name.

## Life inside

Life inside was a routine. Everyone got up at the same time, did the same medical checks with the same machines, and sat down to breakfast together. Romain wrote that the one narrow corridor had "a rush hour" every morning. Five days a week they worked on experiments and spent an hour in the gym; two days they had off. Each man took a turn on duty, going to the store at night with a barcode scanner to fetch the next day's food, which was mostly dried food in packets. The menu could not be changed, because scientists were studying what the food did to their bodies.

They broke up the routine however they could. They celebrated every birthday, with presents the families had hidden in the box before the start. They kept Halloween in home-made costumes. For Christmas, which the Russians did not usually celebrate, Romain and Diego built a tree out of cardboard boxes and used the wires from an experiment as fairy lights. For the Russian New Year, which the Russians celebrated like nothing else, everyone stayed up until two. For the Chinese New Year, Wang Yue, who practised Chinese calligraphy, painted six posters with a wish for each of the six.

On December 1st 2010 the lights went out. The ventilation stopped and the water pressure fell. The control room said the building's transformer was on fire. The crew sat in the kitchen under one lamp, moved all the frozen food into the one freezer the engineers could restart, ate cake and cereal, and went to bed in the dark. The power came back the next morning. That afternoon the crew were told it had all been a drill. Diego said afterwards that it was one of the best things that happened, because it was different.

## Mars

On February 1st 2011, after eight months of "flying," the crew reached "Mars." Alexey opened the hatches into the lander, which had been sealed all that time, and the crew found it stuffed with boxes from floor to ceiling: food, and presents from their families. They passed the boxes along the corridor by hand for two hours. Romain said it was the second-best day of the whole mission.

On February 8th three of them, Alexandr, Diego and Wang Yue, moved into the lander, and a few days later the hatch between them and the other three was closed. On February 14th Alexandr and Diego put on real Russian spacesuits, made lighter for the purpose, and walked out onto the sand of the pretend Mars for an hour and twelve minutes, while the real Russian mission control, outside Moscow, watched on television. There were two more walks, on February 18th and 22nd. They drove a little rover and planted sensors in the sand. Diego wrote that the spacesuits were "fantastic." On February 27th the lander "came back," the hatch was opened, and the six were together again for the long flight home.

## The long way home

The way home was the hard part, and everyone said so. There was nothing new to do. The experiments were the same experiments, done for the twentieth time. The tasty things in the store ran out: the tinned tuna in the first month, the chocolate bars by the fourth. In April the control room pretended that all communication had broken down, and sent nothing for a week, to see how the crew would manage on their own. They managed fine. August was the worst month, because their families were on holiday and sent fewer messages. Romain called August and September "dull."

But the crew did not fall out. At the one-year mark the institute asked them questions, and they all said the same thing: no quarrels, a good team. Romain wrote: "we never had any conflicts between us." Diego wrote: "I think we have always been one cohesive group." Alexey, asked what he missed most, said: "Fried meat." Wang Yue said he missed Chinese food.

When the phone was switched back on in September, everything got better. The crew started a countdown ten days before the end. And at two in the afternoon on November 4th 2011 the hatch was opened and the six walked out, smiling, into the television lights. Diego said: "we have accomplished on Earth the longest space voyage ever." Romain said he was proud to prove "that a human journey to the Red Planet is feasible." The scientists from the European Space Agency said the crew had done "surprisingly well." Everyone agreed: it had worked.

## What the watches said

Four hours after the hatch opened, the six took off their watches. Fourteen months later, in January 2013, the American scientists published what the watches had recorded. It was not the story anyone had told.

First, the whole crew had slowed down. Month by month they moved less and less. By the last quarter of the mission each man spent more than an hour a day less awake and moving than in the first quarter, and about half an hour more each day lying still, and slept about half an hour more each night. Altogether the six slept 673 hours more in the second half of the mission than in the first. They were not ill. They had just gone quiet, like animals in winter. The scientists used the word "torpor."

Second, one man's day had stretched. Our bodies have a clock inside that runs a little longer than twenty-four hours, and the sun resets it every morning. In the box there was no sun, only dim fluorescent lamps with very little blue light in them, and blue light is what the body clock listens to. One of the six, whom the scientists only ever called "crewmember b," stayed up late under the lamps in the first weeks, and his clock slipped. Within sixteen days he was falling asleep after four in the morning. Soon his day was not twenty-four hours long but about twenty-five. Every day his bedtime came an hour later, all the way round the clock and round again, so that some weeks he was asleep in the afternoon and awake all night. For about one hour in every six of the whole mission, he was asleep when everyone else was awake, or awake when everyone else was asleep. Another man had started taking long naps in the day, and the naps grew. Between them, the two were out of step with the rest of the crew for a fifth of the flight.

Third, one man was short of sleep the whole time. The scientists called him "crewmember f." He slept about six and a half hours a night, less than anyone else, and as the months went on it got worse, not better. Every week the crew did a three-minute test on a laptop: press a key the moment a yellow number appears. The test shows when someone is too tired to pay attention. This man made more than half of all the mistakes the whole crew made in 520 days, and in the videos the laptop took of their faces, his was the one with the drooping eyelids, thirty-nine times out of fifty-one.

The scientists never said which man was which, and nobody should guess. What matters is that four of the six had some problem with sleep, and the problems started in the first weeks and never went away. Two of the six, "c" and "d," had no problems at all, and they were the two the others talked to most.

The weekly questionnaires told a third story, in between the other two. On average, the crew said they were fine. But one man reported feeling low in nine weeks out of ten, and two men reported most of the arguments, mostly with the control room. The scientists had also tested, before the start, how much each man liked to make himself look good on paper. The man who admitted feeling low was the one least bothered about looking good. Two of the men who reported no problems at all were the most bothered. So the questionnaires may have missed things too.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A thing can work and still not be the whole story.** The crew came out healthy and on time, and they never fell out. That was true. The weekly bulletins said there was nothing wrong, and that was true too, for the things they were checking. But the watches, which nobody was reading, showed six men slowing down month by month, one man's day stretching to twenty-five hours, and one man too tired to pay attention. Both things were true at once. "Is the crew all right?" and "is the crew changing?" are different questions.

**Asking people how they are is a measurement too, and it has its own problems.** The crew said no quarrels and good sleep. Some of them were the kind of people who like to look good on paper, and the scientists knew this because they had measured it. A watch on the wrist cannot be polite. If you want to know how people in a system are doing, do not only ask them.

**The record nobody reads while it runs is the one that tells you most afterwards.** The memory cards went out with the rubbish every week and were put in a drawer, because they were for a scientific paper, not for running the mission. So nobody noticed one man's bedtime slipping an hour a night, and nobody changed the lamps. The lesson is not that the watchers were lazy. It is that someone has to be given the job of reading the quiet instrument while there is still time to do something about it.

**Six people are six different experiments.** Two of the crew were untouched by 520 days in a box. Four were affected, each in a different way, and each from the start. The man with the twenty-five-hour day had passed every test before the mission, because at home, with the sun, his clock worked fine. The box changed him, and you could only have found that out in the box.

So the lesson is simple. When a sealed system comes out looking fine, go and find the instrument that was not asking anyone how they felt, and read what it says.
