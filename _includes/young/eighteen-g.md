## The number nobody had measured

After the Second World War, the people who designed aircraft for the American armed forces worked to a rule. A human body, the rule said, could stand about eighteen g. One g is the pull of gravity, the force that holds you to the ground. Eighteen g means being pushed eighteen times as hard as that, which is what happens for a moment when a fast aircraft hits the ground and stops. So seats, belts and cockpits were built to hold together at about eighteen g, and no stronger, because if the pilot could not survive more, a stronger seat was a waste of weight.

The trouble was that nobody had measured eighteen. It was a number that everyone knew and nobody had read off an instrument. During the war some pilots had walked away from crashes that should have killed them, and others had died in gentle ones because the seat or the belt broke. The number did not fit the facts. But the number was what the aircraft were built to.

## The doctor who rode the sled

John Paul Stapp was born in Brazil on July 11th 1910, the son of an American missionary. He grew up to be a doctor, a biologist and a physicist all at once, which took him a long time: he finished medical school in 1944, when he was nearly thirty-four. He joined the Army Air Forces as a flight surgeon, a doctor who looks after fliers, and in 1946 he was sent to the Aero Medical Laboratory at Wright Field in Ohio, where the Air Force studied what flying did to the body. His first job was to test oxygen equipment at 45,000 feet. He tested it on himself.

In March 1947 he was given a new project. Find out how much deceleration a human body can really take, so that seats can be built to the true number. Deceleration is slowing down. A crash is the fastest slowing down there is.

## The Gee Whiz

You cannot crash aircraft on purpose with people in them. So the Air Force paid a company, Northrop Aircraft, to build something that could crash in a controlled way: a sled on a railway track in the California desert, at a base called Muroc that is now Edwards Air Force Base. The track was 2,000 feet long. The sled weighed about 1,500 pounds, was pushed by rockets, and was stopped at the far end by forty-five pairs of brakes that gripped two steel keels under it, like vices. By choosing how many brakes would bite, the crew could decide how hard the stop would be. A run took five seconds of rocket to reach about 180 miles an hour, half a second of coasting, and then a stop in about twenty-six feet. The crew called the sled the Gee Whiz.

The first rider was meant to be a dummy, a man-sized doll weighing 185 pounds, called Oscar Eightball. Oscar found out what could go wrong. Once the brakes failed and the sled ran off into the desert. Once the harness broke, and Oscar went through an inch-thick wooden windscreen and flew 110 feet before he landed between the rails. Stapp decided on his first day that the dummy was not enough. The project was supposed to find a human limit, and he would be the human.

He rode for the first time on December 10th 1947, sitting backwards, with one rocket, to about ninety miles an hour. The next day he used three rockets. By May 1948 he had ridden sixteen times and had been stopped at thirty-five g, nearly twice the number the Air Force had been building to. He was not dead. He was not even badly hurt.

The rides did hurt. When he faced backwards and the stop was hard, the blood left the front of his head and he went blind for a moment, a white-out. In 1949 the seat was turned round to face forward, and now the blood rushed the other way. He cracked ribs. He lost the fillings from his teeth. He broke his right wrist twice; the second time, in 1950, he set the bone himself on the walk back to his office. Each injury was a measurement. The project was looking for the point at which a body is hurt in a way that will heal, and the only way to find that point is to reach it.

In June 1951 he made his hardest ride at Edwards. The sled went into the brakes at about 155 miles an hour and came out at about 34, in thirty-one feet. An instrument on his chest read 46.2 g. The tiny blood vessels at the back of his eyes burst, and for a month he had blind spots in his sight. Then they cleared. He had taken two and a half times the number the aircraft were built to, and he had healed.

## A new question

By then the aircraft had changed again. In 1947 a pilot had flown faster than sound for the first time, and the new jet fighters could do it too. If a pilot had to bail out at that speed, the wind would hit him like a wall, and then he would slow down very hard. Could a man live through that in an open ejection seat, which fires the seat and the pilot out of the aircraft? Or would every supersonic aircraft need a heavy sealed capsule to throw the pilot out in?

The Gee Whiz could not answer that. It was too slow and its brakes were too sudden. So in 1953 Stapp moved to Holloman Air Force Base in New Mexico, where there was a longer track, 3,550 feet, built to launch missiles, with a water brake at the end. Scoops under the sled dipped into a trough of water, and the amount of water decided how hard and how long the stop would be. Northrop built him a new sled called Sonic Wind No. 1, with room for nine rockets.

He rode it first on March 19th 1954, to 421 miles an hour, faster than anyone had ever travelled on the ground. The stop was twenty-two g. On August 20th he rode again, this time to test the wind. Doors on the front of the sled flew open at speed and the air hit him all at once, as it would hit a pilot whose canopy had gone. He wore a helmet that covered his whole head. He called it the easiest ride he had made.

## December 10th 1954

For the big ride there would be no windscreen at all. The straps were the thing being tested, so they are worth listing. A wide belt across his lap. Shoulder straps. A strap behind his back that pulled his elbows in, because above 400 miles an hour the wind can flail a loose arm hard enough to break it. Straps above and below his knees, with his wrists tied to the top one. A chest strap so tight that he could breathe only with his diaphragm, the muscle under the lungs. A rubber block between his teeth with a measuring instrument inside it. The helmet was tied to the headrest so that the wind could not twist his neck.

Nine rockets fired. In five seconds the sled covered 2,800 feet and reached 632 miles an hour, nine-tenths of the speed of sound. A jet trainer was flying down the track above him, with a young pilot named Joseph Kittinger at the controls, and the sled passed it. The wind on Stapp's body was about two tons. Then the sled coasted for half a second and hit the water.

It stopped in 1.4 seconds. The instrument on his chest drew the stop as a line: a jump at the start to thirty-five g, a long shelf at twenty-five g that lasted more than a second, and a spike up to forty g. Nobody had ever held so much force for so long. His own account, given to a magazine a few weeks later, was this. "It went from black to yellow, I saw the water splash when I hit the brake, and then came the redout. After that I didn't see a cockeyed thing."

The redout was his eyes filling with blood. The stop had driven the blood in his head forward, and the small vessels in the whites of his eyes had burst. He said the pain was like having a tooth pulled without anaesthetic. When the sled stood still, he could see nothing but a shimmering pink. He lifted an eyelid with his fingers and still saw nothing. The crew ran to him. The Northrop engineer, George Nichols, remembered it years later: "His eyes had hemorrhaged and were completely filled with blood. It was horrible. Absolutely horrible."

After a minute or two Stapp saw blue specks. After about eight minutes the specks joined up into sky and clouds. A surgeon held up fingers and he counted them, and then he knew that the backs of his eyes had not torn loose and that he would not be blind. He had been blind for eight and a half minutes. He had, he said, two of the finest black eyes any man ever had. In the hospital he ate a large meal and spent two hours with the film crews.

## What the instrument said

Stapp was the instrument. The dials on the sled could measure the force, but only he could say what the force did to a person, and whether a person would go again. The rule the project used was this: a volunteer stops at the first injury that will heal. And whether it will heal is something you only find out afterwards, by waiting.

The ride proved two things. The wind at 632 miles an hour, on a man who was properly strapped and helmeted, did almost nothing to him; it was, as the Air Force's report put it, negligible beside the stop. And the stop, twenty-five g for more than a second, was harder than any bail-out from a jet was expected to be. A pilot could leave a supersonic aircraft in an open seat and live, if the seat held his head and his arms and legs.

The Air Force released the news on December 27th 1954, as a speed record. Stapp told the reporters, by telephone, that the record was only a side effect. "My eyeballs became uncaged, but there was no impairment of thinking," he said. In 1955 the Air Force gave him its Cheney Award for valour, and in September 1955 his face was on the cover of *Time* magazine as the fastest man on earth. He wanted one more ride, at a thousand miles an hour, on a longer track. He never got it. His commanders decided he was too valuable to risk, and in June 1956 the Sonic Wind flew off its rails in a test with nobody aboard. After that the sled carried chimpanzees.

## The seat belt

Stapp had noticed something else. In 1954 the Air Force lost about 700 men in aircraft crashes and about 630 in car crashes. The two kinds of crash were the same kind of physics. So in 1955, at Holloman, he began crashing cars.

The cars came from the base scrapyard. They were driven into walls and into each other with dummies inside, rolled over, and stopped short by a cable. Pigs under anaesthetic were swung into steering wheels, to show that a padded wheel that gave way did less harm than a hard one. Volunteers sat on a seat with only a lap belt and were stopped hard, to find out how much a belt could be allowed to pull before it hurt too much. Four men in turn were stopped from eighteen miles an hour in six inches, taking 4,800 pounds on the belt. "We used four different subjects," Stapp told Congress later, "because none of them wanted to volunteer for it a second time."

On May 17th 1955 the laboratory crashed a car in front of engineers from the car companies, who had come at the request of the Society of Automotive Engineers, which had just set up a committee on seat belts. That meeting became the first of a yearly conference on car crashes that still carries Stapp's name. He said in public, whenever he could, that to drive without a belt was "negligent suicide", and he wore one in his own car.

In April 1966 Stapp went before a committee of the House of Representatives that was writing a law about car safety. He showed them the film of the Gee Whiz. He showed them that in the armed forces, over twenty years, accidents had killed about 97,000 people and everything else together about 28,000. And he did the sum for them. The four tons that had stopped him from 155 miles an hour in thirty-one feet would stop a man from sixty miles an hour in three feet, which is about how far the front of a car crumples against a wall. A man could walk away from that, he said, if nothing fell on him and if his straps held. The law was signed on September 9th 1966, with Stapp in the room, and from the 1968 models every new car sold in America had to have seat belts.

## After

Stapp went on finding people who would test a limit on themselves. In 1957 he chose Joseph Kittinger, the pilot who had flown the chase plane over the Sonic Wind, to fly a balloon to 96,000 feet, and in 1959 and 1960 his laboratory sent Kittinger up again to jump from the edge of space. That story is in "[The Right Glove](/experiments/2026/10/20/the-right-glove.html)".

Stapp retired from the Air Force in 1970 and ran his crash conference for the rest of his life. He died at home in New Mexico on November 13th 1999, aged eighty-nine. His sled, the Sonic Wind, is in the National Air and Space Museum in Washington. The Gee Whiz is in the Air Force's museum in Ohio.

One last thing happened to his numbers. The 46.2 g that he measured on his chest at Edwards in 1951 is now written, in almost every book and on museum labels, as the force of the ride in December 1954, which the Air Force's own records put at twenty-five g with peaks of forty. The count of his rides is given as twenty-nine, twenty-eight, twenty-six and twenty-one in different official places. The reading had left the laboratory and turned into the kind of number that eighteen had been.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Find out where a number came from.** Everyone knew that a body could take eighteen g. Seats and belts were built to it. But nobody had ever measured it, and the first time anyone did, the real number turned out to be more than twice as big. A number that has been built to for years can still be a guess.

**Sometimes the only instrument is a person.** A dial can measure a force. It cannot tell you whether a person can stand it, or whether they would go again. Stapp used his own body as the instrument, and the readings came out as cracked ribs, broken wrists and bleeding eyes. The rule was to stop at the first injury that would heal, and you only know that it healed by waiting.

**A good reading changes what gets built.** Once the true number was known, the Air Force built seats that faced backwards, stronger harnesses, and open ejection seats for supersonic jets. Then the same numbers went into cars, as seat belts, padded dashboards and doors that stayed shut. The law of 1966 came from readings taken on a sled.

**Numbers keep moving after you read them.** Stapp's 46.2 g belonged to one ride, and it has been pinned to a different one ever since. Even the count of his rides is given four ways. A reading is only as good as the care people take in passing it on.

So the lesson is simple. When everyone agrees on a number, ask who measured it. If nobody did, somebody has to, and then everybody has to copy it down right.
