## The man

Alfred Wegener was a German scientist who studied the weather. He is famous today for a different idea: that the continents slowly drift about on the surface of the Earth. In 1930 most scientists thought that idea was wrong, and it has nothing to do with this story, except that it made him well known.

He loved Greenland. Greenland is a huge island near the North Pole, and almost all of it is covered by an ice sheet: a sheet of ice so thick that it buries mountains, and so wide that the middle of it is 400 kilometres from the nearest coast. In 1930 Wegener was forty-nine, a professor in Austria, and he went back for the fourth time, to run the biggest experiment of his life.

## The plan

Nobody had ever spent a winter on the Greenland ice sheet. Nobody knew how cold it got in the middle, or how thick the ice was, or how much snow fell there in a year. Wegener wanted to find out. His plan was to build three weather stations in a line across Greenland: one on the west coast, one on the east coast, and one right in the middle of the ice, 400 kilometres from each coast and about 3,000 metres above the sea. All three would take readings every day for a whole year, from August 1930 to August 1931. The middle station was called Eismitte, which is German for "Ice-Middle".

Two or three men would have to live at Eismitte all winter. They needed a house, fuel, food and instruments, and every bit of it had to be carried 400 kilometres across the ice. Dogs pulling sledges could do it, but slowly: the round trip took about a month. Wegener had a better idea. He had two motor sledges built in Finland. Each was six metres long, with a cabin for two people and an aeroplane engine at the back turning a propeller. They were called *Schneespatz*, "Snow Sparrow", and *Eisbär*, "Polar Bear". Nobody had ever tried one on the deep snow of an ice sheet.

So the experiment had three parts. Would a year of readings tell them about the weather in the middle of the ice? Would explosions and their echoes tell them how thick the ice was? And, though nobody wrote this part down as a question, could the middle station be supplied?

## What went wrong first

The ice went wrong first. The expedition's ship reached Greenland in May 1930 and could not get through the sea ice to the place where they wanted to land. They waited for seven weeks. Then everything had to be carried up a glacier (a river of ice flowing down from the ice sheet) to the edge of the ice sheet, 950 metres up, on the backs of Icelandic ponies.

Johannes Georgi, the scientist who was to run Eismitte, went in first with dog sledges and reached the middle of the ice on July 30th 1930. He started taking readings. He was alone there for six weeks. Two more dog-sledge trips brought him supplies, and the second one brought his companion Ernst Sorge on September 13th. But the house had not come, and most of the paraffin, the fuel for heating and light, had not come either. Those were the heavy things that the motor sledges were supposed to carry.

The motor sledges were not working yet. They had been carried up the glacier in pieces and put together at the edge of the ice. On August 31st Wegener and his engineer Curt Schif tried to drive them in loaded for the first time, and had to turn back in fog and a snowstorm. Then it snowed, and snowed again.

## The diary

Wegener kept a diary, and the pages from the first week of September 1930 still exist. They are the record of a man finding out that his machines could not do what he had built the whole plan around.

On September 2nd twenty centimetres of soft new snow lay over the whole ice sheet. "Introducing propeller sledges into polar research is really a hard birth," he wrote, and, "we must be glad if we succeed at all in getting everything necessary 400 kilometres in." On September 4th they drove into a storm, and on the steepest slope the Snow Sparrow stopped dead with its engine at full power. The engines, he worked out, were too weak: 100 horsepower, when the Finnish sledges they were copied from had 150, and his were bigger and loaded with freight.

That evening he did the sums. About 2,700 kilograms of supplies were already at Eismitte, carried in by the dogs. About 4,000 kilograms were still lying at the edge of the ice, and 2,500 of them had to go in. He wrote that the danger "that our station Eismitte fails is painfully great".

On September 5th the sledges made their first real run, 80 kilometres in and 80 back. The next morning he wrote: "What now? Now the catastrophe is here. It is impossible to equip the central firn station in the way intended." The machines' range, he worked out, was only half what had been calculated, so Eismitte was twice as far away as they could manage.

He asked himself whether Georgi and Sorge should be brought back out and the station given up, and answered himself in the same entry: "We cannot take the station's crew back!" The supplies would have to go in by dogs, and he wrote down how many sledges that would take: "we need 15!" The last page of the diary that survives is dated September 10th. It says: "The weather is magnificent."

## The note

The dog-sledge party that had just come back from Eismitte brought a note from Georgi and Sorge. It said that if no more supplies and instruments reached them by October 20th, they would give up the station and walk out to the coast, pulling their sledges by hand. Wegener thought that two men walking 400 kilometres in the Greenland winter would die. So he decided to take the supplies in himself, by dog sledge, and to get there before October 20th.

He left the west station on September 21st 1930 with fifteen sledges, 2,000 kilograms of supplies, Fritz Loewe, a scientist, and thirteen Greenlanders, the local people who drove the dogs.

The snow that had stopped the motor sledges stopped the dogs too. At the depot at 62 kilometres, nine of the Greenlanders turned back because the sledges could not get through. By October 6th, after sixteen days, Wegener had reached only 151 kilometres. The supplies could not be carried any further, so he left them in the snow and sent back three more Greenlanders. That was the last anyone on the coast heard of him.

Three men went on: Wegener, Loewe, and a young Greenlander called Rasmus Villumsen, who was twenty-two. They were carrying nothing now but their own food. The point of going on was to reach Georgi and Sorge before they walked out, or at least to put food in the depot at 200 kilometres for them to find. The cold went down to 54 degrees below zero. On October 30th, after forty days, they reached Eismitte with three sledges and twenty dogs. The last load had been dropped ten kilometres short.

Georgi and Sorge were still there. The cold and the deep snow had kept them from leaving on October 20th, and anyway, by then, they had dug themselves a cave in the firn, the old packed snow under the surface, and moved into it on October 5th. It was warmer than a tent. Wegener and Villumsen arrived in good health. Loewe's toes were all frozen.

## The hole

The station was a hole in the snow. Georgi had dug a small room two metres down for his barometer, and Sorge had dug a bigger one. They hung sacks and reindeer skins over the entrance to keep the cold out, poked a hole through the roof for air, cut bunks out of the snow and used the empty food boxes as a table.

Georgi's report, written in May 1931, gives the sums for the winter. There was enough food, because every box meant to last 28 meals could be made to last 30. But there was only enough paraffin for one litre a day, for heating, cooking and light together. That was not enough to run the stove, so they put the stove away and lived on the warmth of the two lamps and the cooker. The floor of the cave was 15 to 20 degrees below zero. They did all their work sitting in their sleeping bags.

Loewe's feet were so badly frozen that Georgi had to cut off all the toes on his right foot and four on his left, with a pocket knife, tin shears and a hand saw. There was nothing to put him to sleep. Loewe lay in his sleeping bag for the whole winter. He could not leave with Wegener.

The roof began to sink after Christmas, under tonnes of new snow. The men had to dig the floor deeper to keep the room tall enough to sit up in, and in the end they built a pillar of packed snow in the middle to hold the roof up.

## The birthday

Wegener stayed a day and a half. There was food for three men until the end of May, with stretching, but not comfortably for five. On November 1st 1930, his fiftieth birthday, he and Villumsen set off back to the coast with two sledges, seventeen dogs and 135 kilograms of food for men and dogs. Sorge wrote later that Wegener was cheerful, and that the idea of all five men staying "was hardly mentioned in the conversations, as if it were out of the question".

Nobody could talk to them after that. The radio transmitter that was meant for Eismitte had been left behind at the coast to make room for more important supplies. From November 1st, nobody at the station and nobody at the coast could know anything about the two men until a sledge got through in the spring.

At the coast a party went out to the depot at 62 kilometres on the days Wegener had named and waited until December 7th. Nobody came. They decided that he must have stayed at Eismitte for the winter, and radioed to Germany that nothing could be known for certain before the end of April.

## The spring

On May 7th 1931 the three men in the cave saw two black dots on the western horizon, coming fast. They were the motor sledges. On the hard snow of spring, the Snow Sparrow and the Polar Bear had driven the whole 400 kilometres, the trip they could not make in September. Sorge ran out and hugged the mechanic, and both men asked at the same moment, "Is Wegener here?" Nobody answered.

Sorge and Weiken, the surveyor, searched the route as they went back west. At 255 kilometres they found Wegener's sledge: from there the two men had gone on with one. At 189 kilometres Wegener's skis were standing upright in the snow, three metres apart, with a broken ski stick between them. On May 12th they dug down a metre and found a tent site, and in it a body carefully sewn into two sleeping-bag covers, with a reindeer skin laid over it. It was Wegener. His clothes were dry and in good order, so he had not frozen to death. Sorge thought his heart had given out from the effort of skiing. His diaries were not there. They buried him again as they had found him, built a vault of snow blocks over him, and put his skis back in their holes with a black flag on each. The companion piece [*The grave*](/deeper/fifteen-sledges/the-grave/#young) tells it in Sorge's words.

Villumsen had buried him. Then he had gone on west alone, with the sledge, the dogs and the diaries. Searchers found the places where he had camped at 171 and 175 kilometres, and at 155 kilometres they found where his dogs had lain down. After that there was nothing. The dogs, with the wind on one side, had probably drifted off the marked route. He was never found. He had turned twenty-three on November 17th, if he was still alive.

## The readings

The station kept going. Georgi stayed at Eismitte, for the last ten weeks of the year alone, and the readings ran right through to August 6th 1931, a few days past the planned year. Wegener's brother Kurt came out to take charge, and the explosions were set off that summer to measure the ice. The ice in the middle of Greenland turned out to be about 2,700 metres thick, sitting on rock not far above sea level.

The readings are still there. Georgi's hourly measurements, 26,292 of them, are in a public data archive on the internet. They say that the year in the middle of Greenland averaged 30 degrees below zero; that February 1931 averaged about 47 below; and that on March 20th 1931 it reached 64.8 degrees below zero, the lowest reading of the year. Sorge dug a shaft fifteen metres deep beside the cave and measured how the snow packed down, year by year, into ice. The rule he worked out from that, called Sorge's law, is still used to read the age of ice from the layers in it.

Back in Germany, people wanted someone to blame. Georgi had written the note that set the date. Wegener's brother blamed him in print, and the quarrel went on in the courts until 1937. Seventy years later a historian went through all the papers and decided that nobody could be blamed on their own.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Do not build your plan on a machine you have never tried.** The whole station depended on two motor sledges that had only ever run on flat frozen sea. On the slope of the ice sheet, in deep snow, they could go only half as far as the sums had said. Eismitte was put 400 kilometres in because the machines were supposed to manage 400 kilometres. If anyone had tested them on that snow first, the station would have been put somewhere the sledges could reach.

**Do the sums early, and believe them.** Wegener did the sums on September 4th and wrote the answer down: the station could not be supplied that autumn. That was the instrument speaking, and he read it correctly. The trouble is what he did next. He turned the missing supplies into a journey, and the journey arrived with nothing. The sums had been right.

**A message from far away is already out of date when it arrives.** Georgi and Sorge wrote "October 20th or we walk" in September, in a tent, when they were cold and short of fuel. By October 20th they were in a cave and had decided to stay. Wegener spent his whole autumn, and then his life, answering a note that no longer meant what it said. Neither end could tell the other, because the radio had been left behind.

**Count the people as well as the kilograms.** Wegener counted the food at the station and the food on his sledges and the days to the coast. The sums came out: three men could stay, two must go, and twenty kilometres a day would do it. The thing the sums did not count was the two men who went. He was fifty years old and had already crossed 400 kilometres of deep snow once that autumn, and the second time he did not get through.

**Keep the readings.** Georgi stayed in the hole until the year was finished. The readings were kept every hour, and ninety years later anyone can look at them. The men who kept them were blamed and forgotten. The data outlived all of that. The part of the experiment that worked was the part the people in the cave kept doing after the plan had failed.

So the lesson is simple. Test the thing you depend on before you depend on it, and when the sums say the plan has failed, change the plan rather than paying for it yourself.
