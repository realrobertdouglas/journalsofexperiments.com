## The morning of October 13th 1947

Just before noon, a big four-engined bomber called a B-17 was flying over the Atlantic Ocean, about 350 miles east of Jacksonville, Florida. It was nearly four miles up, at the level of the tops of a thin sheet of cloud. Below it and around it, thirty to fifty miles away, was the wall of a hurricane. A second B-17 followed with cameras. A bigger bomber, a B-29, flew behind them both. On board the B-29 was a scientist from the General Electric company called Vincent Schaefer.

At 11:38 a machine in the first B-17 began to pour crushed dry ice out into the cloud. Dry ice is frozen carbon dioxide, a solid so cold that it burns your fingers. The plane flew in a straight line for thirty minutes and 110 miles, dropping about 80 pounds of it. On the way, the crew also threw two 50-pound loads into one tall cloud. Then all three planes turned round and flew back to look. The sheet of cloud they had seeded had broken up into scattered snow clouds over about 300 square miles. They did not fly into the eye of the storm. They went home to Tampa, landed at four in the afternoon, and nobody went back to look again.

The hurricane was heading out to sea, away from America. Two days later, early on October 15th, it came ashore fifteen miles south of Savannah, Georgia, with gusts of wind of ninety-five miles an hour.

## The people

The idea came from the General Electric Research Laboratory in Schenectady, New York. Its most famous scientist was Irving Langmuir, who had won the Nobel Prize for chemistry in 1932. His partner was Vincent Schaefer. A younger scientist, Bernard Vonnegut, worked with them. (His brother Kurt became a famous writer.)

In 1946 Schaefer made a discovery. A cloud that is colder than freezing is usually not made of ice. It is made of tiny water droplets that have not frozen, because there is nothing in them to freeze on. Scientists call this "supercooled" water. Schaefer had a home freezer in the laboratory, lined with black velvet, and he would breathe into it to make a little cloud. One hot July day the freezer was not cold enough, so he put in a lump of dry ice to cool it. In an instant the cloud turned into millions of ice crystals. A tiny grain of dry ice did the same. So did a sewing needle dipped in liquid air. Anything cold enough would do it.

On November 13th 1946 Schaefer went up in a rented plane and dropped about three pounds of dry ice into a cloud over Pittsfield, Massachusetts. Snow fell out of the cloud. He wrote in his notebook that he shook hands with the pilot and said, "We did it!" Vonnegut then found that a chemical called silver iodide would do the same job, because its crystals are shaped like ice.

In December 1946 Schaefer seeded a cloud and that afternoon the heaviest snowstorm of the winter fell on Schenectady. The laboratory did not claim it had made the storm. But the company's lawyers got worried. If a cloud-seeding experiment did damage, General Electric could be sued. So the company made a deal with the Army, the Navy and the Air Force. From February 1947 the government would own the planes and run every flight. General Electric would do only the laboratory work. The scientists were told they must not direct any flight. The project was called Project Cirrus.

## What they wanted to know

Langmuir had a theory. When water freezes it gives off heat. So seeding a cloud would warm it a little, the warm air would rise, and the rising air would spread the ice crystals and make more cloud. A few pounds of dry ice would not be the energy of the storm. It would be the trigger. Langmuir liked to compare it to a match: a very large forest can be set on fire by a single match.

The government agencies hoped that one day they could steer hurricanes, or weaken them. But the hurricane flight of 1947 was never given a clear question. Schaefer said later that "the main reason we made the flight was to see what a hurricane was made up of on top." Could the clouds in a hurricane be seeded at all? That was the test. Nobody wrote down what they expected the storm to do afterwards.

And there was a problem that nobody could fix. A good experiment needs a comparison. If you seed one cloud, you can look at the cloud next to it that you did not seed. If you test a medicine, you give it to some people and not to others. Scientists call the thing you leave alone the "control". But a hurricane is one thing, in one place, once. There was no second hurricane to leave alone. Whatever the storm did, there would be only one result, and no way to know what the storm would have done without the dry ice.

## The storm

The hurricane was born in the Caribbean Sea on October 10th 1947. It crossed Cuba, grew into a hurricane in the Gulf of Mexico, and on the night of October 11th crossed the southern tip of Florida. It did not do much wind damage, because another hurricane had wrecked that coast a month before. But it poured rain on ground that was already flooded. Most of south Florida, the Weather Bureau wrote, "resembled a great lake."

The Project Cirrus planes flew down from New York, to Alabama and then to MacDill Field at Tampa, Florida. By the time they arrived the storm was out in the Atlantic, moving north-east, and the forecasters expected it to keep going out to sea. The flight was planned for Monday morning, October 13th, when the storm would be 300 to 400 miles from the coast.

The man in charge of the flight was a Navy officer, Lieutenant Commander Daniel Rex. His report said the storm had an eye about thirty miles across, with a thick wall of cloud around it, and thin sheets of cloud spreading out from the wall. The planes could not go into the eye, because their homing equipment had failed and they might have lost each other. So they seeded the thin sheet of cloud outside the wall. The seeded cloud turned to snow, exactly as the freezer experiments said it would. The report said the storm was surprisingly mild where they flew: "It was no storm at all in much of the area but seemed more like relatively fair weather."

That night the ships at sea sent in too few reports, and the Weather Bureau lost track of the storm. On October 14th the Bureau thought the storm had two centres. One plane found winds of 80 miles an hour. Then the storm moved west, straight at the coast. At seven in the morning on October 15th it came ashore just south of Savannah. About 1,500 houses were damaged in the city. Two National Guard planes were crushed in their hangars. The tide rose twelve feet above normal at Savannah Beach. One man, a lumberjack called Neill McCord, was killed when a tree fell on his cabin. The damage in Georgia and the Carolinas came to about three million dollars.

## Who was to blame?

The seeding flight had been secret. But the newspapers had known since September that scientists wanted to try "hurricane busting". A small Georgia newspaper, the *Brantley Enterprise*, printed the story on October 23rd under the headline "Did 'Dry Ice' Experiment Start Hurricane?" It said a "tight security lid" had been clamped on the flight, and that one scientist "discounted reports that the tests might have turned the storm suddenly toward the Georgia coast." The paper asked: "Is there a hurricane detective in the house?"

Langmuir gave a talk about the flight on November 17th 1947. He was honest about what nobody knew. "What did happen, nobody knows," he said. He showed a map of the storm's path, and on the same map he drew the path of another hurricane, from October 1906, which had turned back towards the coast in almost the same place. Hurricanes do this sometimes, he was saying. But he also said that with more knowledge "we should be able to abolish the evil effects of these hurricanes."

The head of the Weather Bureau, Francis Reichelderfer, thought that was a wild exaggeration, and said so at a meeting in Norway in 1948. When Langmuir asked him about it in 1949, Reichelderfer said he had needed to correct "the greatly exaggerated statements" Langmuir had made. Langmuir answered with his match and his forest.

Then Langmuir changed his mind about the 1906 hurricane. In 1953 he counted up 304 hurricanes from a book of old storms. Only 42 of them had ever made a sudden sharp turn. He worked out that the chance of a hurricane turning in any six-hour period was about one in 110. The 1947 storm had turned within six hours of the seeding. So, he said, there was "considerable evidence that seeding hurricanes does tend to change their behavior." That is the number in the title. It was Langmuir's substitute for the control he could not have.

But it was a strange kind of evidence. He had chosen the six hours after the storm turned inside them. And his number only showed how often hurricanes turn on their own. It said nothing about why this one did.

## The lawyers

Nobody sued General Electric. But in 1951 the United States Senate held hearings about controlling the weather, and the company's scientists were asked about the hurricane. A senator from Florida asked Schaefer what the results of the seeding were. Schaefer said: "We do not know."

The company's research director, C. G. Suits, said that nobody would dare to experiment on hurricanes any more, because of the risk of being sued. Another official said General Electric could have faced "several hundred million dollars' worth of suits" if the storm had turned the wrong way. The senator who ran the hearings summed up the whole experiment in a few words. The storm had been seeded out at sea, he said, and "because of some circumstance which no one understands, it immediately bent back and started up against the Georgia coast." Nobody knew whether the seeding had bent it back or not. "That is the problem."

Project Cirrus went on with other work for a few more years and ended in 1952. The company's final report on the 1947 work did not even describe the hurricane flight. No one seeded a hurricane again for fourteen years.

## The ship's log

In 1957 three forecasters from the Weather Bureau, Conrad Mook, Eugene Hoover and Robert Hoover, decided to find out where the hurricane had really been when the dry ice fell. The companion piece "[The log of the Lisieux](/deeper/one-in-a-hundred-and-ten/the-log-of-the-lisieux/#young)" tells their story.

They found that Langmuir's map had placed the storm using one radio report from one ship, a French cargo ship called the *Lisieux*. That report had put the storm in the wrong place. So the forecasters wrote to the French shipping company, which found the ship's written log and sent it from Paris. The log showed that the wind at the ship had been blowing from the east, not the west. That meant the storm was on the other side of the ship from where the map had put it. The storm had already begun to turn before the planes arrived.

The forecasters then collected the logs of every ship that had been near the storm and redrew its path. They found something else. A week earlier, on October 6th, a smaller storm had made the same turn in almost the same place and come ashore near Brunswick, Georgia. Nobody had seeded it. The weather maps showed why: a big area of high pressure had built up to the north-east, in the path the storms would have taken, and blocked them, so they turned west. Both storms, the forecasters wrote, had followed "the normal outcome" of the weather pattern that month.

Langmuir died in August 1957, the month after their paper came out.

## Afterwards

The next hurricane to be seeded was Esther, in September 1961, far out in the Atlantic. The Navy dropped silver iodide into the wall of the eye, and the eye changed shape for an hour and a half. The scientists wrote that whether the seeding had caused it "is of course open to question". That experiment led to a big government programme called Project Stormfury, which seeded four hurricanes between 1962 and 1983. It was given up when scientists found that most hurricanes do not have enough supercooled water for seeding to work on, and that hurricanes change shape and weaken by themselves all the time. As one hurricane scientist put it, that makes it "almost impossible to separate the effect (if any) of seeding from natural changes."

In 2013 the government's hurricane scientists went back over the 1947 storm with all the old records. They found that it had never split into two storms. There had just been too few ships to see where it was.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**An experiment needs a comparison.** When Schaefer seeded a cloud over Schenectady, he could look at the cloud next to it that he had not seeded. When the planes seeded the hurricane, there was no second hurricane to leave alone. So when the storm turned, nobody could say what it would have done by itself. Langmuir tried to invent a comparison afterwards, with his one in 110. The Weather Bureau found a real one, ten years later: the storm of October 6th, which nobody had seeded, and which turned the same way.

**If every result proves you right, you have not tested anything.** Langmuir said a single match can burn down a forest. That sounds clever, but think about it. If the storm turned, the match had caught. If the storm had not turned, the forest was wet. There was no result that could have shown Langmuir he was wrong. A real test is one that could fail.

**The thing that gets measured is the damage, not the cause.** The only instrument that reported on the seeding was the city of Savannah: broken houses, crushed planes, a lumberjack's cabin. That told everyone how hard the storm had hit. It could not tell anyone why the storm had come. The same thing happened to the rainmaker Charles Hatfield in 1916, in the story "[Four Billion Gallons](/experiments/2026/10/19/four-billion-gallons.html#young)": a flood proved nothing about who had caused it.

**The answer was in a book nobody had read.** The whole question of where the storm was when the dry ice fell was settled by a ship's log, written by hand on a French cargo ship and found ten years later in Paris. The scientists had three bombers, cameras and a weather station. What they needed was one ship's log, and the patience to go and get it.

**The most honest answer was the shortest.** Asked in the Senate what the seeding had done, Schaefer, who had been on the flight, said: "We do not know." That was true on the day, and it is still the best summary of the experiment.

So the lesson is simple. Before you try to change something that happens only once, ask how you will know whether you changed it. If there is no way to know, the experiment has no result, however big the storm.
