The main entry tells how the Vasa sank and what the court decided. This is what the court actually heard, person by person, told plainly. The full version, which the button at the top switches to, gives the whole of the surviving record in English, and the sources.

## The court

On September 5th 1628, four weeks after the Vasa sank, a court of seventeen men met in the royal castle in Stockholm. It was led by the admiral of the realm, Carl Carlsson Gyllenhielm, who was the king's half-brother. A prosecutor, called the fiscal, asked the questions. A clerk wrote down the answers.

The clerk's record has not survived whole. What we have is a copy, and the first page of the copy is missing, so the record begins in the middle of the first witness's answer. Nobody knows exactly what came before. The Vasa Museum has typed out the copy from the original in the Swedish National Archives, and everything below comes from that.

A few words need explaining. A ship is "crank" when it leans over too easily. "Ballast" is the heavy stone loaded into the bottom of a ship to keep her upright. The "upperworks" are the parts of a ship above the water. And the "certer" was the builder's list of measurements, the plan.

## The master of ordnance

The first witness we can read is Erik Jönsson. He was the army's master of ordnance, the officer in charge of guns, and the king had sent him from Prussia to command the ships. He said that no gun had come loose; all were tied down fast. When the ship began to lean he ran down to the guns, and the water came in through the gun ports. He shouted up for the sails to be let loose, and was told they already had been. Before he could climb back up, the water was so high that the ladder came loose, and he barely got out.

He said he did not know the ship's condition. But he was sure of one thing: even if she had not been under sail, she would still have gone over, because she was heavier above than below.

The admiral told him that he had been appointed a vice admiral and should have checked the ballast. Erik Jönsson answered that he was no captain or admiral and had never said he was. He was a gun officer. The captain and the master knew more about ballast than he did.

The fiscal said the builder would have loaded the ship a foot deeper if anyone had told him she was crank. Erik Jönsson asked how. The gun ports were only three and a half feet above the water.

## The lieutenant

Next came Petter Gierdsson, the lieutenant, who had fitted the ship's ropes and sails. He said he had not known the ship was crank; he only had to do with the rigging, and had not seen the ballast. The guns were all well tied down. The ship sat fourteen feet deep at the front and sixteen at the back, he said, and the gun ports were about four feet above the water.

He was asked whether he had not seen that she was crank before she sailed. He said that while she was still tied up, when some people walked together to one side of the deck, the ship leaned after them.

When she sailed, only four sails were up. When she started to lean, the men let all the ropes go, but the wind was so weak that it could not even pull the sails out, and the men had to push them out by hand. One man alone held a rope. He had never believed, he said, that any ship could go over in so little wind.

Asked why she went over, whether she was badly built or had too little ballast, he said he did not know. He did not understand shipbuilding.

## The master

Then came Jöran Matsson, the ship's master, who was in charge of the ballast. The fiscal accused him of not doing his job. Matsson said he had put in as much ballast as would fit. He had not trusted his men to do it well enough, so he went down with a lamp himself and packed in all he could.

He was asked whether he could tell the ship was crank. He said that when she sailed she leaned a little and came up again, and when she came to Tegelviken and began to lean, men were ordered to pull the guns in, but before they had got even one gun in, the ship went over.

Then he told the court about the test. The captain, Söfring, had told Admiral Fleming that the ship was crank. And when the ship lay at the crane below the castle, with her ballast in, and with Admiral Fleming watching, thirty men had been made to run from one side of the ship to the other. The first time, she leaned over by one plank. The second time, two planks. The third time, three planks. Then Fleming told them to stop. If they had run across again, she would have gone over. And the admiral had said that he wished the king were at home.

Was the ship fully loaded with ballast when she sailed? All but one boatload, Matsson said. And if they got her up again and loaded her right down to the gun ports, she would still go over. Why? Because the ship was narrow in the bottom and had no belly. He had told the admiral so. The admiral had told him he was putting in too much ballast, because the gun ports were coming too near the water. Matsson had answered, "God grant that she will yet stand right on her keel." The admiral said the builder had built ships before, and Matsson should not worry about it.

All the guns were tied fast, he said, except one little one that had no carriage and could not have mattered. And if the ship could not carry four sails in calm weather, how could she carry all her sails in a storm?

## The boatswain

Per Bertilsson, the high boatswain, was the senior sailor in charge of ropes and gear. The fiscal suggested he had been drunk. He said he had not been drunk; he had been to communion in church that morning. The ballast, he said, was as much as the ship could carry. She had not seemed crank while she lay still, but as soon as she was under sail she leaned, and letting the ropes go did not bring her up again.

## The builders

Hein Jacobsson, the shipbuilder, was asked why he had built the ship so narrow and badly that she had no belly to sit on. He said he had built her exactly to the plan Master Henrik had given him, on the king's orders. He had even made her a foot and five inches wider than the plan. And everyone said she was well built.

Arent de Groote, the merchant who had signed the contract, was asked the same. He said he had built to the plan he had agreed with the king, and in the style of a French ship he had seen in Holland, which he had shown the king. There was nothing wrong with the building; the problem must have been the ballast.

The fiscal said the builder had once told the captain that the ship was so steady she could sail without any ballast at all. The builder said he had never said that. With more ballast, he said, she would have been safe. The captain, Söfring, answered that with more ballast the drain holes and the gun ports would have been under water, and then the guns could not be used. And he said something more: the ship the builder was building now, the Vasa's sister, was built the same way, and he feared she would go the same way too. The builder said he had to build to the plan he was given.

De Groote said that if every shipbuilder in the world looked at the Vasa, they would say there was nothing wrong with her. So the fiscal asked: if there was nothing wrong with her, why did she tip over? The two men answered that they did not know. God might know.

Then de Groote said that he had told the king how long and how wide the ship would be, and the king had been pleased and wanted her so. The fiscal answered that even so, the men who understood ships should have told the king what she could carry.

The builder was asked whether he had a clear conscience about building such a ship. He said she was as well built as any ship could be. The fiscal replied with the sentence that is the court's real finding: the ship was well built, but not rightly proportioned.

## The yard master

One more builder was called, Johan Isbrandsson, the Crown's own master at the yard, who kept the old ships repaired. He could see no fault. She was as wide as the Kronan, another big ship, and seemed to him steadier. He thought it impossible that such a ship could go over. The court asked how high the Kronan's gun ports were above the water. About four feet, he said. So a captain was sent across the harbour to measure them. It was the only measurement the court ever made.

## The experts

At the end of the record, eight experts who had been asked to sit with the court gave their opinions, one after another. Nearly all said the same thing. The ship was too heavy above the water and too light below. The hold, the bottom part of the ship, was too small, and should have been two feet deeper so that more ballast could go in. The bottom was built too sharp and too narrow to carry the heavy top. "Therefore the fault was in the building," one of them said.

One expert, Master Wellam, who was really Willem de Besche, an ironmaster from Finspång, said that a shipbuilder ought to work out beforehand how high a ship should stand above the water. And he explained the problem with a picture. Put a pound of lead in one pan of a pair of scales and a pound of feathers in the other. Pull the lead pan down and let it go: the feathers will lift it back up. That was the Vasa. The weight in the bottom was too light.

That is where the copy ends. The record was read to the king's council on September 11th 1628. No verdict was written down. Nobody was punished.
