## The first attempts

The king's council did not wait for the inquest. On August 13th 1628, three days after the ship sank, it gave an Englishman called Ian Bulmer permission to try to raise her, and told everyone at the shipyard to help him. He was to be paid nothing until he had done it. The council also wrote to Willem de Besche, an ironmaster who had once raised a loaded ship from forty metres down, and asked him to come and check Bulmer's method.

Bulmer worked fast. Within a week he had pulled the Vasa upright on the bottom, using an old warship moored above her as a lifting platform. She had sunk lying on her side; now she stood on her keel, as she still did when the divers found her more than three hundred years later. Then de Besche took over, rigged seventeen heavy ropes and chains, and tried to wind her up. In October a councillor wrote that she lay in her bed "and would not even stir".

Admiral Fleming came home from the war and took charge. He wrote to the king that when the men had pulled with all their strength, the hooks and anchors fixed in the gun ports had let go, and that divers were needed. The king had three divers sent from the far eastern edge of Sweden. They were fishermen by trade, and in the summer of 1629 they were paid a hundred daler for three months' work, with a little money for brandy. In June 1629 Fleming wrote to the king that there was "greater difficulty than I have at any time been able to presume". After that the Crown gave up.

## The guns

What ropes could not do, a diving bell could. A diving bell is like a big upside-down bucket, weighted with lead and lowered on a rope. The air trapped inside it goes down with it, so a man standing on a platform underneath can breathe and work for a while. In the 1660s two men, Albrecht von Treileben and Andreas Peckell, used one to go down to the wreck for her bronze guns, which were worth a great deal of money.

An Italian priest named Francesco Negri watched them in October 1663. He wrote in his diary that the diver went down dressed all in leather, with double boots of animal hide, standing on a lead platform under the bell. He was told the man could stay down half an hour. It was late October, and after about fifteen minutes the bell was pulled up and the diver came out shivering. Working in thirty metres of water in near darkness, the divers broke up most of the upper deck to reach the guns and brought up more than fifty of them, each weighing over a tonne.

## Forgotten

After that the Vasa was forgotten. People knew roughly where she had sunk, but not exactly. In 1920 a professor who wrote about her in a newspaper put her on the wrong side of the channel. The same year two brothers asked for permission to blow up the wreck so they could sell the old black oak, which was prized for furniture. They were refused. That refusal is the reason there is a ship to see today.

## The search

Anders Franzén was an engineer in the Swedish navy who was interested in old wrecks. He had a theory. In most seas, a wooden wreck is eaten by shipworm, a creature that bores into wood. But the Baltic Sea is not salty enough for shipworm. So an oak ship that sank in Stockholm harbour might still be there, whole.

From 1954 he spent his spare time dragging a hook along the harbour bottom from a small boat, and lowering a sampler he had made himself, a weighted tube with a sharp edge that fell into the mud and came back with a plug of whatever it had hit. On the south shore he found rusty stoves, ladies' bicycles, Christmas trees and dead cats. In 1955 he went back to the archives and found the council's letter to the king from August 1628, which said the ship had sunk on the other side of the channel, by an island called Beckholmen. He also got hold of a chart of the harbour bottom that showed a lump fifty metres long in front of a dry dock there. He was told it was rubble from building the dock. A navy diver, Per Edvin Fälting, told him the rubble had been dumped somewhere else.

In the summer of 1956 the two of them searched off Beckholmen. On August 25th 1956 the sampler came up with a plug of old, black oak. They moved the boat twenty metres along the lump, dropped it again, and brought up more. Then navy divers went down. Fälting felt his way along a wooden wall in water so dark he could see nothing, and found a row of square holes, and above it a second row. Gun ports, on two decks. Only one ship like that had been lost near Beckholmen.

## The lift

Raising a ship weighing more than a thousand tonnes, stuck in clay for three centuries, took five years. Navy divers spent eighteen months cutting six tunnels through the clay under the hull with jets of water, working in the dark. Through the tunnels they threaded steel cables, and fixed the cables to two big floating pontoons on the surface. In August 1959 the pontoons took the strain, and in eighteen lifts, each of about a metre, they moved the ship from thirty-two metres of water to sixteen, closer to shore. There she lay for a year and a half while divers made her tight enough to float.

On the morning of April 24th 1961, at three minutes past nine, with television cameras and thousands of people watching, the Vasa's timbers came up out of the water. She was pumped dry over ten days, and on May 4th 1961 she floated on her own keel for the second time in her life, into the very dry dock whose rubble she had been mistaken for.

## Inside her

Ten young archaeologists went into the black mud that filled the ship. They washed the decks with garden hoses, numbered everything they found, and kept the finds under water in old bathtubs. In five months they brought out more than thirty thousand things from 1628: gun carriages, sailors' chests, bones from salted meat, hundreds of cannonballs, pewter plates, a gilt clock, more than four thousand coins, and the sails, folded in a locker. The 120 tonnes of ballast stones went out by lift. They found the skeletons of eleven people on the decks, and divers later found four more in the broken stern.

For seventeen years the ship was sprayed with a kind of wax that takes the place of the water in old wood, and then dried slowly for ten years. In 1990 her own museum opened around her. Nearly all of her wood is the wood that sank. In 2018 every iron bolt put into her in 1961 was replaced with stainless steel, which made her eight tonnes lighter.

## The sister

The builder questioned at the inquest, Hein Jacobsson, was building the Vasa's sister ship, the Äpplet, at that very time. The captain told the court he feared she would sink the same way. She did not. The builder had already suspected the Vasa was too narrow, and made the Äpplet a metre wider. She served the navy for nearly thirty years, and in 1659 she was sunk on purpose, with other old warships, to block a channel so that enemies could not reach Stockholm by sea.

In December 2021 archaeologists from the Museum of Wrecks, searching that channel with the navy, found a big wreck with gun ports on two levels. They measured it, took samples of the wood, which turned out to be oak cut in 1627 near Stockholm, and checked the old records. On October 25th 2022 they announced that it was the Äpplet. For the first time, the ship that sank can be measured against the ship that did not.
