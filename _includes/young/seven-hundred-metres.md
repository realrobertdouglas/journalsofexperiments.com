## A letter

On October 9th 1963 an engineer in Venice called Alberico Biadene wrote a letter to a colleague who was on holiday. The colleague, Mario Pancini, ran the Vajont dam in the mountains north of Venice, and the letter told him to come home. The mountain above the dam's lake was moving faster, Biadene wrote: twenty centimetres a day at some points. There were cracks in the ground, dips in the road, and the trees on the slope were leaning. It all "makes one think the worst", he said. But he had a plan. The lake was being lowered a metre a day, and that morning it should be at 700 metres above the sea. He wanted to get it down to 695, "always with the aim of creating a band of safety for the waves".

The number 700 had come from a model. A year before, a professor in Padua had built a copy of the lake at a two-hundredth of its real size, and had tipped gravel into it to see what wave a landslide would make. His report said that if the water was kept at 700 metres or lower, even the worst landslide he could imagine would be safe.

At thirty-nine minutes past ten that night, with the water at 700.4 metres, the side of the mountain fell into the lake. It was about 270 million cubic metres of rock, and it took less than a minute.

## The dam and the mountain

The Vajont dam was built by SADE, the electricity company of Venice, in a narrow gorge above the town of Longarone. It was finished in 1960, and it was the tallest arch dam in the world: 262 metres high, a thin curved shell of concrete that pushed the water's weight into the rock on either side. Behind it the lake could hold 150 million cubic metres of water.

The lake lay in a valley with a mountain on each side. On the south side was Monte Toc. In 1959 the company sent a young geologist, Edoardo Semenza, whose father Carlo had designed the dam, to look at the slopes around the lake. He found something worrying: the whole lower slope of Monte Toc, from a high flat shelf called the Pozza down to the river, seemed to be the body of an old landslide (a mass of rock that had slid off the mountain long ago and stopped). If the surface it rested on sloped towards the lake, he wrote, the water might start it moving again, "with consequences hard to evaluate". The company's own senior geologist, Giorgio Dal Piaz, disagreed. He thought the rock was solid.

The lake began to fill in February 1960. In October, with the water at about 650 metres, a crack more than two kilometres long opened across the slope of Monte Toc, high above the water. On November 4th 1960 about 700,000 cubic metres of the slope's foot slid into the lake in half an hour, and made a wave two metres high. Nobody was hurt. But now everyone knew the mountain was moving.

## The two ideas

The company called in Leopold Müller of Salzburg, a rock engineer. In February 1961 he wrote that about 200 million cubic metres of the mountain was creeping slowly towards the lake, and that nothing could stop it. But it could be controlled. When the lake had been lowered after the November slide, the movement had slowed within a week. So, he wrote, "it will be possible to establish a relation between the level of the lake, the precipitation and the velocity of sliding, and so to have the possibility of regulating the velocity". The idea was that the mountain could be let down into the lake slowly, a little at a time, by raising and lowering the water, until it found a new resting place.

That was the first idea. The second was about waves. If a piece of the mountain fell into the lake, it would throw up water. Could a model tell how big the wave would be? Carlo Semenza thought it could, and asked Augusto Ghetti, the professor of hydraulics at Padua, to build one.

Everyone agreed to try both ideas at once. The water would be raised in steps, as an experiment, and the mountain watched. The official papers from Rome used that very word: an "experimental filling". Survey pillars were set up all over the slope and read every day, and four deep holes were fitted with piezometers (tubes that show how high the water stands inside the rock).

## The model

Ghetti's model was built in the summer of 1961 at a power station at Nove. The whole lake, five kilometres long, was copied in brick and plaster at a scale of 1 to 200, so that the model was 29 metres long and 12 metres wide, with a tiny copy of the dam at one end. The landslide was played by gravel, small rounded stones eight to ten millimetres across. Real rock sliding on wet clay was impossible to copy, Ghetti wrote, so he made "the simplest hypothesis": that the mountain was loose rubble. Edoardo Semenza, who came to watch, remembered that "all the material immediately slid into the reservoir when the supports were removed". He suggested using bricks instead, but the gravel was kept.

In the first tests the gravel ran down a tilted metal plate when the nets holding it were let go. In the later ones the plate was reshaped into the curve the geologists expected, and the gravel was dragged into the water by a tractor. Twenty-two tests were run, with the lake full and with it at 700 and 670 metres.

The report was finished on July 3rd 1962. Ghetti was honest about what he could not know. "The forecasts of the manner of the landslide event are as uncertain as could be," he wrote. Even his slowest tests, in which the gravel took three minutes to fall, were "somewhat high velocities of descent compared with the possible forecasts". With the lake full, the fastest fall sent a wave more than 22 metres over the dam. But when the water started lower, the waves were much smaller. From 700 metres, the worst test threw barely any water over the dam at all. So he concluded that "the level of 700 metres above the sea may be considered of absolute safety in respect even of the most catastrophic foreseeable landslide event". He also suggested extending the model down the gorge, to see what a wave would do to Longarone. That was never done.

## The experiment

The lake was raised a second time, from the end of 1961. It went past 650 metres, where the mountain had moved so much in 1960, and this time the mountain hardly moved. It went on up to 700 metres by November 1962. That month it rained very hard, and the slope began to move again, about a centimetre a day. The lake was lowered to 650, and the movement stopped.

This seemed to prove Müller's idea. The mountain moved when the water first touched a new band of the slope, and settled when the water was lowered, and did not move again when the water came back. Müller wrote later that this "led authorities and technicians to the conviction that a gradual stabilization of the moving mass would be brought about by raising the water level in individual steps". In March 1963 the company asked permission to raise the lake to 715 metres, fifteen metres above the model's safe level, and Rome agreed.

The third filling began in April 1963. By July the lake was at 705 metres. In August it rained very hard again, and the slope began to move: half a centimetre a day, then one. In early September the lake reached 710 metres, and was held there. The slope moved faster: three and a half centimetres a day by the middle of the month. On September 26th the engineers ordered the lake to be lowered. On October 1st Pancini went on holiday.

## The last week

From October 1st the lake fell a metre a day, and the mountain went faster. By October 8th the fastest points were moving ten centimetres a day, and then twenty. The thirty-five families still living on the slope were moved to the village of Casso, and the road along the lake was closed. On October 8th a government engineer named Bertolissi wrote a report for the ministry. The mountain was speeding up, he said, as it had before the slide of 1960. He expected a slide "in successive stages, in blocks", as in 1960. And he wrote down what the model had said: "with the maximum filling and with the instantaneous collapse of the landslide the consequent wave would reach a height of 25 metres". Since the road was 30 metres above the water, he thought that "should be sufficient for the safety of all persons". Longarone, below the dam, was not in his report. Nothing in the model had reached it.

The two men who read the instruments on the slope every day, an engineer named Cicco and a surveyor named Dal Prà, did not move their own families. They believed the numbers. Both died that night with their families.

At about a quarter past ten on the night of October 9th a man in Casso, high above the lake on the far side, was woken by the sound of rolling rocks. It grew louder. At about twenty to eleven a great wind broke his windows, the house shook, and the roof lifted so that rain and stones came into the bedroom. He was the only person on the mountain who saw anything and lived.

## What happened

At 22:39 the whole slope of Monte Toc, nearly two kilometres across and up to 330 metres thick, slid into the lake. The experts who studied it afterwards wrote that it had moved "five or six million times" faster than ever before, in one piece, like a single enormous block. It crossed the lake in less than a minute. The water had nowhere to go. The water was thrown sideways against the opposite slope, reaching 200 metres above its old level, and towards the dam, where the gorge narrowed and the water rose higher still. It went over the top of the dam by more than 100 metres, fell 400 metres into the gorge, and came out into the valley of the river Piave still 70 metres high. Longarone stood directly in its path.

The dam itself did not break. It is still there today. But the wave destroyed Longarone and four other villages. The official count of the dead was 1,917, 1,450 of them in Longarone; some counts say about 2,000. Forty-five men who lived in huts on top of the dam were killed.

A journalist named Tina Merlin had been writing about the Vajont for four years in a newspaper called *L'Unità*. In 1959 the company had her prosecuted for it, and she was acquitted. In February 1961 she had written that an enormous mass was threatening the valley, and asked: "It cannot be known whether the collapse will be slow or whether it will come with a terrible crash. In the latter case the consequences cannot be foreseen." Nobody answered her. On the morning after the disaster she wrote: "I am writing these lines with my heart gripped by remorse for not having done more to induce the people of these lands to rebel against the mortal threat that has now become a tragic reality. Today, however, one cannot only weep. It is time to learn something."

## Afterwards

The Italian parliament held an inquiry, which reported in July 1965. The majority of the members decided that the slide "was foreseen by no one", and that the experts' forecasts, "even considered in their worst combination, excluded dangers for the public safety". But the minority disagreed: "The tragedy could and should have been avoided." The courts took until 1971 to decide. In the end two men went to prison: Biadene, who had written the letter, and a government official named Sensidoni. The judges found that the flood had been "aggravated by foresight of the event". Pancini, the man the letter was written to, had killed himself before the trial.

Twenty years later two American engineers, Alfred Hendron and Franklin Patton, went back to the mountain for the US Army Corps of Engineers. They found layers of soft clay on the surface the slide had left behind, which made the rock far weaker than anyone had assumed. And they put the record of the lake beside the record of the rain. Every time the mountain had moved, it had rained hard just before. The engineers had believed that the lake controlled the mountain. It did, partly. But the rain controlled it too, and nobody had put the rain into the plan. They drew a line on a chart, which they called the "failure envelope", showing which mixtures of lake level and rainfall made the slope move. Above a lake level of about 710 to 720 metres, it would have moved even with no rain. In September 1963 the lake was at 710, and it rained.

The thing nobody had expected, they wrote, was the speed. "The high velocity acquired by this slide mass was easily the most unforeseen phenomenon associated with the Vaiont Slide." Gravel in a model cannot do what wet clay under 270 million cubic metres of rock does: as the rock began to slide, the friction heated the water in the clay, and the slope lost half its grip in seconds.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A model can only answer the question you ask it.** The model at Nove was asked how big a wave gravel would make. It answered honestly, and it even warned that nobody really knew how the real mountain would fall. But nobody asked it the question that mattered: how fast would the real mountain move? Gravel could not have said. The real mountain fell in one piece, faster than the fastest test. The safe number, 700, came out of the report without the warnings around it, and the people it was supposed to protect lived in a town the model did not even include.

**When an experiment works twice, it has not proved itself for ever.** The engineers raised the lake, and the mountain moved. They lowered it, and the mountain stopped. They raised it again, and the mountain did not move. That looked like proof that the water was in control. But the winter of the second filling was dry, and the autumns of 1960 and 1963 were wet. The rain was doing half the work, and the rain was not in the plan. If something important is missing from your idea, a test can seem to succeed when it is really just lucky.

**Instruments only tell you about what they are pointed at.** The pillars on the slope were read every day, and they said the mountain was speeding up. The engineers read that as the start of a slide like the one in 1960, a piece of the front falling off. The American engineers later said the two most important things, the water pressure deep in the slope and the movement along the sliding surface, were the two things nobody had measured. The instruments were good. They were measuring the wrong things.

**Listen to a warning even when it gets the details wrong.** Tina Merlin's figure of 50 million cubic metres was a quarter of the real size, and she was wrong about how the lake would do its damage. But she asked the one question that counted: would the collapse be slow, or sudden? The men with the real figures never asked it. A warning should be judged by the question it asks, not only by the numbers it gets wrong.

So the lesson is simple. When you test something big and alive, like a mountain, design the test to find out what you are afraid of, not just what you hope. And never trust a number from a model further than the question the model was asked.
