## A city under the snow

Greenland is covered by a sheet of ice more than a mile thick. In the 1950s the United States Army wanted to know whether soldiers could live inside it. Not on top of it, where the storms are, but underneath, in tunnels cut into the snow.

The Army had a base at Thule, on the coast of north-west Greenland, and from 1954 its engineers tried things out on the ice behind it. They found a Swiss machine made for clearing avalanches off mountain roads, called the Peter snow miller, that could cut a deep trench through the snow and throw the snow out the top. The snow it threw out packed down and hardened within a few days into something almost like soft stone. In 1957 they built a small test camp of trenches roofed with wood and metal, and in 1958 they tried a trench roofed with nothing but an arch of the hardened snow, cast over a curved metal mould and left to set. It held.

So in 1959 the Army started a much bigger camp, 138 miles out on the ice from the edge, at a place so cold that the snow never melted. It was called Camp Century. It was to be a whole town under the snow for 100 men, all year round, with a nuclear reactor to make its electricity. The reactor was the most important part. A reactor needs no fuel brought across the ice on sleds, and if one could be made to work in the hardest place in the world, it could work anywhere.

## What they wanted to find out

The Army wrote down what it wanted to learn. Could a camp be cut into the snow, roofed with snow, and lived in all year? Could a small nuclear reactor be carried there in pieces, put together, and run by soldiers? And could the camp last ten years?

There was also a question the Army did not write down, and did not tell Denmark, which owns Greenland. The Army wanted to build a huge network of tunnels under the ice, thousands of miles long, with trains running through them carrying 600 nuclear missiles, so that an enemy could never know where the missiles were. The plan was called Project Iceworm. Camp Century was a small test of whether the tunnels could be built and kept open. Nobody at the camp knew that. The plan stayed secret until 1996, when the papers were found in an archive in Washington. There is a companion piece about it, "[Iceworm](/deeper/thirty-inches/iceworm/#young)".

## How they built it

The camp was built in two summers, 1959 and 1960. Everything came out from the coast on long trains of sleds pulled by tractors, 138 miles each way: about 5,600 tons of it. The engineers cut a long main trench for vehicles and about twenty side trenches running off it, each about 24 feet wide and 24 feet high, and roofed them with arches of hardened snow. Inside the trenches they put up prefabricated huts, standing on wooden cribs with a gap of two feet under the floor and a gap at the sides, so that the snow could move without crushing them.

The reactor came by ship to Thule in July 1960, in 27 pieces, each small enough to be carried by an aircraft, and went out to the camp on the sleds. It was too big for a normal trench, so three trenches 40 feet wide were cut for it and roofed with curved steel arches covered in snow. The engineers admitted that nobody had ever tested a roof like that. There was no time to try anything else. The reactor was switched on, on October 2nd 1960. A crew of 18 soldiers ran it.

Water came from a well. Steam was blown down into the ice until it melted a shaft 100 feet deep and a pool at the bottom, and a pump brought the water up. It was purer than distilled water. Sewage went the other way: it was poured into a pit in the snow, where it melted its own way downwards. The plan said the sewage pit must be at least 500 feet from the nearest building. It was built 150 feet away, because winter was coming and there was no time.

That was a pattern. The ventilation system that was meant to carry warm air out of the tunnels was never installed. The screens that were meant to keep the heat of the buildings off the snow walls were left out of the plans. The metal moulds for the snow arches, which were meant to be taken out once the snow had set, were left in. The engineers knew all this and wrote it down.

## Thirty inches a year

Snow is not a solid. Under enough weight it flows, very slowly, like very thick treacle. A trench cut in it starts to close the day it is cut: the walls bow inwards, the roof comes down, and the floor, strangely, rises up in the middle. The engineers knew this. They had measured it at the test camp. So from the very first winter they marked points on the walls and roofs of the trenches at Camp Century and measured, again and again, how far the points had moved.

The answer was about 30 inches a year. That is how much lower the roof of an average trench got every year. A trench 24 feet high lost a tenth of its height each year, and the rate was getting faster, because four feet of new snow fell on the camp every year and the weight on the roofs kept growing.

Heat made it worse. Snow flows a hundred times faster at just below freezing than at 40 degrees below. Every building, every light, every vehicle and every man in the camp gave off heat, and the screens that should have kept that heat off the snow had never been built. The sewage pit was the worst. In its first two years more than 3 million gallons of warm waste went into it, and all that warmth went into the snow around it. The floor at the end of the nearest trench sank four feet, and the two huts closest to the pit had to be taken out. The smell, the Army's report says, became "almost unbearable" in the nearest barracks.

The reactor trenches went fastest of all. Their steel arches had been covered with snow unevenly in the rush to finish, so they took the weight on one side, and within a year they were buckling and pressing down on the roofs of the reactor buildings. Twice the Army had to jack the arches up by six feet to keep them off the reactor.

What kept the camp open was men with chain saws. To hold the tunnels at their proper size, about 20,000 cubic yards of snow had to be cut off the walls and ceilings every year and carried out. Soldiers stood on the roofs of the huts with electric saws and cut the snow back from the eaves. A Danish scientist who visited in 1964 said the hanging walls of ice in the passages were called "brain crushers".

## What it was like to live there

The Army made a film about Camp Century in 1961 and called it "City Under the Ice". It showed a town with every convenience, as the Army's own description puts it, "from a nuclear power plant to a shower". There was a mess hall, a chapel, a cinema with a new film every night, a library, a hobby shop, a gym, a hospital with an operating room, and a bar where any drink cost 25 cents.

The Danish scientist Willi Dansgaard came in July 1964 to collect old snow for an experiment, and kept a diary. He found about 250 men living there in summer, and no women at all. On the wall of the mess hall was a poster about what to do if an atom bomb was coming, which ended by telling you to put your head between your knees and kiss yourself goodbye. He noticed that the living quarters were only about 100 metres from the reactor. And he saw the passages being cut free with chain saws, because the snow was closing in on everything.

## What happened to the camp

The reactor ran for nearly three years, until July 9th 1963. Then the Army decided to stop keeping men at the camp through the winter, to save money. A reactor cannot be left alone for half the year, so in the summer of 1964 it was taken apart and shipped home. The men who took it apart found it was more radioactive than anyone had expected, so each man could work near it only for a short time, and more men were needed. From then on the camp was used only in summer. In 1966 the last working party lived in huts on the surface, not in the tunnels. After that nobody came back to live there.

The Army's own report on the camp, written in 1965, says the project was a success. The snow tunnels worked. The snow roofs worked. The well worked. The reactor worked. And then it says that ten years is the longest a camp under the snow can be expected to last, with constant work to keep it open. The camp had been built for ten years. It was given up after seven.

In May 1969, three years after the camp was abandoned, a small party of Army engineers flew out to it and climbed down through an escape hatch to photograph what was left. The passages where a man had once walked upright were now crawl spaces. The steel escape towers were crushed. The arches over the reactor buildings had come down through their roofs. The mess hall's ceiling was pushed in. Nobody went down again after that, and within ten years the tunnels had closed completely, with everything still inside them: the huts, the railway, the fuel tanks, the diesel engines, and the sewage pit.

## The jars of mud

One other thing had been going on at Camp Century, in a trench of its own. Scientists from the Army's cold regions laboratory were drilling a hole straight down through the ice to the rock underneath, to bring up a long core of ice that would show what the weather had been like for thousands of years. It took six summers. On July 4th 1966 the drill reached the bottom of the ice, nearly 1,400 metres down, and kept going, and brought up three and a half metres of frozen mud from the ground under the ice sheet.

The ice became famous. Willi Dansgaard and his team in Copenhagen measured it and found a record of the climate going back 100,000 years, the first of its kind. That was the beginning of a whole science. The mud was forgotten. It was put into glass jars, about thirty of them, and the jars went into a freezer in Buffalo, New York, with the scientist who had been in charge of the core. In the 1990s, when he retired and nobody there wanted it, he rang Copenhagen and said: come and get it, or it goes into the lake. The Danes came and got it, and the jars of mud went with it, and sat in a freezer in Copenhagen for twenty more years.

In 2017 the Danish scientists were moving their ice collection to a new freezer and found boxes marked "Camp Century sub-ice", full of jars. One of them called them the cookie jars. By then there were ways of reading old mud that had not existed in 1966. In 2019 two jars were opened. Inside were twigs and moss leaves, the plants of a tundra, lying in soil that had never been scraped by moving ice. Atoms in the sand grains, made by cosmic rays that only reach the ground when there is no ice on top, showed that the ground had been open to the sky within the last million years. Greenland had been green, at least once, and the ice sheet had melted and grown back again. That means it can melt again. The full story of the core is in the companion piece "[The jars](/deeper/thirty-inches/the-jars/#young)".

## What is left under the ice

In 2016 a team of scientists led by William Colgan worked out from the Army's old reports what had been left under the snow at Camp Century. About 9,000 tons of buildings and rails. About 200,000 litres of diesel fuel, in tanks that have probably burst. About 24 million litres of sewage and waste water. The radioactive water that had been drained out of the reactor into the ice. And the chemicals called PCBs that were in the paint and the machines of that time, which are poisonous and last for ever. All of it was about 36 metres under the surface by then, and sinking deeper every year as new snow fell.

The Army had left it there because, in 1967, everyone assumed the snow in the middle of Greenland would keep falling for ever and the camp would be buried for good. Colgan's team ran the climate forward on a computer and found that if the world keeps warming as it is, the site could start to melt instead of gaining snow by about the year 2090. After that, sooner or later, what is under the ice would come out.

Greenland's government, which did not exist in 1959, asked Denmark what it was going to do. Denmark sent scientists to measure. Since 2017 the Geological Survey of Denmark and Greenland has had weather stations on the site, has drilled into the snow and has mapped the buried camp with radar. Its first results were calmer than the first report: the snow is still piling up, the camp's remains will be about 60 metres down by the year 2100, and it is very unlikely that any melt water reaches them before then. What happens in the century after that is still an open question, and the stations are still measuring.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Measure the thing you cannot change, and believe what it says.** The engineers did not guess how fast the snow would close in. They marked the walls before the first winter and measured them for four years. The answer was 30 inches a year, and they believed it: they wrote that a camp under the snow could last ten years at most, and they gave this one up after seven. Nobody was hurt. That is what listening to an instrument looks like.

**The same measurement answers the question you did not write down.** The Army said it was testing whether 100 men could live under the snow. It was really also testing whether thousands of miles of tunnel could carry missiles for years. The closure measurement answered both questions at once. If a trench with one warm hut in it closes 30 inches a year, a railway tunnel full of warm missiles cannot work. The snow did not know which experiment it was in.

**Heat is a weight.** Everyone knew that warm snow flows faster. The plans had screens to keep the heat off the walls, ventilation to carry it out, and the sewage pit far away from everything. All of those were dropped to save time. The snow then did exactly what the plans had said it would do. The camp did not fail because the snow surprised anyone. It failed because the margins that had been designed were never built.

**Decide what you are leaving behind before you leave.** The camp was abandoned on the assumption that the snow would bury it for ever. Nobody measured that assumption, because nobody thought the ice sheet could change. It is the one thing about Camp Century that now matters to people who were not born when the tunnels closed.

**Keep the sample you cannot read yet.** The jars of mud were useless in 1966. They were packed, labelled, kept frozen and carried across an ocean by people who had no use for them, and fifty-one years later they answered a question about the future of the whole ice sheet. The reason they survived is that someone labelled them and someone else refused to let them go into the lake.

So the lesson is simple. When you build something to find out whether it will last, measure how fast it is failing from the first day, and believe the number even when the thing still looks fine. And keep what you dig up, labelled, even if you do not yet know what it is for.
