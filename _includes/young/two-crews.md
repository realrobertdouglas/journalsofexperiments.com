## The night of February 17th 1864

At a quarter to nine on the night of February 17th 1864, an officer on the deck of a warship called the *Housatonic* saw something in the water about a hundred yards away. The *Housatonic* belonged to the United States Navy. She was anchored outside the harbour of Charleston, in South Carolina, as part of a blockade: a ring of ships that stopped anything going in or out of the port. It was the American Civil War, and Charleston belonged to the other side, the Confederacy.

The officer said the thing looked like a porpoise coming up to breathe. Then it came straight at the ship, fast, too close and too low for the guns. The captain fired a shotgun at it. A moment later there was an explosion under the ship's stern. The *Housatonic* sank in a few minutes, in water so shallow that her masts stayed above the surface, and most of the crew climbed into the rigging and waited to be rescued. Five men died.

One sailor in the rigging, Robert Flemming, said that about three quarters of an hour later he saw a blue light on the water. On the shore, Confederate soldiers at a fort called Battery Marshall said they saw the signal they had been told to watch for, and lit a light to guide the boat home. The boat never came home.

The boat was a submarine called the *H. L. Hunley*. She had just become the first submarine in history to sink an enemy ship. She was named after a man she had drowned four months earlier, along with seven others. Before that she had drowned five more. The eight men inside her that night knew all of this, because the general in charge had ordered that they be told.

## Who built her

Horace Hunley was a lawyer and politician from New Orleans. In the winter of 1861 to 1862 he and two partners, James McClintock and Baxter Watson, built a submarine there. When the Union navy captured New Orleans in April 1862, they sank their boat so it could not be captured and moved to Mobile, Alabama. There they built a second boat in a machine shop called Park and Lyons. It sank in rough water while being towed, with no one inside.

Hunley paid for a third boat himself, and it was named after him. It was built in the same shop in the spring of 1863. A soldier named William Alexander, who was an engineer, helped build her, and so did his friend George Dixon, another engineer from the same regiment. Dixon had been shot in the leg at the battle of Shiloh in April 1862. The bullet hit a twenty-dollar gold coin in his pocket and bent it. He had the coin engraved and carried it for luck.

The Confederate army at Charleston wanted the boat badly. The city was under siege, and a Charleston shipping company offered $100,000 to anyone who could sink the Union ironclad *New Ironsides*. In August 1863 General Beauregard, who commanded at Charleston, telegraphed the railways: "Please expedite transportation of Whitney's submarine boat from Mobile here. It is much needed."

## What the boat was

The *Hunley* was made from an iron steam boiler, cut in half lengthways and widened, with pointed ends. She was about forty feet long and less than four feet wide. Eight men sat in a row on a bench and turned a crank that ran the length of the boat and spun the propeller. One more man stood at the front, with his head in a small hatch, and steered.

To dive, the men let seawater into tanks at each end of the boat until the top was three inches under water. Then the captain tilted two fins on the sides, like the fins of a fish, and the boat went down. To come up, he tilted them the other way, or the men pumped the water out of the tanks. Along the bottom of the boat were heavy iron weights. If the boat got stuck on the bottom, the crew could turn bolts from inside with a wrench and drop the weights, and the boat would float up.

There was one serious mistake in the design. The water tanks at each end had no lids. If the boat tipped, or if someone left the valve open too long, the water poured out of the tanks and into the part of the boat where the men sat.

The air inside lasted only a short time. The crew lit a candle when they shut the hatches. When the candle went out, there was not enough oxygen left to burn, and it was time to come up. The candle was the only instrument the boat had for measuring air.

The weapon was a torpedo, which in those days meant a container of gunpowder that exploded when it touched something. The plan at first was to tow it on a rope 200 feet long, dive under an enemy ship and let the torpedo hit the ship behind. This was tested in the Mobile River in July 1863 against an old barge, and it worked. But at sea the torpedo kept drifting up against the boat. So the builders put it on the end of a long pole sticking out from the front instead. Now the gunpowder was only about sixteen feet from the crew. Nobody tested what that would do to them.

## The first crew

The boat came to Charleston by train on August 12th 1863. The army took her away from her owners almost at once and gave her to a navy officer, Lieutenant John Payne, with a crew of volunteers.

On August 29th 1863 she was tied up at a wharf, ready to go out that night and attack the fleet. The hatches were open. A steamer passed close by, the boat heeled over, and water poured in through the hatches and the open tanks. The boat sank in seconds. Payne, who was standing in a hatch, jumped clear. Two other men got out. Five men drowned.

## The second crew

The boat was raised. Horace Hunley came from Mobile, asked for her back, and got her. He brought Dixon with him and a crew of men from the shop that had built her. For a while Dixon practised diving under a navy ship anchored in the harbour, and the boat went under and came up again every time.

On the morning of October 15th 1863, Hunley took the boat out himself. The army's journal says she left the wharf at 9.25 and went under at 9.35. Air bubbles came up. She did not. The water was nine fathoms deep, about fifty-four feet, and nobody could reach her. A diver found her three days later, and it took until November 7th to raise her.

When they opened the hatches, Alexander wrote down exactly what they found, because he wanted to know what had gone wrong. Hunley was at the front with his head in the hatch, one hand on top of his head as if trying to push the cover open. In his other hand was a candle that had never been lit. The valve that let seawater into his tank was wide open, and the wrench for closing it was lying on the floor. At the back, Thomas Park was in the same position. The bolts holding the iron weights had been partly turned, but not far enough to drop them. Alexander worked out what had happened. Hunley had opened the valve to take on more water, the boat had gone down into the dark, and while he tried to light the candle the tank had overflowed into the boat. Eight men died, Hunley among them.

The two crews together had cost thirteen lives, by the official count. Other people counted differently. Alexander said twenty-three. Two deserters who crossed to the Union fleet in January said seventeen. A Confederate engineer who had towed the boat said at least thirty-three. The Union admiral, John Dahlgren, wrote to Washington: "So far the trials have been unlucky, having drowned three crews of 17 men in all. Still she does dive."

## The third crew

General Beauregard said he would not let the boat be used again. Dixon and Alexander asked, and were refused, and asked again. In the end Beauregard agreed, on two conditions. First, every volunteer had to be told the whole history of the boat and everyone she had killed. Second, she was not to dive. She would run on the surface, with only her hatches showing, and ram the torpedo on its pole into a ship's side, the way an ordinary torpedo boat did. The one thing she had been built to do was now forbidden.

Eight men volunteered anyway. Most came from a navy ship in the harbour. Four of them had been born in Europe. One, James Wicks, had been a sailor in the United States Navy until 1862, and had changed sides. All winter they went out from Battery Marshall, on Sullivan's Island, four nights a week on average. They would walk seven miles along the beach, practise for two hours in the bay, take a compass bearing on the nearest Union ship as it anchored for the night, and crank out into the dark. Most nights the wind and the tide beat them, and they turned back exhausted. Sometimes they came up for air so close to the Union boats that they could hear the sailors singing.

One evening they tried an experiment. They sank the boat to the bottom of the bay, with everyone cranking, to find out how long the air would last. Any man could say "up", and the boat would come up. Alexander wrote: "In twenty-five minutes after I had closed the after manhead and excluded the outer air the candle would not burn." They kept cranking in the dark. Nobody wanted to be the first to say the word. Finally, "as the voice of one man, the word 'up' came from all nine." They had been down two hours and thirty-five minutes. When they opened the hatch it was night, and a message had already gone to Beauregard that the boat was lost with all hands.

Dixon wrote to a friend in Mobile in January 1864. He said that "to catch the Atlantic Ocean smooth during the winter months is considerable of an undertaking and one that I never wish to undertake again." He also wrote: "I have got a splendid crew of men the best I think I ever seen." A few days later Alexander was ordered back to Mobile to build a gun. Two soldiers from an artillery company took his place.

## What happened

On February 17th 1864 the wind dropped and the sea was calm. There was a moon, which was dangerous, but Dixon had waited for months. The boat went out on the falling tide towards the *Housatonic*, a new ship of 1,240 tons that had been anchoring closer in than the others.

The Union sailors saw her coming but could not stop her. The gunpowder went off against the ship's side, the stern blew apart, and the *Housatonic* sank. Then the blue light, or something, was seen on the water, and then nothing.

For ten days the Confederates did not even know which ship had been sunk. The newspaper called it a glorious success. Beauregard telegraphed that there was "little hope of safety of that brave man and his associates." In Mobile, Alexander telegraphed headquarters every day and got the same answer: "No news of the torpedo boat."

In November a Union diver dragged the seabed for 500 yards around the wreck, looking for the submarine. He found nothing.

## Finding her

For more than a century people looked for the *Hunley* in the wrong place, close to the shore. On May 3rd 1995 divers working for the author Clive Cussler found her four miles out, in about thirty feet of water, under three feet of sand. She was a thousand feet further out to sea than the *Housatonic*, on the far side from home, with her bow pointing towards Sullivan's Island.

On August 8th 2000 engineers lifted her out of the sea in a cradle of thirty slings hung from a steel frame, with thousands of people watching from boats, and took her to a laboratory in Charleston. In 2001 archaeologists opened the hull.

The eight men were still inside, each at his place on the bench. Their skeletons showed no new injuries and no sign that they had struggled or tried to get out. Dixon was at the front. Beside him were a lantern, a gold watch and the bent gold coin from Shiloh, engraved "My life Preserver". The back hatch was locked. The pumps had not been set to pump water out. The iron weights on the keel were still bolted on. Nobody had tried to drop them. The crew were buried in Charleston on April 17th 2004, next to the men from the first two crews.

## What killed them

In 2017 a scientist named Rachel Lance and her colleagues at Duke University tested the one thing the Confederates had never tested. They built a model of the *Hunley* one-sixth the size, put a pressure gauge inside it, and set off gunpowder charges next to it on a scaled-down pole. Then they worked out what the real torpedo, 135 pounds of gunpowder about sixteen feet away, would have done.

The answer was that the explosion did not need to break the hull. The shock wave hit the iron wall, the wall flexed, and a second shock wave passed into the air inside the boat, where the men were breathing. Their calculation gave each man less than a sixteen per cent chance of surviving it. They concluded that "the crew was instantly killed by primary blast trauma": the blast damaged their lungs, and they died where they sat. That fits what the archaeologists found: no struggle, no attempt to escape, nothing touched.

Not everyone agrees. The lantern found beside Dixon has clear glass, not blue. In 1864 a "blue light" was actually a hand-held flare that burned white, which could be seen for miles, so the signal from the shore might have been real. If it was, the crew were alive for at least forty-five minutes after the explosion, and something else killed them. The scientists at the laboratory still list four possible explanations.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A warning can be read correctly and still miss the thing that kills you.** Everybody read the two drowned crews. The Union admiral read them as proof the boat could dive, and hung nets around his ironclads. General Beauregard read them as a reason to ban diving. The builders read each sinking as a mistake that could be fixed: open hatches the first time, an open valve the second. All of them were right about what they saw. None of them was looking at the torpedo on the pole.

**Fixing every accident is not the same as being safe.** Every time the boat sank, someone could name the reason, and the reason was corrected. That made the boat seem safer each time. But a boat that only ever kills people for reasons that can be fixed never builds up a record against itself. Thirteen dead in two months should have been a verdict. Instead it was two lessons.

**The experiment they never ran was the one that mattered.** The boat was tested for diving, in a calm river, with the gunpowder towed 200 feet behind. It was never tested with the gunpowder sixteen feet from the crew, which is how she was actually used. The first time that test was run was the night of the attack, and the result took 136 years to come back.

**Telling people the risk is not the same as knowing the risk.** The eight men were told everything the boat had done, and they volunteered anyway. That was honest. But the danger they were warned about, drowning, was not the danger that killed them. A true warning about the wrong thing is still the wrong warning.

So the lesson is simple. When something has already failed twice, do not just ask what went wrong each time. Ask what has never been tested at all, because that is where the next failure is waiting.
