## The ship in the morning

On the morning of December 10th 1952, a French doctor named Alain Bombard was alone in a rubber boat in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean. He had been alone for fifty-two days. He had not eaten any ordinary food in all that time, and he had not drunk any ordinary water. He had lived on raw fish, on the juice he squeezed out of fish, on tiny sea creatures called plankton, on rain when it fell, and, for a few days at the start, on small sips of sea water.

That morning he looked out of his tent and saw a ship. He flashed a mirror at it until someone noticed. The ship turned, raised a British flag and dipped it three times, which is the salute one warship gives another. Her captain called across: did he need help?

"Just the time, please, and my exact longitude," Bombard called back.

Longitude is how far east or west you are. Bombard thought he was nearly at the islands of the Caribbean. The ship's answer told him he was six hundred miles short. He had got his sums wrong for seven weeks. He gave up. He rowed across, climbed aboard the ship, which was called the *Arakaka*, had a hot shower, and was given a meal: a fried egg, a small piece of liver, a spoonful of cabbage and some fruit. Then, an hour and a half later, he climbed back down into his little boat and sailed on. Thirteen days later he reached the island of Barbados.

When the newspapers found out about the meal, they said he had "admitted" it. He answered: "But that was only one day in 65. It makes no difference to my theory."

That answer is what this story is about.

## Who he was

Alain Bombard was born in Paris on October 27th 1924. He became a doctor. In 1951, when he was twenty-six, he was working at the hospital in Boulogne, a fishing town on the north coast of France. Early one morning a fishing boat missed the harbour entrance in the mist and hit the stone wall of the breakwater. Forty-three men drowned. They were carried into Bombard's hospital, still wearing their lifejackets, and he could not save a single one. The wall they drowned beside had a ladder every twenty yards.

Bombard started to count. About 200,000 people died at sea every year. Around 50,000 of them died after they had got safely into a lifeboat or onto a raft. Why? Bombard decided that most of them did not die of hunger or thirst at all. Nine out of ten died within three days, and you cannot starve in three days. They died, he thought, of fear and despair: of being sure they were going to die.

## What he wanted to prove

Bombard's idea had two halves.

The first half was about the body. He said the sea had everything a person needs. Fish are mostly water, and their flesh has very little salt, so if you squeeze them you get something you can drink. Fish give you protein and fat and vitamins. Plankton, the tiny drifting creatures that whales eat, give you vitamin C, which stops a disease called scurvy. And for the first few days, before you catch a fish, he said you could drink a little sea water, about a pint and a half a day, for no more than five days. Everyone knew that sea water kills you. Bombard said it only kills you if you drink too much of it, or drink it too late, when your body has already dried out.

The second half was about the mind. If a shipwrecked person knew the sea could feed him, and knew that someone had already done it, he would not give up. "Despair is a greater danger than thirst," Bombard wrote.

The official books disagreed completely. The American survival manual of 1950 said, in slanting letters: "Do not drink salt water." The new Air Force manual, printed in December 1952, the very month Bombard landed, said: "Don't drink body fluids of fish, urine, or salt water. They are dangerous." That one sentence said no to both of Bombard's sources of water.

Scientists had tested sea water on volunteers in hospital wards. In 1943 a British doctor called Ladell gave men a small amount of sea water each day and found that they kept a little more water in their bodies than men who had none. But the committee he worked for still said: do not drink it. In 1952 two scientists at Cambridge, Hervey and McCance, tried it on six men for three days and found the same small gain, with more salt in the body, which they thought would be paid for later. So the question was really about amounts and days, and about what happens after day three. Nobody had tried day four or five.

## The boat

Bombard did not have a laboratory of his own, so he borrowed a bench at the oceanographic museum in Monaco and spent six months there, squeezing fish in a fruit press and tasting plankton. A Dutch businessman paid for it. Then he got a boat.

It was a rubber dinghy, the kind that inflates, shaped like a horseshoe, about fifteen feet long and six feet wide, with a wooden board across the open end and a wooden floor. It had a small sail, which could not sail into the wind. There was not a single piece of metal in it. He called it *L'Hérétique*, which means "the heretic", because everyone said what he was going to do was impossible and foolish, and a court had already fined him for going to sea in a beach toy.

He took a sextant for finding his position, a compass, charts, fishing lines, a harpoon gun, a net for plankton, a small parachute to use as a sea anchor, a few medicines he promised himself not to use, some books, a radio that could only listen, and a crate of emergency food. The crate was sealed. That was the whole point of it: if he landed with the seals unbroken, it would prove he had not eaten the food.

## The practice run

First he tried the Mediterranean, with an English sailor called Jack Palmer. They left Monaco on May 25th 1952. Bombard drank sea water from the first day. Palmer could not bring himself to; by the next day his tongue was dry and his hands had a rash, and then he gave in and drank too, and the signs went away. They caught one fish in the first week and then nothing for five days. A storm blew them backwards. After thirteen days they were so hungry that they stopped a passing liner with a smoke bomb and asked for some food, and the ship handed down biscuits, four tins of corned beef and a tin of milk. Bombard did not open his own sealed crate, because that was for the Atlantic. The newspapers said he had failed.

They landed on the island of Minorca on June 11th, and the first thing Bombard did was ask a Spanish naval officer to look at the seals on his food crate. Later the boat capsized while being towed, and Palmer, who was a fine sailor at sea, kept finding reasons not to go back to it. In August, in Tangier, Bombard left him a note and went on alone. He sailed to Casablanca, then to the Canary Islands, which took eleven days. There he had the French consul check and seal his stores again. On Sunday October 19th 1952 he left Las Palmas for the Atlantic.

## Sixty-five days

For the first week he caught almost nothing and drank a little sea water. A wave filled the boat and he baled it out with his hat. His sail tore and he sewed it up with black thread. On the seventh day he caught his first big fish, a dorado, and after that the fish never left him. Flying fish landed on his tent every night.

Every day he did the same things in the same order, because he was both the scientist and the only patient. Collect the flying fish. Fish for an hour. Check every inch of the rubber boat for rubbing. Half an hour of exercises. Ten minutes with the net, for two spoonfuls of plankton. At noon, the sextant. At two o'clock, his medical examination of himself: blood pressure, temperature, skin, nails, hair, the inside of his mouth, and then a careful note on how he felt. In the evening he measured his urine and tested his strength and wrote down everything he had caught and eaten.

After ten days his toenails began to fall out and his hands broke out in a rash. His watch stopped, and because longitude depends on knowing the time, he could no longer be sure how far west he was. After twenty-three days it rained, a huge tropical downpour, and he drank and washed and filled a rubber mattress with water. From then on he had rain, often far too much of it.

What nearly killed him was not hunger or thirst. It was his mistake about longitude. By the middle of November he believed he was a few days from land. He was not; he was about six hundred miles further east than he thought. The wind died. For twenty days the boat barely moved. He read in his handbook that certain birds mean land is near, and he saw all of them, and there was no land. He got diarrhoea. "Forty days is enough for any man," he wrote. On December 6th he wrote his will, and in it he wrote that even if he died, castaways should not despair, because his idea had worked for fifty days.

That is the strange thing about the logbook. He had plenty of fish. He had plenty of rain. The boat was fine. What ran out was hope, because the numbers were wrong. Bombard had set out to prove that despair kills castaways, and his own diary proved it on himself, with his blood pressure written next to it: it stayed normal until the beginning of December and then dropped, he said, as his despair grew.

Then the *Arakaka* came, on December 10th. The meal made him hungry for the first time in fifty-three days, and he lost more weight in the last twelve days than in all the days before.

## The beach

On December 23rd 1952 he landed on the north coast of Barbados. He had lost 25 kilograms, nearly four stone. His blood had half the normal number of red cells. His toenails were gone and his whole body was covered in a rash. But he was alive, and he had crossed the Atlantic.

The first thing he did on the beach was find witnesses. A crowd had gathered, and when they saw the word "rations" on his crate they shouted "Food! Food!" Bombard asked the local schoolteacher and the policeman to look at the seals and agree that they were unbroken. Then he opened the crate and gave all the food away to the people on the beach.

Think about that for a moment. The sealed crate was the only thing in the whole experiment that someone else could check. And a few minutes after it was checked, it was eaten. His logbooks, which he could have sealed, were never sealed either. He said he was far too tired, and he was.

## What people said

The medical journal *The Lancet* said in 1953 that "by enterprise and endurance he has made his point." Notice the words. He had proved that a brave man could keep himself alive. The journal said nothing about the pint and a half of sea water.

Three years later the real test arrived, and it was not a man in a boat. The Medical Research Council in Britain went through the records of 448 ships sunk in the war, and 27,000 people who had been in them. The people who drank sea water in their lifeboats died at ten times the rate of the people who did not. But those people had mostly drunk it late, in big gulps, when everything else had run out, which Bombard had always said was the way to die. The records could not tell the difference between a sip on the first day and a gulp on the tenth.

Then a German doctor named Hannes Lindemann, who had met Bombard in Morocco and did not believe him, tried it himself. In 1955 he sailed a dugout canoe from Africa, drinking a measured amount of sea water every day, less than Bombard's pint and a half. His feet swelled on the second day, and the swelling climbed to his knees. When he stopped drinking it, his legs went back to normal. In 1956 he crossed the Atlantic alone in a folding kayak, in seventy-two days, carrying seventy kilograms of tinned food. He came back with the same conclusion as Bombard about the mind, that a person can survive almost anything if he keeps his courage, and the opposite conclusion about sea water. You can read more about the argument in the companion piece, "[A pint and a half](/deeper/one-day-in-sixty-five/a-pint-and-a-half/#young)".

Bombard lived to be eighty. He helped the French navy write a handbook for castaways, and in 1958 rubber life-rafts became compulsory on many French ships. He went into politics and sat in the European Parliament. He died in 2005. His boat is in a museum in Paris, with the patch on the side worn thin where his back rested against it.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**If your claim is about an amount, you have to measure the amount.** Bombard's idea was that a certain quantity of sea water, for a certain number of days, was safe. But in his logbook he only wrote "a little" and "small amounts." He never measured how much he drank. The one number that could have settled the argument was the one number he did not write down. Lindemann, who did write it down, found the opposite result.

**One day in sixty-five sounds small, but it was the only day anyone else saw.** The meal on the ship did not matter because of the food. It mattered because it was the only day of the whole voyage when someone other than Bombard knew what he ate. For the other sixty-four days, the only evidence was his own word. He was probably telling the truth. But an experiment should not have to depend on that.

**Put your evidence where someone else can read it.** The sealed crate was there to prove he had not cheated. It was opened and eaten on the beach. The logbooks were never sealed at all. If the proof of an experiment depends on how tired the scientist is at the end, the proof is in the wrong place.

**Sometimes the thing you prove is not the thing you set out to prove.** Bombard wanted to show that sea water was safe in small amounts. He never really showed that. What his diary did show, better than anything before it, was that a man with plenty of food and water can still nearly die of despair when he thinks he will never arrive. That was his other idea, and it was the one that lasted. Lindemann, who set out to prove him wrong, found exactly the same thing.

So the lesson is simple. When you test an idea on yourself, decide first what you will measure and who will check it, because afterwards nobody can.
