## The scientist

Alexis Carrel was a French surgeon. He was born near Lyon in 1873, and as a young doctor he worked out a way of sewing blood vessels together so neatly that the blood did not clot. In 1906 he went to work at the Rockefeller Institute in New York, a laboratory built with oil money to study disease, and in 1912 he won the Nobel Prize for his surgery. He was the first doctor working in America to win it.

Around 1910 Carrel took up a new method that had been invented at Yale. It was called tissue culture: a tissue is a group of living cells, and to culture it means to grow it, the way you grow bacteria in a dish. You cut a tiny piece of living tissue, put it in a drop of blood plasma on a small square of glass, let the drop set like jelly, turn the glass over so that the drop hangs inside a little sealed chamber, and keep it as warm as a living body. Cells creep out of the piece into the jelly and multiply. In 1910 Carrel announced that he could grow almost any tissue this way. A French scientist named Jolly said he was wrong: the cells were not growing, they were dying slowly. The experiment in this story began as Carrel's answer to that.

## The idea

Carrel had an idea about why cultures died. In 1911 a culture lived about a week or two and then stopped. Carrel thought this happened for two reasons that could be fixed: the cells poisoned themselves with their own waste, and they used up the food in the drop. If you washed the cells and gave them a fresh drop before they got sick, he thought, they might live for ever.

Behind that idea was a bigger one. If cells only died because of their surroundings, then growing old was something the body did to its cells, not something the cells did by themselves. Take the cells out of the body, keep their surroundings fresh, and they would never age. Carrel said this in 1911: old age and death were an accident, not a necessity. By 1922 his assistant wrote that the cells were "potentially immortal." By 1935 Carrel wrote in a famous book that they were simply "immortal."

There was a test for the claim. Ross Harrison, who had invented tissue culture, said you could not call cells immortal until they had lived in glass longer than the animal they came from. A chicken, people said, lives about ten years. So the tenth birthday of the culture would be the day the claim came true.

## What they built

On January 17th 1912 Carrel's laboratory cut sixteen tiny pieces of heart and blood vessel from chick embryos, the unhatched chicks inside eggs, and put each piece in a drop of plasma. A few of the heart pieces kept beating in their drops. One of them beat, on and off, for 104 days. That beating scrap was the "chicken heart" the newspapers heard about when Carrel won the Nobel Prize that October, and the name stuck to everything that came afterwards, even though the beating stopped in the spring of 1912 and never came back.

What lived on were the ordinary cells of connective tissue, called fibroblasts, which look like little spindles. The laboratory kept them going by a routine. Every two days or so, someone lifted the square of glass, cut out the growing edge of the culture with a tiny knife, rinsed it in salt water for less than a minute, and put it in a fresh drop on a fresh glass. Each move was called a passage, and the passages were counted: 129 in the first year, 1,390 by 1919, 1,860 by the tenth birthday. Most of what had grown was thrown away each time. Only a small piece was kept.

The fresh drop was not plasma alone. From February 1912 it also had in it a few drops of what the laboratory called embryo juice. To make it, they took chick embryos about a week old, minced them with scissors in a little glass dish, spun the mush in a centrifuge so that the solid bits went to the bottom, and drew off the clear liquid from the top. Mixed half and half with plasma, that liquid made the cells grow much faster. Nobody ever found out exactly what in it did the trick.

## One drop

The first drop of embryo juice went into the culture on February 1st 1912. The laboratory's notebook, printed in 1913, says that the next day there was "active growth." Before the juice the cells had grown slowly; after it they grew fast. Carrel was sure the juice was food, and he spent years trying to find the nourishing part of it.

In that same month, February 1912, Carrel did another experiment. He wanted to prove to Jolly that his cells really divided instead of just crawling out of the piece of tissue. So he minced an embryo, spun it, and put a drop of the clear liquid from the top into a drop of plasma with no tissue in it at all. Cells appeared in the drop, and grew, and after a few passages they had made what he called "a real tissue." He had shown that the liquid off a spun embryo could carry living cells with it. He used that fact to win his argument, and then nobody thought about it again for fifty years.

The year 1912 was hard on the cultures. Of the sixteen pieces, five were alive in March. In June a young doctor named Albert Ebeling took over looking after them, and he would look after them for the next thirty-four years. By July there were five again, and by September 25th 1912 only one culture was left, "very small," Ebeling wrote, "but grew actively." Everything that came afterwards grew from that one small piece.

In 1914 Carrel wrote down the rule the laboratory lived by. Cells, he said, "may proliferate indefinitely," which means divide for ever, "if we exclude accidents." Every culture that had died had died of an accident: bad plasma, a germ, a slip of the knife. The rule had no other kind of death in it.

## The birthdays

For the people in the laboratory the culture was a timetable: bleed the hens, open the eggs, cut and wash and feed, every other day, for years. For everyone else it was a birthday. Every January 17th a New York newspaper rang the Institute to ask how the chicken heart was, and every year the answer was the same.

In April 1921 Carrel showed films of the cells to a scientific society, and the next day a New York newspaper described "two nurses and a doctor" in white, cutting the culture in two with sterilised instruments every forty-eight hours. Carrel said that if nothing had been cut away since 1912 the cells would have made "a ball larger than the earth." Another paper worked out that they would have made a rooster big enough to step across the Atlantic. On January 17th 1922, the tenth birthday, the *New York Herald* wrote that the tissue "kept on growing; it has kept on growing ever since," and that "so far as those who are watching and nourishing it can see," it was "immortal."

After that the story grew in the telling. Papers said the heart was still beating. One said it was at the wrong university. A visitor told *Time* magazine in 1925 that it doubled every day and, if it had not been "pared down," would be "a colossal monster overspreading all New York." In 1937 a radio writer in Chicago made a horror play called "Chicken Heart," in which a heart kept alive in a laboratory breaks its glass and grows until it covers the whole world. Children listened to it with the lights off, and the comedian Bill Cosby later told a famous story about hearing it as a boy, alone in the dark, and setting the sofa on fire. In 1942 Ebeling wrote an article to say that none of this was true: there was no beating heart, no jar, no snipping, no scientists watching it day and night. But one thing in the legend was right. The cells were never allowed to grow. Every two days most of them were thrown away and a small piece was kept and fed.

One visitor saw behind the routine. In the summer of 1930 a student from Chicago named Ralph Buchsbaum drove to New York to see the famous cells, because he had tried to keep chick cells alive himself and had never managed more than a year. Carrel was away. Ebeling showed him round but said the strain was "too delicate to risk being shown to visitors." So Buchsbaum asked a young technician, and she let him look. He said the cells looked as if they were dying. She told him, he remembered fifty years later, that "Dr. Carrel would be so upset if we lost the strain, we just add a few embryo cells now and then." Nobody can now check whether she was telling the truth or teasing him. It is the only report there is from inside the room.

## What happened

In 1939 Carrel, who was past the Institute's retirement age of sixty-five, retired and went home to France. The cells went with Ebeling and two technicians to a drug company's laboratory at Pearl River, outside New York, where they were used to test whether new medicines were poisonous to living cells. In January 1940 the newspaper that rang every year was told the chicken heart was dead, and printed its obituary. The next day it printed a correction: the cells were alive, somewhere else.

Carrel spent the war in France, running an institute for the government that was collaborating with the German occupiers. After Paris was freed he was accused of helping the enemy. He died in Paris on November 5th 1944, before any trial. The cells lived seventeen months longer. On April 26th 1946 Ebeling's laboratory threw them away. Nobody wrote down why.

And nobody could do it again. Laboratories all over the world used the same method and the same embryo juice, and their cultures slowed down and died after months. For years everyone blamed themselves. Carrel had shown the way, they said, so the fault must be theirs.

## What came after

In 1958 a young scientist in Philadelphia named Leonard Hayflick was growing normal human cells to look for cancer viruses, and he noticed that all his cultures died in the same way. Each one divided about fifty times and then stopped, no matter when it had been started. The same person fed them all from the same bottles, and young cultures thrived on the shelf beside old ones that were stopping. So the stop was not in the food or the glass. It was in the cells.

He proved it with a clever mix. He put male cells that had already divided forty times together with female cells that had divided twenty times. Twenty divisions later, only female cells were left. Nothing in the dish could have killed the old cells and spared the young ones beside them. In 1960 the journal that had printed all of Carrel's papers refused to print this one, because, the editor wrote, the biggest fact in tissue culture was that cells go on dividing for ever if they are fed properly. Another journal printed it in 1961. The number is now called the Hayflick limit, and the reason for it was found thirty years later: the tips of the chromosomes get a little shorter every time a cell divides, and when they are short enough the cell stops. The companion piece "[Fifty doublings](/deeper/chicken-heart/fifty-doublings/#young)" tells that story, and "[The juice](/deeper/chicken-heart/the-juice/#young)" tells the story of the feed.

Then Hayflick looked back at Carrel. He noticed that the strain had grown best in the weeks when embryo juice was added, and in 1965 he suggested what you may already have guessed: the juice, spun from minced embryos every day, had carried fresh living cells into the culture at every feeding. The "immortal" strain was being quietly restocked, and nobody knew it. Chick cells were measured too. On their own they divide about twenty-five times, in two or three months, and stop. Nobody has ever kept normal chick cells going for even two or three years.

In 1980 a historian named Jan Witkowski went through everything, tracked down Buchsbaum, and weighed three possibilities: that the cells had turned into an abnormal kind that grows for ever, which he thought unlikely; that living cells slipped in with the juice by accident, which he thought possible but not for thirty-four years running; or that people in the laboratory added fresh cells on purpose when the strain was failing, because they knew how much it meant to Carrel. He could not find anyone alive who had worked with the strain to ask. The people are gone, and the question cannot now be settled. What is certain is that the strain was fed with a liquid that could carry cells, every two days, for thirty-four years, and that nobody ever tested the feed.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**An experiment that cannot fail cannot teach you anything.** Carrel's rule was that cells live for ever unless there is an accident. So every culture that died was an accident, and did not count, and the one that lived was proof. If only one answer is allowed to count, you will get that answer for as long as you keep going, and the thirty-fourth year tells you nothing the first year did not.

**Test the thing you add.** The embryo juice was put into the culture at every feeding, and the growth that followed was taken as proof that it was food. In the very same month, Carrel showed that the same kind of juice could carry living cells. Nobody ever ran the culture without the juice to see what happened, because without it the cells died, and that only seemed to prove the juice was good. When the thing you add to make an experiment work could also produce the result by itself, leave it out once and see.

**If nobody from outside can watch, be suspicious.** Nobody from other laboratories dropped in on Carrel's. Ebeling would not show the strain to a visitor; a technician would. Whether or not she told the truth, a measurement that only its owners can see will, sooner or later, say what its owners need it to say.

**One success that nobody can repeat should count for less than many honest failures, not more.** For fifty years, every laboratory whose cells died blamed itself, because one laboratory's cells had not died. It took an experiment built to fail, old cells beside young ones, to make all those failures count.

So the lesson is simple. Before you start, decide what would make you stop. If nothing could, you are not running an experiment. You are keeping a birthday.
