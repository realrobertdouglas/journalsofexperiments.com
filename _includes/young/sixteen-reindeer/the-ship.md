## Oakland, 1926 to 1932

The *Bear* was a revenue cutter: a government ship that enforced the law at sea. In 1926 she went to Alaska for the last time, her thirty-fourth voyage north since 1885. A new cutter, the *Northland*, took over her work. In 1927 President Coolidge signed her over to the city of Oakland, California, as a museum ship, and on 3 May 1929 the Coast Guard decommissioned her, which means she was formally taken out of service.

She had been built at Dundee, in Scotland, in 1874, to hunt seals off Newfoundland, with oak and ironbark sides six to eight inches thick for pushing through ice, and her steam engine had never been changed. In 1930 she played the sealing steamer *Macedonia* in that year's film of Jack London's *The Sea Wolf*.

## Bear of Oakland, 1932 to 1935

In 1932 the explorer Richard Byrd bought her for $1,050. He needed an ice ship for his second expedition to Antarctica, to replace the *City of New York*, a barquentine, which is a sailing ship with square sails on the front mast and fore-and-aft sails on the others. He renamed her *Bear of Oakland*. In September 1933 she was in drydock in East Boston. A drydock is a basin that can be pumped empty so that workers can reach the parts of a ship that are usually under water. A photograph taken there shows men fitting a new propeller, and behind them the propeller post with its rows of bolt heads. Nobody would look closely at those bolts for eighty-eight years.

She left Boston on 25 September 1933, bound for Dunedin in New Zealand and the Ross Sea, still burning coal and carrying sails.

One of the fifty-six men on the expedition was a cook named Joseph Hill Jr., born in Canyon, Texas, in 1913, an Eagle Scout. He wrote to Byrd offering to do any job, and a year later was invited to Boston for an interview. He had no money for the train, so he hitch-hiked from Texas to Boston, and was signed on as a cook. He was twenty, the youngest man there, and was called Joe Boy. He went south as second cook on the *Bear of Oakland*, became Byrd's personal orderly, and in Antarctica drove tractors and helped build the base, Little America II, and the hut 123 miles south of it where Byrd spent the winter of 1934 alone. In 1937 a book came out under his name, *In Little America with Byrd*, written for young readers. He became an aircraft engineer and died on 15 October 1999.

After the expedition Byrd leased the ship to the Navy for one dollar a year.

## USS Bear, 1939 to 1944

In 1939 President Roosevelt put Byrd in charge of a United States Antarctic Service, and the Navy took the ship back. On 11 September 1939 she became USS *Bear*, under Lieutenant Commander Richard H. Cruzen. Her steam engine came out and a diesel went in, and her sailing rig was cut down so that she could carry a small seaplane. She made two voyages south, from 22 November 1939 to 5 June 1940 and from 10 October 1940 to 18 May 1941.

Then she went to Greenland. Her masts were cut down to two, guns were put aboard, and she was given a Coast Guard crew. On 12 September 1941 the *Northland*, the same cutter that had replaced her at Oakland, seized a Norwegian sealing ship, the *Buskø*, on the east coast of Greenland. She was supplying Norwegian stations under a permit from the German-controlled government in Norway. The *Bear* towed her to Boston, arriving on 14 October 1941. The capture is still often called the first American naval capture of the Second World War. That is disputed: the United States was not yet at war, Norway was not an enemy, and the crew were charged with entering the country without papers rather than held as prisoners.

On 8 May 1944 her log records her engine being taken out. On 17 May 1944 she was decommissioned for the last time, and she lay in Boston with no engine until 1948.

## Arctic Bear, 1948 to 1962

In 1948 Frank M. Shaw of Halifax, Nova Scotia, bought her for $5,199 and renamed her *Arctic Bear*, to send her sealing again. His company bought a replacement engine, sold the forty-five tons of zinc ballast the Navy had put in her bilges to pay for the work, and never fitted the engine: the seal hunt was shrinking and the refit cost more than she would earn. Ballast is heavy material carried low in a ship to keep her steady. In 1962 Alfred M. Johnston, who owned restaurants in Philadelphia, bought her to be a floating seafood restaurant and museum. She was repaired at Mahone Bay, Nova Scotia, and a new bear figurehead was carved for her bow.

## 17 to 19 March 1963

She left Halifax on 17 March 1963, St Patrick's Day, behind the tug *Irving Birch*, bound for Philadelphia, with two caretakers aboard, Percy Coffin and Henry Dingle of Dartmouth. She had no engine and no ballast, and she rode high in the water. A gale came up. Early on 19 March, about a hundred miles south of Shelburne, Nova Scotia, the tow line parted. Her front mast broke in two and came down, and the hull was holed. A Coast Guard aircraft from Boston dropped a rubber dinghy; Coffin and Dingle climbed into it and the tug picked them up. At dusk the tug's captain told a reporter by radio-telephone that he could just make out her riding light. She sank some time after 9:10 that night, though one account says the morning.

## The search, 1979 to 2021

In the spring of 1979 eleven Coast Guard Academy cadets, four advisers and Harold Edgerton of MIT spent two weeks looking for her from the buoy tender *Conifer*. A buoy tender is a ship that places and services the buoys that mark channels. Edgerton had invented side-scan sonar, which sends sound out sideways from a towed instrument and draws a picture of the sea floor from the echoes. It found nothing.

From 14 to 28 September 2019 the Coast Guard cutter *Bear*, named after the original, carried a team from NOAA, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, over the area with side-scan sonar. They mapped sixty-two square miles of sea floor and found a wooden wreck about 90 miles due south of Cape Sable, Nova Scotia.

In June 2021 the Coast Guard buoy tender *Sycamore* took the team back with a remotely operated vehicle, an unmanned submersible with cameras that is steered by a cable from the ship, and in about 200 feet of water it recorded some thirty hours of video. Historians compared the footage with the ship's plans and old photographs. The bow was the most intact part, and its iron staples and steel sheathing matched photographs of the *Bear*. Where the engine room should be there was an empty compartment and no engine, as the log of 8 May 1944 said. A few metres beyond the stern lay a cast-metal propeller post with rows of bolt heads. Set beside the East Boston photograph of September 1933, the bolts matched. The team concluded that the wreck was the *Bear* "with a reasonable degree of certainty".

On 14 October 2021, at a pier in Boston, Rear Admiral Nancy Hann announced the finding for NOAA and the Coast Guard. Alongside the pier was the icebreaker *Healy*, named for Michael Healy, the captain who had carried the first sixteen reindeer in the *Bear*. The wreck lies in Canadian waters.

What is left of the ship is a bow, a wheel, some tanks and a propeller post, ninety miles south of Cape Sable, known by her bolts. The engine had been taken out in Boston, the ballast sold in Halifax, the masts lost in the gale. What remained was the part built in Dundee to meet the ice, and it was the ice-bow that was recognised.
