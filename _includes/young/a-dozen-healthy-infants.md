## A baby in a hospital

In 1919 a baby boy lived in a hospital in Baltimore, in the United States. The hospital was called Johns Hopkins, and the building he lived in was the Harriet Lane Home, which was the part of the hospital for sick children. He was not sick. His mother was a wet nurse: a woman who was paid to feed other people's babies with her own milk, because the sick babies on the wards needed it. Wet nurses lived in the Home, and their own babies lived there with them. The boy had been born in March 1919, and he had spent almost his whole life inside the hospital. He had never seen a dog, or a cat, or a rabbit.

Next door to the Home, joined to it by a corridor, was a building called the Phipps Clinic, and in it was the laboratory of a psychologist named John B. Watson. A psychologist is a scientist who studies how people think and behave. Watson was forty-one, famous, and sure of one big idea: that almost everything a person does is learned, not born in them. He had an assistant, Rosalie Rayner, who was twenty-one and had just finished college. They borrowed the wet nurses' babies for tests and paid the mothers a dollar a visit.

The boy was the one they chose for the most famous test of all. In the paper they wrote about it they called him "Albert B." They said he was "healthy from birth and one of the best developed youngsters ever brought to the hospital," that he weighed twenty-one pounds at nine months, and that he was "on the whole stolid and unemotional." Stolid means calm and hard to upset. That was why they picked him. "We felt that we could do him relatively little harm," they wrote.

## What Watson wanted to find out

Watson believed that a newborn baby is afraid of only two things: a sudden loud noise, and suddenly being dropped. Everything else people are afraid of, he said, they learn. Somewhere, some time, a mouse was there at a moment when a child was frightened by something else, and the fear stuck to the mouse. He called this a conditioned fear, after the Russian scientist Pavlov, who had taught dogs to dribble at the sound of a bell by ringing it every time they were fed.

It was a good idea with a problem: nobody had ever shown it happening. So in the winter of 1919 Watson decided to make a fear, on purpose, in a baby, and watch it grow.

He wrote down four questions. Could a baby be taught to fear a white rat by showing him the rat and making a loud noise at the same moment? If so, would the fear spread to other furry things? Would it last? And, the fourth question: once a fear had been made, how could it be taken away again?

## What they built

The laboratory had a table with a mattress on it, in a small room. Behind the table hung a steel bar, four feet long and as thick as a finger, and there was a hammer to hit it with. The sound it made was enormous. There was a basket with a white rat in it, and a rabbit, a dog, a monkey, a fur coat, a package of cotton wool, some masks, a set of wooden blocks for the baby to play with, and a newspaper that could be set on fire.

There was also a cine camera. Watson had persuaded the university to give him $450 for a thousand feet of film, a great deal of money in 1919, because he wanted a record that people could see. The film still exists, and the pictures on this page come from it.

In the first week of December 1919, when Albert was eight months and twenty-six days old, they showed him everything. He reached for the rat. He reached for the rabbit and the dog and the monkey and the burning paper. "At no time did this infant ever show fear in any situation," they wrote. "The infant practically never cried." Then Rayner made him look at her hand, and Watson, behind him, hit the bar. He jumped and held his breath. The second time, his lips trembled. The third time, he cried. It was the first time anyone in the laboratory had ever seen him cry.

## The seven blows

Then they waited two months. Nobody knows why. Watson's marriage was breaking up, and the university closed for Christmas.

On February 13th 1920, when Albert was eleven months and three days old, the rat was taken out of the basket. Albert reached for it with his left hand. As his hand touched it, the bar was struck behind his head. He jumped and fell forward, "burying his face in the mattress. He did not cry, however." They did it once more. He jumped, fell forward and whimpered. Then they stopped, "in order not to disturb the child too seriously," and did not test him for a week.

A week later the rat came out again, with no noise. Albert looked at it hard and did not reach. When it was moved closer he reached a little, and when it sniffed his hand he pulled the hand back. He played with his blocks as usual. Then the bar was struck five more times, each time as the rat touched him. He fell over, turned his head away, whimpered, and at last, on the seventh blow, cried. Then they showed him the rat with no noise at all. "The instant the rat was shown the baby began to cry," the notes say. "Almost instantly he turned sharply to the left, fell over on left side, raised himself on all fours and began to crawl away so rapidly that he was caught with difficulty before reaching the edge of the table."

That is the moment the experiment is famous for. Seven blows on a steel bar had taught a baby to be afraid of a rat.

## Did the fear spread?

Five days later they brought in the rabbit, which Albert had never been frightened of and which had never been paired with the noise. He leaned away from it, whimpered, and burst into tears, and when it touched him he crawled away crying. The dog made him shrink back. The fur coat made him fret and crawl. The cotton wool he kicked away, though he was happy to play with the paper it was wrapped in. Then Watson put his own head down to see whether Albert would play with his hair. Albert would not. Two women in the room did the same thing, and Albert played with their hair at once. Then Watson put on a Santa Claus mask with a white beard, and Albert pulled away from that too.

So the fear had spread, from the rat to furry things, and to the man who had hit the bar.

But it was not as strong as the famous moment makes it sound. Five days after that, the rat alone only made Albert lean away, with no crying, and the notes say the reaction "was much less marked" than before, so they hit the bar again to "freshen" it. When they carried everything into a big bright lecture room and tried again, the rat at first did nothing, and had to be freshened again. The dog, which had been quiet, suddenly barked three times six inches from Albert's face, and he wailed. So did the grown-ups, the notes admit.

Then they left him alone for a month. On March 31st 1920, when he was a year and three weeks old, they tested him one last time. The mask made him cry when his hand was pushed against it. The rat he let crawl towards him without moving; when it was put against his chest he covered his eyes with both hands. The rabbit he pushed away with his feet, then reached out and touched its ear. When Watson put Albert's hand on the rabbit's back, the baby pulled it away and sucked his thumb. Whenever his thumb was in his mouth, the notes say, the fear stopped, and the experimenters had to pull the thumb out to film it.

That same day, Albert's mother took him away from the hospital. The hospital's note, found many years later, says: "Mother suddenly decided to leave the hospital."

## The question nobody answered

The fourth question, how to take the fear away, was never tried. Watson and Rayner listed the ways they would have used, such as feeding him sweets while the rat was there, but Albert had gone, and that autumn Watson was sacked from the university, because he and Rayner were in love and the Baltimore newspapers had found out. He went to New York to work in advertising and married Rayner. He never did another experiment at a university.

He kept telling the story, though, in magazines and books, and each time the details drifted. The bar got shorter. The baby got heavier. The rat became one Albert had "played with for weeks." The calm baby who "practically never cried" became a baby who had never cried at all until Watson made him. And the baby who was "taken from the hospital" became, in a book of 1924, a baby "adopted by an out-of-town family." That last detail was wrong, and it sent people searching in the wrong places for ninety years.

In the same book, Watson wrote the sentence everybody knows: "Give me a dozen healthy infants, well-formed, and my own specified world to bring them up in and I'll guarantee to take any one at random and train him to become any type of specialist I might select." He never had a dozen. He had one. And the very next sentence, which almost nobody quotes, says: "I am going beyond my facts and I admit it."

The fourth question was answered in the end, but by somebody else. In 1924 a student of Watson's called Mary Cover Jones found a boy called Peter, nearly three years old, who was terrified of rats, rabbits, fur coats and cotton wool, though nobody had taught him to be. Day after day she brought a rabbit a little nearer while Peter sat in a high chair eating something he liked, and let him watch other children who were not afraid. After three months he let the rabbit nibble his fingers. That is how fears are still treated today. Peter, too, was only one child, and Jones said so.

## Who was Albert?

Watson and Rayner's paper went into every psychology textbook as the proof that fear is learned, and the textbooks got it wrong in all sorts of ways: Albert was an orphan, the fear had been made with a rabbit, the coat was white, Watson had cured him before he went home. A psychologist named Ben Harris wrote all this up in 1979 under the title "Whatever Happened to Little Albert?" He meant it as a joke about how badly scientists tell their own history. People took it as a question, and set out to find the baby.

It took thirty years. A psychologist named Hall Beck worked out from the ages in the paper that Albert must have been born in the first half of March 1919. A census, which is a count of everyone living in a place, had been taken at the hospital on January 2nd 1920, in the middle of the experiment. It listed three women as "foster mothers," a word that could mean wet nurses: Pearl Barger, Ethel Carter and Arvilla Merritte. The hospital's birth register showed that Arvilla Merritte had a boy on March 9th 1919. Her grandson knew his name: Douglas. He had died in 1925, aged six, of a disease that makes fluid build up inside the skull. In 2009 Beck announced that psychology's lost boy had been found.

Then it got complicated. Three years later Beck and some new colleagues found Douglas's medical file. It showed that he had been very ill from the spring of 1919, with a swelling head and failing eyes. If Douglas was Albert, then the "healthy" baby in the paper was not healthy at all, and Watson must have known and lied. Newspapers and magazines said so.

Then another team, led by Russell Powell and Nancy Digdon in Canada, looked at the first name on the census list. Pearl Barger was sixteen. She had married in 1921, and she had a son born in 1919, two years before the wedding: William Albert Barger, called Albert all his life, who had died in 2007. His medical file was still in the hospital's archive. His mother had been a wet nurse in the Harriet Lane Home from May 1919 to March 31st 1920. He had been born on March 10th 1919, one day after Douglas. In December 1919 he weighed nearly twenty-two pounds, a big, healthy baby; Douglas, at the same age, weighed under fifteen. And he had left the hospital on March 31st 1920, at exactly a year and twenty-one days old, which is exactly the age the paper gives for the last session. Douglas had left a week earlier.

Albert Barger grew up in Baltimore, worked for an airline, and never knew he had spent his first year in a hospital. His niece remembered one thing about him. He did not like dogs. When he visited, the family dogs were shut in another room, and when a dog barked he would sometimes put his hands over his ears. He had also, as a boy, seen his own dog killed in an accident. Nobody can say which of those things made him dislike dogs, and he died before anyone thought to ask him about a rat.

The two teams still do not agree about which baby was Albert. But the fact that two babies, born a day apart to two wet nurses in the same building, could both fit the paper tells you something about the paper.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**One is not a sample.** Watson tested one baby, and the paper says so in its second paragraph: "Experimental work has been done so far on only one child." Everyone who read the paper read past that line. One baby cannot prove that fear is learned, any more than one coin that comes up heads proves that all coins do. Watson himself wrote that he was "going beyond my facts." The textbooks left that bit out.

**If you only have one subject, everything depends on who that subject was.** Because there was only one Albert, it mattered enormously whether he was a healthy baby or a very sick one. A healthy baby makes the experiment honest. A sick baby makes it a lie. The experiment itself could not tell you which, so a hundred years later people had to dig through birth registers and medical files to find out. That is the work a proper experiment does at the start, with more than one baby, so that nobody has to do it afterwards.

**Write down what actually happened, and then leave it alone.** The notes from 1920 show a fear that kept fading and had to be freshened. Watson's later tellings made it tidier every time, and the textbooks tidied it further, until the story had a cure in it that never happened. The notes were the only honest record, and almost nobody read them.

**A film made to persuade is not the same as a record made to check.** Watson bought the film so that people would believe him, and it worked: the five minutes of Albert are the reason the experiment is remembered. But the same five minutes were later used to prove that the baby was ill, and then to prove that he was not. A picture that can prove both sides is not proof.

**The most useful question was the one they gave up.** How to take a fear away would have helped Albert, and it was the only question with a real test in it. They had a month and spent it on something else. The answer came from Mary Cover Jones and Peter, and it is still in use.

So the lesson is simple. Before you believe a famous result, find the number of people it was tested on. If the number is one, you have not been told a law. You have been told a story about one baby, and you do not even know his name.
