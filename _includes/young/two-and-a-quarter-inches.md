## The bridge

In 1907 the biggest bridge in the world was being built across the St Lawrence River, seven miles above the city of Quebec in Canada. It was a cantilever bridge: a bridge built out from each shore like two enormous steel arms reaching towards each other over the water, with a short piece to be hung between the fingertips in the middle. Each arm was balanced on a stone pier standing in the river. The gap between the two piers was 1,800 feet, 90 feet more than the famous Forth Bridge in Scotland, which had been the longest in the world. The steel towers over the piers rose 400 feet above the water.

The bridge belonged to a company in Quebec, but the engineering was done far away. The design was drawn by the Phoenix Bridge Company of Phoenixville, in Pennsylvania in the United States, which also made every piece of steel in its own works and sent its own men to put it up. Every drawing was checked by one man: Theodore Cooper, a famous bridge engineer in New York, sixty-eight years old, who had written the rule book that American bridge builders used. His doctor would not let him travel. He checked the whole bridge from his office on Broadway, and never once saw it while it was being built.

On the site, the Quebec company had two inspectors: a young engineer named Norman McLure, who wrote to Cooper every week, and an older man named Kinloch, who had spent his life putting up steelwork. The Phoenix company's foreman in charge of the men was Ben Yenser. Its engineer on the site was a young man named Birks.

## The bridge gets longer, and heavier

When the bridge was first designed in 1899, the gap between the piers was going to be 1,600 feet. In 1900 Cooper made it 1,800 feet, so that the piers could stand in shallower water nearer the shores, where they were cheaper and safer to build. That was a sensible change. But a longer bridge is a heavier bridge, and a heavier bridge presses harder on every piece of itself.

Before you can design a bridge you have to work out how much it will weigh, because the steel has to be strong enough to hold itself up. In 1904, before the detailed drawings existed, the Phoenix company's designer estimated the weight, and Cooper used the estimate to decide how strong each piece had to be. The estimate was too low. The finished bridge was going to weigh about 73 million pounds instead of about 63 million. Nobody did the sum again until 1906, when the first pieces were already made and standing in Quebec. Cooper worked out that every piece would be carrying about 7 per cent more than he had allowed, and decided there was nothing to be done: "we would have to submit to it," he said.

There was one more thing. In 1903 the Canadian government had wanted to appoint a second engineer to check Cooper's work. Cooper refused: "This puts me in the position of a subordinate, which I cannot accept." The government gave way. After that, the only check on Cooper was Cooper.

## The chords

The most important pieces of the bridge were the lower chords: the great steel members along the bottom of each arm, which carried the whole weight down to the piers. Each chord was about 57 feet long and made of four thin steel plates standing on edge, taller than a man, held apart by a lattice of small steel bars riveted across them, like the sides of a crate. A chord like this is being squeezed from both ends, and a squeezed column has one great enemy: bending. Once it starts to bow in the middle, the squeeze makes it bow more, and more, until it folds. The lattice bars were there to keep the four plates straight. Nobody had ever tested a chord this big, because no machine existed that could squeeze one hard enough.

## Three quarters of an inch

By the summer of 1907 the two arms on the south side of the river were finished, and the men were building the middle piece out from the tip of the south arm, piece by piece. Every piece they added pressed harder on the chords near the pier. In August, when the riveters opened up the joints between chords, they found some of the plates slightly bent out of line. For three weeks letters went back and forth between McLure, Cooper and the Phoenix company about what had bent them. The Phoenix company said the bends had been made in the factory. McLure said they had happened since the chords were put under load. Cooper wrote: "None of the explanations for the bent chord stand the test of logic."

About August 19th McLure measured one rib of the ninth chord on the west side of the south arm, the chord the men called A9L. It was three quarters of an inch out of line.

## Two and a quarter inches

On Tuesday August 27th 1907, at about nine in the morning, Kinloch walked round the big post at the pier and looked along chord A9L. All four of its plates were bent, the same way. "It was a big bend," he said later, "and I was satisfied right off that there was something going on." He called Yenser and Birks up to look. Yenser said: "That has never been there before; I have been over this chord too many times." He said he would not put up any more steel until he knew what was wrong. Birks laughed at him, and said that if he condemned that chord he was condemning the whole bridge, and that the bend might always have been there.

They fetched McLure and measured it, with a cord stretched tight along the chord and a rule held from the cord to each plate. The rib that had been three quarters of an inch out of line a week before was now two and a quarter inches out.

McLure spent the rest of the day drawing it and writing to Cooper in New York and to the Phoenix company in Pennsylvania. He did not telephone or telegraph, because the men were already worried and nobody wanted to alarm them. The letter went by the evening post. That night he went into Quebec to show the drawings to the company's chief engineer, Mr Hoare, and they agreed that McLure should take the train to New York and explain it to Cooper himself.

On Wednesday morning Yenser changed his mind. He told Kinloch he had "too many men out" with nothing to do, so he moved the crane forward and went on building. McLure thought it was "poor policy", but nobody thought the bridge was about to fall: the crane added only a tiny extra load. Hoare came out to the bridge and agreed that stopping the work "would be very bad on all concerned". At half past twelve McLure left for the train.

That afternoon Birks went down to the chord and came back with a comforting idea. The bend ran into a joint that had been riveted up in June. So, he reasoned, the bend must have been there in June, so the chord must have been bent before it was ever put into the bridge. Nobody measured the chord again. Birks wrote a letter to Phoenixville saying the chord had probably always been bent.

## Thursday August 29th

McLure got to New York at half past seven on Thursday morning. Cooper did not come in until nearly half past eleven. He read the letter, questioned McLure hard to see "whether the facts given were actual or whether he had been scared", and said: "It is very serious." McLure told him the men were still building. "By no means right," said Cooper. He wanted to telegraph the bridge to stop, but there was nobody in charge there except the foreman, and he did not think a foreman would take orders from him. So he telegraphed the Phoenix company instead: "Add no more load to bridge till after due consideration of facts. McLure will be over at five o'clock." The telegram left New York at sixteen minutes past twelve and reached Phoenixville at a quarter past one.

But the Phoenix company had already made up its mind that morning. Its engineers had telephoned the bridge at about half past ten and told Birks and Yenser to carry on. Birks met Kinloch on the bridge and said: "Well, it is all right; I have got word from Phoenixville that they have a record that these chords were bent before." Kinloch "laughed at him and told him that it was not." The Phoenix company also telegraphed Hoare that the chords were just as they had left the factory. Hoare, in his office in Quebec, "felt quite comfortable that day about it." McLure had promised to telegraph Kinloch whatever Cooper decided. He did not do it.

The chief engineer of the Phoenix company got back to his office at about three o'clock and found Cooper's telegram. He did nothing except ask two colleagues to wait with him for McLure. McLure arrived at five. The four men talked about the chord and decided to wait for Birks's letter, which would come in the morning.

At the bridge, at 5.31 that afternoon, the ninth chord gave way.

## The fall

The timekeeper, Joseph Huot, was walking out along the bridge when the air pipe for the riveting hammers snapped beside him. He turned and ran for the shore, and the planks were dropping away under his feet as he ran. The whole south half of the bridge, about 17,000 tons of steel, went into the river in about fifteen seconds. A workman named Haley, sitting on the end of a crane far out over the water, said: "the first thing I knew I caught myself going through the air. I realized that the iron fell very much faster than I did." He came up in the river among floating planks and was picked up by a boat. Kinloch, standing on the shore, watched the great steel posts sink straight down "as if they were ice pillars whose ends were rapidly melting away."

There were 86 men on the bridge. Eleven of them lived. Many of the dead were Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawake, near Montreal. One of the men, named Splicer, had stayed away that day because he felt nervous. He told the inquiry that the night before, seven or eight of the Kahnawake men had sat talking about the bent chord. He had made a list of their names. "Are any of these men living?" he was asked. "No, sir, none of them." Yenser and Birks died too. Cooper was telephoned at nine that night and told that the bridge was in the river. When McLure saw him a few days later, all Cooper said was: "Well, it's that chord."

## What the inquiry found

The next day the Canadian government appointed three engineers to find out what had happened. They worked for six months. They read every letter, questioned every survivor, and had a model of chord A9L built at one third of its real size and squeezed in a testing machine until it broke. It broke suddenly, with a bang, when the little lattice bars gave way. A second model, with lattice bars twice as strong, carried much more.

Their report said the bridge fell because the lower chords were badly designed: the lattice bars were far too weak to hold the plates straight once they began to bend. It said the weight of the bridge had been guessed too low and never worked out again, which was "a grave error". It said the two engineers who designed and approved the bridge had made "errors in judgment" in "one of the most difficult professional problems of the day", and that nobody then knew enough about how huge steel columns behave to design such a bridge safely. And it said two things about the last three days. First, nothing anyone could have done after August 27th would have saved the bridge: the chord was already going. Second, "The loss of life on August 29, 1907, might have been prevented by the exercise of better judgment" by the men in charge. The men could have been taken off the bridge. They were left on it while everyone waited for a letter.

Cooper did not agree that the bridge could not have been saved. He said that if someone on the Tuesday had braced the bent chord with "three hours' work and $100 worth of timber and bolts", the bridge would have stood. The inquiry tested that idea and said the chord would soon have failed anyway. But both sides agreed on the men.

## After

The Canadian government took over the bridge and had it designed again, wider and heavier, with nearly twice as much steel. On September 11th 1916 the new middle piece, weighing 5,000 tons, was being lifted into place between the two arms when a steel casting broke and the whole piece fell into the river. A second one was built, and on September 20th 1917 it was lifted into place. Trains were crossing it by 1919, and it still stands. Theodore Cooper retired in 1907. He died in New York in August 1919, aged eighty.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Measuring something is not the same as telling the right person.** Kinloch and McLure found the bend and measured it exactly. The number, two and a quarter inches, was true, and it meant the bridge was about to fall. But the number went by post, because nobody wanted to frighten the men, and it went to two offices hundreds of miles away. The one person in Quebec who could have stopped the work was sitting in his office feeling comfortable. A measurement only helps if it reaches someone who can act on it, in time.

**When people want something to be all right, they find reasons.** Birks, the Phoenix company and Mr Hoare all decided that the chord had always been bent, because that meant nothing was wrong. Nobody measured it a second time to find out. The men who had measured were worried; the men who had only reasoned were calm. Be careful of a comforting explanation that nobody has checked.

**If you get the first sum wrong, every sum after it is wrong too.** The weight of the bridge was estimated before the drawings existed. Every calculation after that was done carefully and correctly, but all of them started from the wrong number. Nobody went back to the beginning. When you change a plan, redo the first sum, not just the last one.

**One famous person is not a check.** The government wanted a second engineer to check Cooper's work, and Cooper refused. Everyone trusted him because of his reputation, and the inquiry said that trusting him was reasonable. It was reasonable, and it was still a mistake. A check is only a check if it is done by someone else.

So the lesson is simple. When your instrument tells you something is wrong, stop, measure it again, and get the people out of the way while you argue about why.
