## The man

Richard Byrd was an American naval officer who became famous for flying. In 1926 he said he had flown over the North Pole. In 1927 he flew across the Atlantic. In 1929 he flew to the South Pole and back from a camp he had built on the ice at the edge of Antarctica, which he called Little America. The government made him an admiral.

In 1933 he went back to Antarctica with two ships and fifty-six men. They reached Little America in January 1934 and dug the old camp out from under four winters of snow. When they turned a switch, the electric lights still came on.

The expedition had a weather man lent by the United States Weather Bureau, William Haines, who was known to everyone as "Cyclone." It had a chief scientist, Dr Thomas Poulter, who was second in command. It had a radio engineer, John Dyer, and a radio operator, Amory Waite, and a mechanic called Pete Demas who looked after the tractors. And it had a doctor from New Zealand, Louis Potaka, who had joined at a few days' notice.

## The idea

Antarctica is a continent, and in 1934 nobody had ever measured its weather in winter away from the coast. All the weather records came from camps by the sea, where the ocean keeps the air a little warmer. Byrd wanted a second weather station far inland: a small hut on the great flat ice shelf, where a man would read the thermometers and the barometer at set hours every day through the whole winter night, when the sun does not rise for four months. His readings would be compared with the readings at Little America, and the difference would show what the inside of the continent was like.

The plan was for three men to live in the hut. Then the ships were late, unloading was slow, and there was not enough time or fuel to set up a proper station. The hut was placed closer than planned, about 123 miles south of Little America by the trail, and Byrd decided to live in it alone.

Some people thought this was a bad idea. An explorer called Sir Hubert Wilkins said that one man "could not hope to maintain these observations without extended intervals for rest." A man has to sleep. Byrd's own reason was simple. He said, "I am doing it because I am anxious to do it."

## The hut

The hut was a wooden box thirteen feet long, nine feet wide and seven feet high: about the size of a small garden shed. It was sunk into a pit until its roof was level with the snow, so that the wind went over it. Inside were a bunk, a table, books, a gramophone and a radio. Tunnels dug in the snow led to the food and the fuel. On the roof stood a box holding the thermometers, and above it a twelve-foot pole with a weather vane and the spinning cups that measure the wind.

The hut was heated by a stove that burned kerosene, a kind of oil. A small petrol engine made electricity for the radio. Both of them gave off fumes. A stove like it had already been thrown out at Little America because its fumes had made several men ill. Everyone thought this one had been fixed.

Tractors drove the stores south along a trail marked with little orange flags. On March 24th 1934 the men dug the pit and bolted the hut together, and Byrd flew down in a small plane. On March 28th the tractors drove away north, and he was alone. "I am not a radio operator," he had said, "so the radio will probably fail." If it did, nobody was to worry. He also said that no one should make anything of what he was doing.

## The first two months

Three times a week Byrd talked to Little America by radio, in Morse code, tapping out dots and dashes with a key. He sent the weather. He sent orders for the spring. In April he sent a short message that was read out on the radio in England: "The perils of my isolation are greatly exaggerated. Tranquillity is a pearl of great price."

He got up before eight every morning for the first reading, climbed the ladder through the trapdoor, and read the thermometers. Five times a day he went up to look at the southern lights. When frost built up on the weather vane he climbed the pole to brush it off. He said later that April and May were the freest months of his life. There were no telephones, no traffic, no formal dinners, "no bores, no stiff collars."

It was very cold. The hut was only twenty degrees above zero Fahrenheit inside, because he used the stove as little as he could to save fuel. Outside, on May 20th, the thermometer fell to 72 degrees below zero. The oil in the clocks froze.

## The poison

Then the fumes began to work on him. Carbon monoxide is a gas you cannot see or smell. It comes from stoves and engines, and it stops your blood from carrying oxygen. A little makes your head ache. More makes you dizzy and sick and weak. A lot kills you.

Byrd's eyes began to smart. He got headaches and strange pains. One day at the end of May, running the engine for the radio, he was badly poisoned. He said afterwards: "The first two weeks of June were really hell. I did not think I had a chance." He wrote in his diary that if he cut the stove down, he froze; if he kept it up, the fumes poisoned him. "To cut the poison, I must endure the freezing cold. To reduce the cold, I must endure the poison."

He spent most of his time in his sleeping bag. Food made him feel sick, so he ate a mouthful at a time, sometimes half frozen. On June 15th he hung a note over his table saying where the letters he had written to his family could be found, in case he died. The same day, on the radio, he told Little America that his health was good, that he had put back the weight he had lost, and that "either my cooking is getting better or I am holding my own in spite of it."

Two days later, in the middle of a radio conversation, he suddenly said: "Let us make this short. I will tell you later why." He had felt dizzy. He explained afterwards that the engine had been giving off fumes. The scientists at Little America told him to be careful. That was all.

And still he climbed the ladder twice a day and read the instruments. The Weather Bureau's printed table shows where he could not. On June 1st, 2nd and 4th a reading is missing. On June 18th, the day after the radio conversation, two are missing. But the column of numbers goes on, every day.

## The messages

Why did he not ask for help? He said later that he did not want anyone to risk their lives driving a tractor 123 miles across the ice in the dark and the cold to fetch him. So his messages said that all was well. Sometimes he was too weak to turn the handle of the small emergency radio. Then he could hear Little America calling and could not answer, and he was afraid that his silence would give him away.

At Little America, Charles Murphy, who ran the radio and had known Byrd for years, became suspicious and kept asking him about his health. Byrd kept turning the questions aside. In July his radio went silent for nine days. When it came back, he mentioned that he had "a bad arm," and said nothing more.

Dr Poulter had a scientific reason to go south: he wanted a second observer at the hut to watch for meteors. Byrd agreed, but insisted that every possible precaution be taken first. The journey would be by tractor, in the dark, at sixty or seventy degrees below zero, across a field of crevasses, which are deep cracks in the ice hidden under snow.

## Three starts

On July 20th the tractor set out with five men. Fifty miles out, they could not find the trail: the flags were buried under the snow. They had zigzagged 130 miles to make fifty. The temperature was 71 below. Byrd ordered them back, and they went back, and a blizzard half buried the tractor on the way.

On August 4th Poulter tried again, with just two men, Waite and Demas, and a load of petrol and two months' food. Twenty-three miles out, the tractor broke down. They went back.

On August 8th, at three in the morning, they set out a third time in another tractor. Poulter and Waite skied ahead in the dark, planting a flag every hundred feet, while Demas drove behind them. The generator failed. Their faces kept freezing. Byrd, who knew they were coming, kept a lamp burning at the top of his weather pole.

On the evening of August 10th they radioed that they were eighty-one miles out. Then nothing. Little America broadcast into the dark, hoping Byrd could hear: "They are at 81. They are at 81. If nothing stops them, they may be there at midnight. Keep the light burning." Just after midnight the radio engineer, Dyer, shouted "There he comes!" It was Waite, sending from Byrd's own set. They had seen the light ten miles away and had not stopped.

Byrd was up to meet them. "Hello, fellows," he said. "Come on down and get warm. I have some hot soup for you." He had not seen another person for four and a half months. He was thin and weak and his hair was long. Then he took the radio key himself and tapped out, slowly: "Tell my friends not to worry. I'm all right."

## What Poulter found

Poulter radioed back that Byrd was "too proud to call for help." He had spent at least half his time in his sleeping bag. He had been living on powdered milk and tinned vegetables. He had written a second note, for the rescuers, in case they came too late, and he hid it when they walked in.

Poulter also said something important about the messages. "As I think back," he said, "I see things in his messages that were put there to keep us from realizing his condition." Everyone at Little America had read what the messages said. Poulter had thought about how they were sent.

And Poulter looked at the weather records. He said: "I marvel at how complete a set of scientific data he has obtained and how painstaking he has been with it."

Byrd was too weak to travel. The three men stayed with him in a hut that Poulter said was "about as crowded as a telephone booth when only one man is in it." They cut a new air vent, took over the weather readings, and waited for him to get stronger. On August 27th he saw the sun for the first time in four months. In October a plane flew down and brought him home. The pilot had to circle for half an hour before he could find the hut under the snow.

## The lowest temperature

The Weather Bureau printed every reading from the hut in 1939, in a book of tables. The inside of the continent turned out to be about eleven and a half degrees colder than the coast, every month. The coldest month was July, with an average of 51.7 degrees below zero.

The coldest moment is the one in the title. At four in the morning on July 21st, the recording thermometer, which draws a line on paper all by itself, reached 83 degrees below zero. But that morning Byrd did not climb the ladder to read the thermometers; he was too ill. That evening he read them, and the lowest they showed was 78 below. The Weather Bureau's rule was that the number that counts is the one a person has read. So the official lowest temperature at the hut is 78 below, and the 83 is in a footnote.

Afterwards Byrd was asked why he had not told anyone how ill he was in July, when the tractor was halfway to him and he ordered it back. He shrugged. "How could I?" he said. "Would you?"

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**If you only have one person, that person is also your only instrument.** Byrd had a thermometer for the cold and a barometer for the air pressure and spinning cups for the wind. He had nothing to measure carbon monoxide except his own body: his smarting eyes, his aching arms, his dizziness. The thing that nearly killed him was the one thing at the station that nobody had thought to measure.

**Listen to how a message is sent, not just what it says.** Byrd's messages said that all was well, and they were sent slowly and with difficulty by a man who could barely turn the handle. Everyone read the words. Poulter noticed the hand. That is why he went.

**A brave rule can be a dangerous rule.** Byrd would not call for help because he did not want others to risk their lives for him. That is a kind rule. But it meant that the people who could have helped him were kept in the dark, and they went anyway, three times, into far more danger than if he had told them the truth in June.

**Honest data from a dishonest reporter.** The strange thing is that Byrd never faked a single weather reading. When he could not read the thermometers, he left the space blank. He lied about himself and told the truth about the weather. The blanks in the table are the most honest record of the winter there is.

So the lesson is simple. When one person is both the scientist and the experiment, somebody at the other end of the line has to watch the person as carefully as the numbers.
