## The ship

Nearly four hundred years ago, Sweden was at war with Poland. The king of Sweden, Gustav II Adolf, needed ships to carry his soldiers across the sea and to fight the Polish ships. He wanted the biggest warship anyone had seen in the Baltic, with more guns than any other. He named her after his own family. She was the Vasa.

She was built in Stockholm, the capital of Sweden, at the navy's shipyard, where three or four hundred people worked in the summer. She was about forty-seven metres long. Her tallest mast was more than fifty metres high, as tall as a fifteen-storey building. She carried sixty-four bronze guns, and forty-eight of them were big ones that fired a cannonball weighing ten kilograms. Most warships of that time had one deck of heavy guns. The Vasa had two, one above the other. She was covered in hundreds of carved and painted figures: lions, soldiers, kings and mermaids. Inside the lid of every gun port there was a carved lion's face, so that when the ports were opened the lions grinned at the enemy.

She was built by a Dutch shipbuilder named Henrik Hybertsson. In those days nobody could work out on paper whether a ship would float upright. There was no mathematics for it yet; that came a hundred years later. A shipbuilder built a new ship by copying what had worked before and making it a bit bigger. The king sent his own list of measurements for the Vasa, and the builder promised to follow them "as far as possible". Then he fell ill, stayed in bed for eight weeks, and handed the work to his assistant, Hein Jacobsson. In 1627 Henrik Hybertsson died. The ship was finished by a man following a dead man's plan, which the king had approved.

## Why a ship can tip over

A ship floats upright because the heavy part of her is low down and the wide part of her is in the water. If too much weight is high up, in the masts and the guns and the decks, the ship becomes what sailors call "crank" or "tender": she leans over easily and comes back up slowly. To fix that, builders put ballast in the bottom of the ship. Ballast is heavy stuff, usually stones, loaded into the very bottom of the hull to pull the ship upright. The Vasa had about 120 tonnes of stone in her.

But there was a catch. The more ballast you put in, the deeper the ship sits in the water. The Vasa's lower row of gun ports was only a metre or so above the water. If you put in more stone to make her steady, the gun ports would go under and the water would come in. If you took stone out to keep the ports dry, she would lean over. The ship could not have both: enough ballast to stand up, and gun ports high enough to use the guns. That was the problem, and it was built into her shape. The bottom of the hull was too narrow and too shallow for everything piled on top of it. One sailor said she had "no belly".

## The test: three planks

In the summer of 1628 the Vasa lay at the crane below the royal castle in Stockholm. Her ballast was in. Her guns were not yet aboard. The king was far away in Prussia with his army, and he kept sending letters saying that the ship must be ready. One letter said she must be ready by July 25th 1628, or the king would be very displeased.

The ship's captain, Söfring Hansson, thought the ship was crank, and he said so to the admiral in charge, Admiral Fleming. So a test was arranged. Thirty men were told to run from one side of the upper deck to the other, all together, and then back again. If you make thirty men run across a ship like that, the ship rolls. A steady ship rolls a little and stops. A crank ship rolls more with every run.

The ship's master, Jöran Matsson, who had loaded the ballast, was there. Later he told a court exactly what happened. The first time the men ran across, the ship leaned over by the width of one deck plank. The second time, two planks. The third time, three planks. Then the admiral told the men to stop. If they had run across again, Matsson said, the ship would have gone over.

The admiral had seen it. Everyone there knew what it meant. Matsson told him the ship was too narrow in the bottom. The admiral said back that Matsson had put in too much ballast, because the gun ports were too near the water. Both of them were right, which was the whole trouble. Then the admiral said something that Matsson remembered: he wished the king were at home. He did not write to the king about the test. He did not order the ship changed, which would have taken months. He let her be made ready.

## The day she sailed

Sunday, August 10th 1628 was a calm day. Many of the sailors went to church in the morning. In the afternoon, between three and four o'clock, the Vasa cast off from below the castle. The quay was crowded with people and the water was full of small boats. The sailors had been allowed to bring their wives and children aboard for the first short trip, out to a fortress in the islands where the guests would be put ashore before the ship went on to join the fleet.

The wind was light and came from the wrong direction, so the ship was pulled along the shore on ropes until she reached the current that runs out of the city. Even then, while she was still tied up, an officer noticed that when a group of people walked across the deck together, the ship leaned after them. Then four of her ten sails were set, and the guns fired a salute to say goodbye to the city. The gun ports on both decks were open, with the lions showing.

The wind was so weak that when the captain ordered the sails loosened, the ropes would not run out on their own and the men had to push the sails out by hand. One officer said afterwards that he "had never believed that any ship would have gone over with so little wind". The Vasa drifted along under the cliffs on the south side of the harbour. Then she came out past a gap in the cliffs where the wind could reach her. A gust pushed her over to the left. She came slowly back up. A stronger gust pushed her over again, further, and this time the water poured in through the open gun ports on the lower deck.

Inside the ship, men were ordered to pull the guns back in and shut the ports, but before they had got even one gun in, the ship went over. The water rose so fast that a ladder came loose and one officer was hit by a hatch cover and nearly drowned. The Vasa sank slowly to the bottom, thirty-two metres down, with her sails still set and her flags still flying. She had sailed about a kilometre. Her masts stuck up out of the water, and people clung to them until boats picked them up. Some swam to the shore, 120 metres away.

Nobody knows exactly how many people died. One nobleman wrote in a letter that it was about thirty, "with boatswains, wives and children". A foreign diplomat wrote that it was more than fifty. The museum that looks after the ship today says about thirty of the roughly 150 people on board. Most of them were trapped inside.

## The inquest

That same evening the captain was arrested and taken to the castle. The next day the king's council questioned him. He swore that nobody had been drunk and that every gun had been tied down properly; if one gun had been loose, he said, they could "hew him into a thousand pieces". The ship, he said, was simply crank.

The council wrote to the king on August 12th 1628 to tell him the news. The letter took more than two weeks to reach him in his army camp. He wrote back that the ship had been lost "through the imprudence and carelessness" of the people in charge of her, and that they must raise her from the bottom. He wanted the captain to be in charge of raising her.

On September 5th 1628 a court of seventeen men met in the castle to find out whose fault it was. One by one the surviving officers were asked: did you know the ship was crank? Did you check the ballast? Was it your fault? Each one said it was not his job. Then the master, Jöran Matsson, told the court about the test with the thirty men and the three planks. Nobody argued with him. But the admiral who had stopped the test was away with the fleet, and the court never called him.

Then the builders were questioned. Hein Jacobsson said he had built the ship exactly to the plan his master had given him, on the king's orders, and had even made her a hand's breadth wider. "All people say she was well built," he said. The merchant who had signed the contract said the same, and added that he had told the king how long and how wide the ship would be, "and His Majesty was pleased with it, and would have it so." When the court asked why, if she was so well built, she had tipped over, the two men said they did not know. "God may know it."

That was the moment the inquest died. The ship had been built to the king's measurements, and the king had approved them. Nobody was going to say the king was wrong. The court's prosecutor summed up: the ship was "indeed well built, but not rightly proportioned as she ought to be." A group of experts, sea captains and an ironmaster, wrote down their opinions at the end. They all agreed the bottom of the ship was too small for the top. One of them explained it with a picture: put a pound of lead on one side of a pair of scales and a pound of feathers on the other, pull the lead side down and let go, and the feathers will lift it back up. The ship, he said, had too little weight in the bottom and too much above.

The court took only one measurement. It sent a captain across the harbour to measure how high the gun ports of another ship stood above the water. Nobody was ever found guilty. Nobody was punished. The dead builder got the blame. The captain, the master and the builder all went on to good careers. The admiral was never asked a single question, and died a hero in a sea battle sixteen years later. ## Three hundred and thirty-three years

People tried to raise the Vasa straight away, with ropes and anchors, and could not. An Englishman managed to pull her upright on the bottom, and there she stayed. In the 1660s two men with a diving bell, which is like a big upside-down bucket with air trapped inside it, went down to the wreck in the dark and hauled up more than fifty of her bronze guns. After that the ship was forgotten.

In the 1950s an engineer named Anders Franzén spent his spare time reading old letters in the archives and dragging the harbour bottom with a hook, looking for her. He found, he said, rusty stoves, ladies' bicycles, Christmas trees and dead cats. Then he found the council's letter of 1628, which told him where to look. On August 25th 1956 his sampler came up with a plug of old black oak. A navy diver went down, felt along a wooden wall in the darkness, and found a row of square holes with another row above them: gun ports, on two decks.

Divers spent two years digging tunnels under the hull and threading steel cables through them. On the morning of April 24th 1961, at three minutes past nine, with thousands of people watching, the Vasa came up through the water after 333 years on the bottom. She was almost whole. The archaeologists took the 120 tonnes of ballast stones out of her, and thirty thousand other things, and the bones of fifteen people. Today she stands in her own museum in Stockholm, and nearly all of her wood is the wood that sank in 1628.

Her sister ship, the Äpplet, which the same builder was building in 1628, was found on the sea bed near Stockholm in 2021. He had built her a metre wider. She served the navy for nearly thirty years, and was sunk on purpose in 1659 to block a channel. Now the two ships can be measured side by side, to see what else the builder changed. ## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A test tells you something even if you stop it.** Thirty men ran across the deck three times, and the ship leaned one plank, then two, then three. The admiral stopped the test because he was afraid the ship would tip over. That fear was the result. The test had said, as clearly as any test can, that the ship was not safe. Stopping it did not make the answer go away. It only meant nobody had to write it down.

**Knowing is not the same as doing something.** The captain knew the ship was crank and told the admiral. The master knew, and told the admiral. The admiral saw it with his own eyes. Everybody knew. But nobody was in charge of acting on it. The admiral said he wished the king were at home, as if the problem belonged to someone who was far away. A warning that nobody has to act on is not really a warning.

**Some problems cannot be fixed by being careful.** The master loaded the ballast himself, by lantern light, because he did not trust his men to do it well enough. It made no difference. The ship needed more ballast than her gun ports allowed. That was a problem in her shape, not in how carefully she was loaded. When a design is wrong, everyone who works on it can do their job perfectly and it will still fail.

**A deadline is not a safety check.** The king wrote that the ship must be ready by July 25th. Fixing her properly would have taken months. So the question "is she safe?" was quietly swapped for the question "is she ready?", and she was ready: rigged, ballasted and armed. She sailed with her gun ports open and sank in her first kilometre.

**If the person who ordered the thing cannot be blamed, nobody will be.** The king had approved the ship's measurements. As soon as the court heard that, the inquest was over. The only person it could blame was a builder who had been dead for a year. That is why nobody was punished, and it is also why nothing was learned.

So the lesson is simple. When a test gives you a frightening answer, the frightening answer is the result. Write it down, and decide who has to act on it, before the deadline decides for you.
