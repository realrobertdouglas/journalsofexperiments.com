## The submarine

*Thresher* was a nuclear submarine: a submarine driven by a nuclear reactor, so that it could stay under water for weeks. She was built at the Portsmouth Naval Shipyard, on an island in a river between Maine and New Hampshire, where the United States Navy had been building submarines since 1917. Her keel was laid in May 1958, she was launched on July 9th 1960, and she joined the Navy on August 3rd 1961. She was the first of a new kind. She was quieter than any submarine before her, she carried the biggest sonar ever put in one, and she was built to dive nearly twice as deep as the submarines that came before: about 1,300 feet, where the older boats went to 700.

Going deep is dangerous for a simple reason. At 1,300 feet the sea presses on every square inch of the hull with the weight of about 600 pounds. If a pipe breaks at that depth, the water does not leak in. It comes in like a jet from a fire hose.

A submarine keeps the sea out with its steel hull, but also with hundreds of pipes. Pipes bring sea water in to cool the engines and let it out again. Every joint in those pipes has to be as strong as the hull. In *Thresher* most of those joints were made by a method called silver brazing: a fitting slips over the end of the pipe, and melted silver is run into the gap to glue the two together. If the silver flows all the way round, the joint is as strong as the pipe. If it only flows part of the way round, the joint will still pass a pressure test, and nobody can tell by looking at it. *Thresher* had more than 3,000 brazed joints of two inches across or bigger in the pipes that mattered most.

## The warnings

Brazed joints had been failing in submarines for years. In November 1960 a five-inch joint broke in the submarine *Barbel* at about 650 feet and flooded her engine room; she got up. Other submarines had the same kind of trouble. *Thresher* herself had two small joints fail on her first trials in 1961. In September 1961 the admiral who ran the submarines of the Atlantic Fleet sent a message to Washington saying that these floods were "cause for grave concern" and were happening "with alarming regularity", and asking for a way of testing a brazed joint without taking it apart.

The man who ran the Navy's nuclear reactors, Admiral Hyman Rickover, had already decided. In the part of the ship that was his, the reactor and its machinery, he ordered every sea-water pipe to be welded instead of brazed. A weld melts the two pieces of metal into one, and a weld can be X-rayed to see if it is good. But Rickover's part of the ship was a small part. The rest of *Thresher* belonged to the Bureau of Ships, the Navy's office for designing ships, and to the shipyard.

In November 1962 the captain who had commanded *Thresher* for her first year, Commander Dean Axene, wrote his report on her. He called her the best submarine of her kind afloat. He also wrote: "In my opinion the most dangerous condition that exists in THRESHER is the danger of salt water flooding while at or near test depth."

## Twenty out of 145

In July 1962 *Thresher* went back to the Portsmouth shipyard for an overhaul: a long period in dock in which a ship is taken apart, repaired and put back together. The yard thought it would take six months. It took nine, and the work that was planned at 35,000 days of labour took more than 100,000.

In August 1962 the Bureau of Ships sent the yard a letter. A new instrument had just been developed that could test a brazed joint with sound waves, the way a doctor's ultrasound looks inside a body, and see how much of the joint was really bonded. The letter said that the failures of brazed joints were so serious that the Bureau considered it "a matter of urgency" to inspect them, and it told the yard to keep at least one testing team at work on *Thresher*'s joints for the whole of the overhaul and to test as many as it possibly could. Any joint with less than 40 per cent of its circumference bonded was to be counted as defective and repaired.

The yard put one team on the job. The team was told to test the joints that were easy to reach first, the ones without insulation wrapped round them, and to strip the insulation off the others "if time permitted thereafter."

By November 29th 1962 the team had tested 145 joints. Twenty of them, 13.8 per cent, failed. That is about one in seven. If one in seven of all the 3,000 joints was bad, there were several hundred bad joints in the ship.

The yard's inspectors reported the twenty and asked whether they should strip the insulation and test the rest. On December 4th 1962 the yard decided not to. The yard's commander knew about the decision. A copy went to the captain of the submarine. Nobody told the Bureau of Ships, which had ordered the tests, and nobody told any admiral above the captain. The twenty bad joints were repaired. The rest were left as they were. The court that looked into it afterwards found that the yard "did not exercise good judgment", and the yard's commander admitted that he should have looked harder. The reason he gave was the deadline. The ship was already months late, and stripping the pipes would have made her later and cost more. The Bureau's letter had asked for a report on the tests. The yard's report arrived in Washington after the ship was lost.

## The air that froze

There was a second problem, and nobody had tested for it either. If a submarine starts to sink, the crew can force compressed air into tanks called ballast tanks to push the water out and make the ship lighter. This is called blowing the tanks. *Thresher* kept her air at very high pressure and let it into the tanks through valves that lowered the pressure, and inside those valves were little cone-shaped wire strainers to keep dirt out. The rules for how much air a submarine should carry had not changed since the Second World War, when submarines dived to 400 feet. Nobody had ever tried blowing a deep submarine's tanks all the way, at depth, to see if it worked. One admiral told Congress afterwards: "The fact that we did not blow those tanks in the first ship that came out and make a definitive test at test depth was an error. We should have done it; yes, sir."

## April 10th 1963

*Thresher* left the shipyard on April 9th 1963 under a new captain, Lieutenant Commander John Wesley Harvey, who had taken command in January. He was 35 and had served in the first nuclear submarine, *Nautilus*, when she went under the North Pole. There were 129 men aboard: 12 officers, 96 sailors, and 21 others, mostly engineers from the shipyard who had come along to check their work. With her went a small ship called *Skylark*, a submarine rescue ship built in the Second World War, whose job was to stay above her and talk to her on an underwater telephone. *Skylark*'s rescue chamber could reach 850 feet. In the water where *Thresher* was going to dive, the bottom was 8,400 feet down.

On the morning of April 10th, about 220 miles east of Cape Cod, *Thresher* began her deep dive at 7:47. She went down in steps, stopping to check for leaks, and reporting to *Skylark* on the telephone. At 8:53 she said she was going on to her test depth, the deepest she was built to go. By 9:09 she was there.

Then, at about 9:13, a calm voice came up the telephone: "Experiencing minor difficulties. Have positive up angle. Am attempting to blow. Will keep you informed." The men on *Skylark* heard the sound of air rushing into tanks. It did not last long. The captain of *Skylark* asked, "Are you in control?" four times, and got no answer. At 9:17 a last message came up, mostly lost in crackle. Two officers heard the words "test depth", and thought they heard "exceeding" before it. The sailor writing the log wrote down what he thought he heard: "nine hundred North". Nobody knows exactly what it meant. One reading is that she was 900 feet below her test depth. A minute or so later the navigator, who had heard ships sink in the war, heard "a rather muted, dull thud". The Navy's underwater listening stations recorded it too, at eighteen minutes past nine. It was the hull collapsing, probably at about 2,400 feet, in a tenth of a second.

Nobody on *Skylark* believed it yet. The captain thought the telephone had broken. He did not send a message for more than an hour, and when he did, he left out the words about the minor difficulties and the blow. That evening the Navy announced that *Thresher* was overdue, and the next morning that she was lost with everyone aboard. It was, and still is, the worst submarine accident in history.

## What the court found

The Navy set up a court of inquiry: a panel of senior officers whose job is to find out what happened. It sat for eight weeks, heard 120 witnesses and filled 1,718 pages. It could not say for certain what had gone wrong, because nothing came back from the bottom but oil and scraps. But it worked out the most likely story. A pipe joint in the engine room broke at or near test depth. Water sprayed across the electrical switchboards and the reactor shut itself down, which it was designed to do when something went wrong, so the submarine lost its power. The crew tried to blow the tanks.

The court then did the test that nobody had done before the dive. In *Thresher*'s sister ship *Tinosa*, which was still being built, it had the air blown through the ballast system at full force at the pier. In about thirty seconds the little wire strainers in the valves iced up and burst. Air expanding through a narrow gap gets very cold, and there was moisture in the air. The blow that was supposed to save the ship would have choked itself in half a minute.

The court listed the brazed joints first among the likely places the water came in. It wrote that one in seven failures "was a clear indicator that additional action was required." It also wrote that nobody should be blamed: the problems had grown up over years, as submarines were pushed deeper and faster than the old rules allowed for, and most of them "would not have come to notice had THRESHER not been lost." The one person the court named at first was the captain of *Skylark*, for not reporting what he had heard. His silence, the court said in the same sentence, could not have changed anything. A senator asked whether he was being made a scapegoat. He was not punished, and later he was given a submarine to command.

Congress held its own hearings. Admiral Rickover told them that the real lesson was not brazing against welding. It was that the ship had been built to two standards, a strict one for the reactor and a loose one for everything else, and that in the loose part of the ship the rules had been treated as targets to aim at rather than as rules. He put the joint problem in two sentences: "They would find a percentage wrong in a sample. Then they would only repair those they found wrong in that sample."

The search went on all summer. In June a camera towed across the bottom photographed scraps of metal. In August a deep-diving craft called the *Trieste* photographed a piece of *Thresher*'s sonar dome lying on the mud. The following year the wreck was found, broken into five big pieces.

## After

The Navy changed almost everything. Every submarine was kept to 500 feet until it had been checked. In June 1963 the Bureau of Ships started a programme, which came to be called SUBSAFE, to certify every submarine: every pipe joint between the hull and the sea had to be tested with the ultrasonic instrument or welded and X-rayed; every submarine got an emergency blow system that sent air straight from the tanks to the ballast tanks through as few fittings as possible; the wire strainers were taken out; the standard for a brazed joint was raised; the rescue ship on every deep dive carried a tape recorder. A new submarine's deep dive now had to be made in water no deeper than one and a half times its test depth, so that a sunken boat could be reached. A fuller account is in [*Maximum reasonable assurance*](/deeper/nine-hundred-north/maximum-reasonable-assurance/#young), and the four men's memories of the last six minutes are in [*The telephone log*](/deeper/nine-hundred-north/the-telephone-log/#young).

Since 1963 the United States Navy has lost one submarine, *Scorpion*, in 1968, and she had not yet been through the programme. It has never lost a submarine that was. The Navy's records of the inquiry stayed secret for fifty-seven years. A retired submarine captain, James Bryant, went to court to get them, and from 2020 the Navy released them a few hundred pages at a time. He found, he said, no cover-up. He found a Navy whose ships had got better faster than its habits.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**If one in seven of a sample fails, the problem is not the sample.** The yard tested 145 joints out of 3,000 and found twenty bad ones. It fixed the twenty. But twenty bad joints in 145 means there were probably hundreds more in the pipes nobody had looked at. A test on a sample tells you about the whole. The right question was not "which ones?" but "how many more are there?"

**A warning has to travel upwards.** The inspectors found the bad joints and wrote them down. The report went to the planning office of the shipyard and stopped. The Bureau that had ordered the tests was never told, and the admirals above the captain were never told. A test result that never reaches the people who make the rules might as well not exist.

**Test the way out before you test the way down.** The deep dive was an experiment on the hull and the pipes. The way out, if the experiment failed, was blowing the tanks, and nobody had ever tried it all the way. It took the court thirty seconds at a pier to find out that it froze. Before you try something dangerous, make sure the thing that is supposed to save you actually works, under the conditions where you will need it.

**A rule that you only aim at is not a rule.** The court found that the shipyard was treating the Navy's standards "as goals rather than requirements." Twenty years later the Navy checked its own safety programme and found the same thing starting to happen again. Standards keep turning back into targets unless somebody keeps checking.

**Blame is easy to put in the wrong place.** The only man named at first was the captain of the rescue ship, who had left some words out of a message that could not have saved anyone. The people who left 2,855 joints untested were not named, because there were so many of them that no one person was responsible. Rickover said that responsibility can only belong to a single person, and that unless you can point to the person who is responsible, nobody really is.

So the lesson is simple. When a test on a few things finds some of them bad, assume the rest are the same until you have checked, and make sure the people who wrote the rule hear about it. Before you go to the edge, prove that the way back works.
