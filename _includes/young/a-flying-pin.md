## The rabbits

Rabbits are not native to Australia. A farmer brought two dozen wild ones from England in 1859 and let them go, and within a lifetime they had spread over the whole southern half of the continent. Nobody could count them. The best guess a careful scientist would give in 1952 was somewhere between one and three billion. Ten rabbits eat about as much grass as one sheep, so every ten rabbits meant one sheep fewer, and in dry country they ate the plants down to bare earth and the soil blew away. People trapped them, poisoned them, dug out their burrows and built fences across the whole of Western Australia, and the rabbits kept coming. During the Second World War, when the men who usually fought them were away, there were more rabbits than anyone had ever seen.

## The virus

Myxomatosis is a disease of rabbits. It was first noticed in 1896 in a laboratory in South America, when all the rabbits in it died. In Brazil, where the disease comes from, it lives in a local wild rabbit, gives it nothing worse than a lump on the skin, and is carried from rabbit to rabbit by mosquitoes. In European rabbits, the kind Australia has, it is nearly always fatal. A Brazilian scientist worked all this out in 1942 and published it, but nobody in Australia read his paper until 1951, and that turned out to matter.

A Melbourne children's doctor, Dame Jean Macnamara, saw the disease in an American laboratory in 1933 and came home sure it could rid Australia of rabbits. She spent the next seventeen years telling the government's scientists so, in letters and in the newspapers, and they did not enjoy it. One of them said she thought of him as "a boil on the bum of progress."

The scientists did test the virus. They gave it to every farm animal, every kind of native animal they could catch, and a hare. Only rabbits got sick. Then, from 1937, they tried it on wild rabbits: first inside a fence on Wardang Island off South Australia, and then in dry country inland. The rabbits that were injected died. The disease hardly spread to the others. In 1943 the work stopped.

## The six places by the river

After the war a new group at the Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation (CSIRO for short: the government's science body) was told to try again. Its leader was Francis Ratcliffe, and the man who did most of the work on the ground was Ken Myers, at a field station in Albury on the River Murray. This time they chose places near water, because they thought mosquitoes might help the disease along. Through 1950 they put the virus into wild rabbits at six places along the Murray: a dairy farm at Gunbower, the hills near Albury, two pine woods at Balldale, a granite hill at Coreen, and an old gold mine at Rutherglen.

The method was patient. They caught rabbits with ferrets, injected each one under the skin with a little of the virus, marked its ear, and let it go. Then they counted. On warm sunny evenings, when rabbits come out of their burrows, a man walked the same path at the same slow pace and counted what he saw. He noted which animals a fox or a cat or an eagle had killed, and how many mosquitoes bit his bare arm in a minute.

The last and most careful trial was at Rutherglen. The gold mine was a cone of mine waste sixty feet high, fenced on thirteen acres, with about nine hundred rabbits living in it. On October 27th 1950 Myers injected sixty-six of them. From the seventh day, when they began to look sick, he drove all the rabbits underground by day so the sick ones would lie against the healthy ones. Then he watched the disease pass through the population in waves. The first wave caught about thirty-five rabbits. The next caught sixteen, then twelve, then eight. Each wave was smaller than the one before. Each sick rabbit, on average, was passing the disease to only about six-tenths of another rabbit, and a disease that does that dies out. By December 15th there were a thousand rabbits on the hill, three hundred more than in August. The disease had killed about seventy of them.

The team went back to Canberra at the end of November, as the head of a famous Melbourne laboratory put it, "prepared to write up the account of a fruitless experiment."

## December

In the first week of December rabbits began dying again at Balldale, where the disease had been given up as dead. On December 10th word reached Canberra that they were dying by the hundred on the river flats at Corowa, seven or eight miles away. Then the reports came from everywhere. One December outbreak was on the Darling River, nearly four hundred miles from Corowa. By the middle of February 1951 the disease was along the Murray, the Murrumbidgee, the Lachlan and the Darling, and into Queensland. From the mouth of the Murray to the farthest point it reached that summer was 860 miles. In some places along the rivers nine rabbits in ten died within a month, and a scientist could drive for a day through country that had been swarming with rabbits and see hardly one.

Myers went back to the river to find out what had changed, and what had changed was the air. The spring of 1950 had brought the biggest floods in memory. When the water went down it left lagoons full of weed along the rivers, and in them bred a mosquito called *Culex annulirostris*, which bites at dusk and likes rabbits. Of about 1,400 baby mosquitoes Myers dipped from the lagoons, nearly nine in ten were this one kind. The disease stopped where the mosquitoes stopped: walk away from the river onto drier ground and both faded out together. Sick rabbits, too slow to flick them off, were bitten three times as often as healthy ones. A colleague caught 168 of the mosquitoes, ground them up, and injected the mush into a laboratory rabbit, which caught myxomatosis. When the evenings turned cold in March the mosquitoes stopped flying, and by April the disease stopped with them.

The winter trials had failed because the right mosquitoes were not flying in winter. The disease had never needed rabbits to touch each other. It needed something to carry it from skin to skin.

## The other disease

In the same valley, in the same weeks, people were falling ill with a brain disease that nobody had seen since 1918. Forty-five people caught it between December 1950 and April 1951, most of them children, and nineteen of them died. The government had just put a new virus into the rabbits along those rivers. Nobody could name the disease killing the children. It was not hard to see why people joined the two together.

The scientists knew they were different. Macfarlane Burnet, who ran the Walter and Eliza Hall Institute in Melbourne, had two of his staff on the human disease, and one of them, Eric French, grew its virus from the brains of the dead. It was a mosquito-borne brain fever of a known family, and they named it Murray Valley encephalitis. Side by side in the laboratory, the two viruses were not even distant relations. But a laboratory test is not something a frightened town can read. The hospital board at Mildura, where a dozen people had fallen ill, challenged Burnet and the government minister in charge of CSIRO, Richard Casey, to inject themselves with the rabbit virus if it was so harmless.

Burnet talked it over with Frank Fenner, a young professor of microbiology who had decided only weeks before, on February 1st 1951, to make myxomatosis his life's work. They agreed it was perfectly safe. Fenner made up a dose, enough to infect a hundred rabbits, and injected Burnet. Burnet injected Fenner. When Ian Clunies Ross, the chairman of CSIRO, heard what they had done, he said that he was the one responsible for the virus and he should be included, so Fenner injected him too. This was in March 1951. None of them got anything worse than a small red spot where the needle went in.

They did not want it talked about. On March 8th 1951 Casey told Parliament only that "two research workers voluntarily inoculated themselves with the virus, and there was no reaction," and that the experts agreed there was "no connexion whatsoever" between the two diseases. Not until October, when a member of Parliament asked for the names of the "three senior officers" and called it "a very gallant experiment," did the three agree to be named. On October 11th 1951 Casey read their names to the House, and the members stood up and cheered.

Asked about it that evening, the three were plain. Burnet said: "It wasn't an experiment, because we all knew the answer in advance." They had done it, he said, "to squash this fallacy," and "the best way was to have a really big injection ourselves." Clunies Ross said it was "a completely unexciting story." Fenner said: "It was nothing at all. We felt we had to do it for the sake of unbelievers." The next morning a Melbourne newspaper said the news "goes far to restore our faith in human-kind."

## What the mosquito was

Within a year Fenner and an insect scientist named Max Day had worked out how the mosquito carried the disease. The virus did not grow inside the mosquito at all. A mosquito that fed on a sick rabbit through clean skin could not pass the disease on. One that poked its mouthparts through one of the lumps the disease makes on the skin came away with virus on them, and the next rabbit it bit caught the disease through its skin. Fenner found he could do the same thing with an ordinary pin: push it through a lump, then prick a healthy rabbit. The mosquito, Burnet said, was "merely a flying pin." Any biting insect would do, and in the field they found the disease being carried by a black fly, by sandflies, and in winter by the rabbit louse. That was why it had failed on a dry island and in a dry winter, and why it ran along the rivers in a wet summer.

The flying pin explained one more thing. A rabbit infected with the original virus died in a few days. A rabbit infected with a slightly milder strain stayed alive, and covered in lumps, for weeks, and was bitten through them all that time. So the milder strains got carried further, and the virus in the field grew milder on its own. At one place where the first outbreak killed about 998 rabbits in every thousand, the second, a year later, killed about 900. And the rabbits that lived through the milder disease bred rabbits that were harder to kill. Burnet had predicted all of this in 1952, before it happened, and Fenner spent the next fifteen years measuring it. Even so, by the time he and Burnet gave their estimates, somewhere between a quarter and a half of all the rabbits in Australia were dead, perhaps three or four hundred million of them.

And the human disease? The institute's doctors went through the rainfall records and found that every year of that brain fever since 1917 had followed a November of flooding on the rivers that feed the Darling. The floods bred the mosquito, and the same mosquito, *Culex annulirostris*, carried the human virus from water birds to people. The next summer was a normal one, and the institute could find no trace of the virus anywhere in the valley. The people who tied the two diseases together had been wrong about the rabbit virus and right about something bigger: the two outbreaks had one cause, a flood, and one carrier, a mosquito.

Australia had tried biological control once before, when toads were shipped from Hawaii to Queensland in 1935 to eat a beetle, and let go before anyone had checked that they would. That story is [*Twelve Beetles a Night*](/experiments/2026/10/20/twelve-beetles-a-night.html#young).

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**You can test the wrong thing very carefully.** The scientists measured how many rabbits there were, what the foxes and cats were eating, and whether sick rabbits wandered. They measured all of it well. But they ran the trials in winter, when the mosquito that mattered was not flying, because they still half believed the disease passed by touch. Their careful answer, that the disease died out, was the right answer to the wrong question.

**Keep watching after you think it has failed.** The team went home in November to write up a failure. The real result arrived in December, by telephone, when no scientist was watching. If the rain had come a month later, or the landholder at Balldale had not noticed, the virus might have been given up for good.

**Know the difference between finding out and showing.** When the three scientists injected themselves, they said straight out that it was not an experiment, because they already knew the answer from years of tests on animals and from the two viruses side by side in the laboratory. Their arms proved nothing new. They were a way of showing people a proof that was already there. That is fine, as long as the real proof comes first and everyone says which is which.

**When people connect two things, look for the real link.** The rumour that the rabbit virus was killing children was wrong. But the people who believed it had noticed something true: two diseases, in one valley, in one summer. The real link was a flood and a mosquito. Saying "no connection" was correct and not enough.

**A weapon that kills ninety-nine in a hundred will change.** The virus got milder and the rabbits got tougher, and the reason was the same pin that made the disease work in the first place. Nothing that kills so well can keep doing it forever.

So the lesson is simple. Before you trust what an experiment tells you, ask what it was actually able to test, and keep watching after the day you have decided what it means.
