## A door that opened outward

The DC-10 was a big airliner built by the McDonnell Douglas company at Long Beach, in California, in the early 1970s. It carried about three hundred people, with three engines: one under each wing and one in the tail.

Under the floor of the passenger cabin were three holds for baggage and cargo. The one at the back, on the left side, had a door in the bottom curve of the fuselage. A passenger door on an airliner opens inward first and is bigger than its hole, so that the air pressure inside the cabin pushes it shut harder and harder as the aircraft climbs. A cargo door low down cannot do that, because the hold behind it is full. It has to open outward. That means that when the aircraft is high up and the cabin is pumped full of air, the whole pressure of the cabin, about five pounds on every square inch of the door, is trying to push it open.

The designers answered this with four things, one after another. Four hooks on the bottom edge of the door were driven by an electric motor over four pegs on the door sill, and went just past the middle of their swing, so that the pressure pushed them against stops instead of pushing them open. Then a sliding tube inside the door put a steel pin behind each hook, so the hooks could not move at all. The tube was moved by a handle on the outside of the door, and the same handle closed a little flap in the big door called the vent door. If the pins were not in, the handle was supposed not to come down and the flap was supposed to stay open, so that the aircraft could not be pumped up with air at all. And a switch at the end of the tube turned off a warning light in the cockpit.

So there were four signs that the door was locked, and the whole idea was that you could not have the signs without the lock. One more thing mattered. The cabin floor above this hold was not built to take a sudden blast of air from below, and the cables that moved the tail of the aircraft, the elevators and the rudder and the controls of the tail engine, all ran through the beams of that floor. The floor was built on the belief that the door would not open.

## Windsor, June 12th 1972

On the evening of June 12th 1972 an American Airlines DC-10 was being loaded at Detroit for a flight to New York. The last door to be shut was the back cargo door. The man on the ramp closed it electrically, listened for the motor to stop, and tried to pull the handle down. It would not come. So he pushed it down with his knee. The handle went down, the little vent flap closed, though it looked slightly crooked, and he told a mechanic, who said the aircraft could go. The warning light in the cockpit never came on.

The flight took off at 7:20 in the evening with 56 passengers and a crew of 11. Five minutes later, climbing through about 11,750 feet over Windsor, in Canada, just across the river from Detroit, the crew heard a thud and dust flew into their faces. The captain, Bryce McCormick, thought they had hit another aircraft. What had happened was that the hooks had never gone past the middle of their swing, so the air pressure had been pushing back through them on to two small bolts that were never meant to carry it. The bolts broke, the hooks sprang off their pegs, and the door tore away. The air rushed out of the hold, and the floor of the lounge at the back of the cabin, with nobody sitting in it, broke and fell into the hold.

The cables ran through that floor. The rudder jammed hard over. The elevator would only move if both pilots heaved on it. The tail engine ran down and could not be controlled. "We have a control problem, we have no rudder," the captain told the ground. "I got a hole in the cabin, I think we've lost number two engine."

Then the first officer, Peter Whitney, said the thing that saved everyone. They still had the two wing engines, and if they pushed one harder than the other the aircraft would turn. They flew it back to Detroit that way, asking for a long, gentle approach, and landed at 7:44 with the nose so flat that the captain doubted the wheels were down. The aircraft swerved off to the right edge of the runway, and the pilots steered it back with the engines. Everyone went down the slides. Nine passengers and two stewardesses had small injuries, mostly burns from the slides.

The American accident investigators, the National Transportation Safety Board, took the door to the factory and tried to pull the handle down with the pins out. It could be done, with a push of about 120 pounds. Pushing that hard bent the sliding tube inside the door, and the bent tube closed the vent flap, let the handle stow, and touched the switch that put out the cockpit light. Four signs had turned out to be one sign, and a knee could beat it.

## The fix that was only advice

Eleven days after Windsor the Safety Board sent two recommendations to the Federal Aviation Administration, the FAA, which is the government body that makes the rules for aircraft in America. The first: make it "physically impossible" to put the handle down unless the pins were in. The second: put vents between the cabin and the hold, so that the floor would not fall in if the door went.

The FAA has a tool for this, called an airworthiness directive. It is a law. Every American airline has to obey it or the aircraft cannot fly, government inspectors check that it has been done, and a copy goes to every foreign government whose airlines fly that type. The FAA's office in Los Angeles wrote one four days after Windsor. It was never sent. The head of McDonnell Douglas's aircraft division appealed to the head of the FAA, and the two men agreed that the company would send out its own notices instead, called service bulletins. A service bulletin is advice from the manufacturer. Nobody has to follow it, nobody checks, and it does not have to go to foreign governments. People later called this the gentlemen's agreement.

The bulletins came out in June and July 1972. One put a small window in every cargo door, beside the handle, so that a man on the ramp could look in and see whether the pin was behind the hook. Another ordered heavier wiring, so the motor would drive the hooks all the way. A third ordered a plate to be fitted behind the handle's shaft so that it could not bend when the handle was forced. That third bulletin was the one that answered Windsor.

An engineer at the company that built the fuselage for McDonnell Douglas, Dan Applegate, wrote to his boss on June 27th 1972 that it seemed to him inevitable that in the next twenty years DC-10 cargo doors would come open, and that he would expect this usually to mean the loss of the aircraft. His company did not pass the letter on.

The floor was argued about by letter. In February 1973 the FAA wrote to the company that losing an aircraft because its floor collapsed "would be unacceptable." The company wrote back that making the floor strong enough would add a lot of weight, and that in its opinion this was "unwarranted," because the aircraft's structure had an excellent safety record. A year later the FAA asked again. On February 25th 1974 the company replied that it could not take on the study alone, suggested the FAA might pay for it, and said it would do nothing more until it heard back. Six days later the second door opened.

## Ship 29

Every DC-10 had a number from the factory line. Number 29 first flew at Long Beach in February 1972, four months before Windsor, and was sold to Turkish Airlines in December 1972. By then the bulletins were out, and the company's records said that all of them had been done on this aircraft. But the bulletin about the plate did not list it, and the plate had never been fitted. Someone had started the job, filing the end of the tube, and stopped.

Other things had gone wrong with the door in its first year. The pins had been adjusted so that they stopped short of where they should be. The switch for the warning light had been packed with washers so that it put the light out while the pins were still three millimetres from the hooks. The mechanics had often had to close the door by hand with a tool. The French investigators later set up a brand-new door exactly as this one had been rigged, with the plate removed, and pulled the handle down with the pins out. It took about fifty pounds. With the pins set properly, they found, it could not be done at all, even without the plate.

## Paris, March 3rd 1974

On Sunday March 3rd 1974 that aircraft, now called TC-JAV, flew from Istanbul to Paris on its way to London. At Orly airport the back hold was emptied of baggage and mail. Nothing new went in. At about 10:35 in the morning, by Greenwich time, a cargo handler named Mahmoudi shut the door. He said afterwards that he did it as usual and noticed nothing wrong. He also said that he had never looked through the little window and did not know what it was for. The airline's engineer who should have checked was away on a course, and a mechanic sent in his place was not seen to look either. The window was too high up to reach from the ground.

The stop was supposed to last an hour and lasted an hour and a half, because 216 extra passengers were squeezed on at the last minute. There was a strike at British Airways, and people who had been trying to get home to London took any flight they could find. When the aircraft took off at 11:30 it had 334 passengers and 12 crew aboard, from more than twenty countries; about 177 were British and 48 Japanese.

It climbed north-east over the fields. At 11:40, at about 11,500 feet, with the cabin pumped up to about the same pressure as at Windsor, the two bolts broke and the door went. This time the cabin was full. The floor above the door did not just sag; a piece of it went out through the hole with two rows of three seats and the six people in them, who fell into ploughed fields near a village called Saint-Pathus. Every cable to the tail was cut or jammed. The co-pilot said, "the fuselage has burst." The aircraft's nose dropped and it sped up to about 430 knots, which is nearly 800 kilometres an hour. The pilots could not pull it up. Seventy-seven seconds after the door opened it flew into the pine trees of the forest of Ermenonville, about 37 kilometres north-east of Paris, and broke into pieces. All 346 people on board were killed. It was the worst air crash there had ever been.

## What happened next

Four days later the FAA sent every American airline with DC-10s a telegram: an airworthiness directive, at last, ordering the three bulletins of 1972 to be done before the aircraft flew again, and ordering a crew member to look through the window at the pins before every flight. A second order, two weeks later, required a new system that would not let the aircraft be pumped up at all unless the door was locked, which was what the Safety Board had asked for in July 1972. When the FAA then checked all 134 DC-10s in the world, two had not had the bulletins done. One was the Turkish aircraft.

In Washington, committees of Congress held hearings in March and April 1974 and asked why the directive of 1972 had been withdrawn. The new head of the FAA wrote that "upon appeal to the administrator by the manufacturer, a decision was made to forego the issuance" of directives. The head of the Los Angeles office explained that a directive "has the force of law" and brings the inspectors out, while a bulletin does not. A congressman summed it up later in the year: the FAA had been "put on notice almost 2 years previously by a near miss at Detroit."

In France a commission of inquiry worked for twenty months. Its report said the accident had been caused by the door coming open, the floor collapsing and the controls being cut, and that the door had come open because the hooks were not fully closed, which the design made it possible not to notice. It found three things had combined: the plate that was never fitted, the pins and the switch that were wrongly set, and the fact that nobody at Orly looked through the window. Its last sentence before the signatures said that all of these dangers "had already become evident, nineteen months earlier, at the time of the Windsor accident, but no efficacious corrective action had followed." And it told the authorities to use directives, "whatever the financial repercussions," whenever safety was seriously at risk.

In 1975 the FAA ordered the floors of every wide-bodied airliner, not only the DC-10, strengthened and vented so that a sudden hole in the hull would not bring the floor down. Later the rules for designing new airliners were changed so that every outward-opening door must have a window to see its locks, a warning light that cannot be fooled, and a way of stopping the aircraft from pressurising if the door is not locked.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A warning that is only advice is not a warning.** After Windsor everyone knew what was wrong with the door and how to fix it. The fix was sent out as advice from the company instead of an order from the government. Nobody had to do it, and nobody was sent to check. For nineteen months the people in charge believed the doors were fixed because the paperwork said so.

**A record of a fix is not a fix.** The factory's papers for aircraft number 29 said the plate had been fitted. It had not. The papers said the pins were set right. They had been changed. Nobody looked at the door itself until it was lying in a field.

**If a safety device can be beaten by an ordinary person, it has measured the person, not the danger.** The vent flap was meant to stay open if the door was not locked. At Detroit a man's knee closed it. At Paris, with the plate missing and the pins set short, a push of fifty pounds closed it. People on the ramp learned that the door was stiff and that you had to push hard, so they pushed hard.

**When the first test fails, test the second thing too.** The floor was not vented because the door would not open. The door opened, on the ground and then over Windsor. The floor should have been looked at straight away, and instead it was argued about in letters for a year, while the aircraft flew.

**Check the story before you learn from it.** Some reports say 345 people died and some say 346. Some say the door opened at 11,000 feet, some at 12,000, one at 13,000. The first directive is dated March 6th in one official record and March 7th in another. The full entry says which is which.

So the lesson is simple. When something has shown you how it fails, make the fix an order, send someone to look at it, and do not believe a piece of paper that says it has been done until you have seen the thing itself.
