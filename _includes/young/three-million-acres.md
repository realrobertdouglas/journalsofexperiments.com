## Three men trying to make rain

In the last week of January 1951, at a place called Kongwa in the middle of Tanganyika, in East Africa, three men were trying to make it rain. Their names were Robbie Robson, Paddy Kenny and Robert Stewart. They worked for a British government company called the Overseas Food Corporation. They had built a machine for making hydrogen out of the back of a lorry, and a charcoal burner that looked like a drainpipe with an aircraft rudder stuck on it. They burned about £5 worth of a chemical called silver iodide and let the smoke rise into the clouds that drifted over every afternoon and rained on the hills fifty miles away. They said they had tried it more than thirty times and it had rained every time.

Three weeks earlier, on January 9th 1951, the government in London had published a report. It said that the company could never pay back the money it had been lent to grow peanuts at Kongwa, which came to about £36 million, and that the whole sum would be written off. Peanuts are called groundnuts in Britain, and this was the Groundnut Scheme. It had been meant to clear more than three million acres of African bush, an area about the size of Connecticut, and grow enough groundnuts to make margarine for the whole of Britain. Now it was being shrunk to a few small farms and renamed an experiment. Of the 1,400 Europeans who had worked at Kongwa, thirteen were to stay.

The rain the three men were trying to make with £5 of chemicals was the thing the plan had taken for granted five years before, without anyone looking at the record. This is the story of that plan.

## The idea

After the Second World War Britain was short of fat. Margarine, cooking fat and soap are all made from oils, and Britain had to buy almost all of its oils from abroad. In 1946 the fat ration was seven ounces a week for each person.

Early in 1946 a businessman named Frank Samuel flew across Tanganyika. His company, the United Africa Company, belonged to Unilever, which made most of the margarine in western Europe. He looked down at hundreds of miles of empty thorn bush and wondered whether it could grow groundnuts. He went to London and put the idea to the Minister of Food, John Strachey. The government liked it. Britain needed the fat, and the government also wanted to show that a public company could do big things in Africa that private companies had never tried.

## The three men and the nine weeks

The government sent three men to look at the land: A. J. Wakefield, who had been the head of farming for the Tanganyika government and knew the country well; D. L. Martin, who ran plantations for the United Africa Company; and John Rosa, a civil servant. They arrived in June 1946 and stayed nine weeks. For six of those weeks they looked at the land from an aeroplane. Their report was on the minister's desk on September 20th 1946.

The report was a plan made of numbers. There would be 107 farms of 30,000 acres each, 3,210,000 acres altogether, in three parts of Tanganyika: at Kongwa, on the railway; at Urambo, further along the railway; and in the far south near Nachingwea, where there was no railway and no port, so both would have to be built. The bush would be knocked flat with tractors, the roots pulled out by machine, and the groundnuts planted and dug up by machine. In the first year 150,000 acres would be planted. By the fifth year the farms would grow 600,000 tons of groundnuts a year. The whole thing would cost £24 million. Every acre would yield 750 pounds of nuts. Clearing an acre of bush would cost under £4.

Two things had to be true for the plan to work. First, that tractors could clear thorn bush at that price and that speed. Second, that the land would actually grow groundnuts: that it had the right soil and enough rain. The plan was honest about the first risk. It said that "serious difficulties and delays, many of them unforseeable, may arise." But it took the land for granted.

Nobody ran a small trial first. The country was hungry, and a trial of a few thousand acres would take years. One member of Parliament later put the argument this way: "one can learn nothing very valuable about farming 100,000 acres from digging up a single cabbage patch." So the experiment and the real thing were the same thing, from the first day.

## The two instruments nobody read

There were two simple ways to check the land before the tractors arrived. One was a rain gauge, which is a tube that measures how much rain falls. The plan said that farming in East Africa "is largely dependent upon an erratic rainfall," which everyone who had been there already knew. For Kongwa the plan used a record of about 25 inches of rain a year. But the record was only seven years long, and it came from a weather station some way off that got more rain than Kongwa did. The Gogo people who lived at Kongwa had their own name for it. They called it "the country of perpetual drought."

The other instrument was a spade. One peer told the House of Lords that the three men "spent perhaps three to four weeks in the Kongwa area, where the bush is so impenetrable that no samples of soil could be taken." Nobody dug up the ground to see what it was made of. Wakefield's own case for Kongwa rested on the groundnuts growing in one fortieth of an acre of an African farmer's field.

In November 1947 Parliament voted for the scheme without a single vote against. The opposition leaders said it was something all parties could unite on.

## Operation Groundnuts

Strachey called it "Operation Groundnuts." He had been in the Royal Air Force in the war, and he thought of the scheme as an army landing, like the ones he had seen in North Africa in 1942. "The enemy," he told Parliament, "is the tsetse fly, the climate, the stubborn African bush, the ignorance of the cultivator, and the lack of communications." He believed in it, he said, "with my whole heart."

The first camp was pitched at Kongwa on January 20th 1947, four months after the report arrived. Kongwa was chosen first because it was on the railway. It was also the driest of the three places.

There were no new heavy tractors to be had anywhere in the world in 1947, so the company bought old ones that the American army had left on beaches in the Pacific after the war, and shipped them to Africa. Most of them broke down. When the Overseas Food Corporation took over the scheme in March 1948 it had 289 tractors, and 91 of them worked. For the southern area, the Vickers factory in Newcastle took Sherman tanks apart, removed the armour and the guns, and rebuilt them as tractors called Shervicks.

Then the land spoke. The thorn bush at Kongwa was fifteen feet high, and underneath it was, the chief engineer wrote, "a solid mass of interlaced rubbery roots." The soil was full of grit. One farmer told Parliament that a steel plough disc 32 inches across wore down by four inches after ploughing just 200 acres. When the rains stopped, the soil set hard like cement, and no machine could work it at all. So a scheme designed to free farming from the rains found it could only plough when the rains came, which is exactly what African farmers had always done.

A town grew up at Kongwa, with nearly 2,000 houses, a hospital with 400 beds, schools and its own police force. More than 100,000 people in Britain applied to work on the scheme. By early 1949 there were nearly 900 Europeans on the staff, where the plan had allowed for 160. Thousands of African workers came, more than the plan had expected. In the south the company built 120 miles of pipeline, a port and ninety miles of railway. By the end of 1949 it had spent £29 million.

## Twelve inches

The first harvest, in the spring of 1948, was supposed to come from 150,000 acres and weigh 56,000 tons. It came from about 7,500 acres, and each acre gave about 500 pounds of nuts instead of 750. One member of Parliament said the harvest was "probably just enough to supply the seed for the following year."

What everyone counted instead was acres. The plan for 1949 had been to clear 82,000 acres. The company decided that was too much and cut it to 50,000. Then it cleared 49,000 and reported that it had hit its target "almost exactly." A farmer who visited Kongwa that winter found the men receiving telegrams from London that said "Send urgentest latest acreage cleared." "You cannot beat Africa with arithmetic," he wrote.

On March 14th 1949 Strachey told Parliament how much rain had fallen at Kongwa since the rains began: 12.53 inches. He said that Kongwa's rain being the least of the three places "was known." Then, in the third week of March, the rain stopped. The groundnuts ripened early and small. By the summer each acre at Kongwa was giving 245 pounds. The whole harvest was just over 2,000 tons. The plan had said it would be a quarter of a million. Strachey told Parliament that the whole season's rain at Kongwa had been under 12 inches, against the average of 25 that the plan had used, and that as far as anyone knew it had never been so low before.

A member of Parliament who had looked into the rain record said what it was worth. He said the job of reading the gauge "was not taken very seriously, and that sometimes the record was taken on Christmas Day and that had to suffice for a long period." Another member said later that the rings of the rain gauges at Kongwa kept disappearing, so that nobody was sure what had fallen in a given year.

In November 1949 the targets came down again. Kongwa would stay at 100,000 acres. By 1954 the scheme would have 600,000 acres, not 3,210,000, and would have cost £50 million, not £24 million. Strachey admitted that on 600,000 acres it could never make a profit. The same month he sacked Wakefield and Rosa from the company's board. Wakefield had been saying in public that the scheme was going too fast and spending too much. Strachey replied that Wakefield's own plan had been far faster and far more unrealistic than anything the company had done, which was true.

## The bubble bursts

In the election of February 1950 the government was returned with a tiny majority, and Strachey moved to another job. The new Minister of Food, Maurice Webb, said in July that the early mistakes had come from "proceeding for probably quite laudable motives at a breakneck speed." The third harvest at Kongwa gave 214 pounds an acre, and some fields were not worth harvesting at all. A team of experts went to Kongwa to decide whether it could grow anything. In September 1950 they said that growing groundnuts there by machine on a large scale should be given up.

In November the company told the government that "the original aims of the scheme have proved incapable of fulfilment." It had learned to clear bush by machine, it said, "but it cannot be done at an economic cost." A reporter cabled home that "the great £36,000,000 groundnut bubble has burst." Six hundred tractors and bulldozers were already on ships going home. Seven hundred workers were waiting for passages. At Kongwa, where 1,400 Europeans had worked, thirteen would be left, and people in the bars called them the bailiffs.

The government's report came out on January 9th 1951. Webb told Parliament on February 20th that "the original plans for producing large quantities of oils and fats in Africa have proved to be beyond attainment." About £36 million had been lent to the company and would never come back, so it would be written off. The company would keep about 64,000 acres under crop as "a scheme of large-scale experimental development," which is to say the small trial that nobody had run in 1946. The Colonial Secretary, closing the debate, began with four words: "The Groundnut Scheme has failed." Parliament voted to carry on with the small version by 302 votes to 295.

In the end the scheme had cleared about 175,000 acres, a bit more than five in every hundred that the plan had promised, and less than half of those were fit to farm. One newspaper worked out that it had grown 2,368 tons of groundnuts against a plan of 227,676. It had bought more groundnuts as seed than it ever harvested.

## What was left

The small experiment ran until 1955 and then passed to a new Tanganyika company. At Urambo the land grew tobacco instead. In the south the big fields were split into small farms for African tenants, and years later the groundnuts there gave 1,000 pounds an acre, more than the plan had hoped, on a tiny fraction of the plan's land. At Kongwa the government admitted in 1954 that "the very uncertain rainfall" had destroyed any hope of growing crops there on a large scale, and the cleared land was turned into a cattle ranch.

The railway in the south closed in 1963. When a survey team visited in 1992 they found the farms at Nachingwea abandoned, Kongwa still a cattle ranch, and the land at Urambo farmed by villagers.

Scientists went on trying to make rain at Kongwa. In 1952 the East African weather service ran proper trials there for four months, sending the chemicals up on balloons and measuring the rain with a grid of gauges across the farms. Their result, reported in the journal Nature, was that on the days they seeded the clouds, less rain fell on the farms, not more. The one instrument the scheme finally read carefully was the one that said no.

The word "groundnuts" lived on in Britain as a name for any big government project that wastes money. In Tanganyika the small tenant farms were copied by the independent government in the 1960s. What the scheme did to the people of the Southern Province while it lasted is told in the companion piece, "[Days of great prosperity](/deeper/three-million-acres/days-of-great-prosperity/#young)".

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Check the thing you are counting on before you start.** The whole plan depended on rain and soil. A rain gauge and a spade could have answered both questions in 1946. Instead the three men flew over the land, and the scheme found out about the rain in 1949 and about the soil when the ploughs wore out. The land had its own name for Kongwa, and nobody asked.

**A target is not a measurement.** For four years the scheme reported its progress as acres cleared. Every time the target was missed, a new, smaller target was set, and then hitting the smaller one was announced as success. Clearing 49,000 acres when the plan had said 150,000 was reported as hitting the target "almost exactly." The number everyone read was the number the plan itself had made up. The numbers that mattered, inches of rain and pounds of nuts per acre, were the ones nobody wanted to look at.

**A small trial is cheaper than a big mistake.** The government said there was no time for a pilot scheme. In the end it ran one anyway, from 1951 to 1955, on the same land, and it gave its answer in about four years. The difference was that by then £36 million had already been spent.

**Being half right is not enough.** The plan had two parts. The engineers did learn how to clear tropical bush with machines, which nobody had done before. But the land at Kongwa would not grow groundnuts whatever the machines did. An experiment with two questions in it has to answer both.

So the lesson is simple. Before you build something enormous, find out what the ground is made of and how much it rains. Then measure the thing you actually want, and not just the thing that is easiest to count.
