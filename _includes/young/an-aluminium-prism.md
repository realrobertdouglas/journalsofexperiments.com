## A new kind of light

In 1895 a German physicist, Wilhelm Röntgen, found X-rays: invisible rays that pass through flesh and wood and show the bones inside a hand. Within a few years other invisible things had been found too, the rays from uranium and from radium, and physicists everywhere were looking for more.

René Blondlot was a professor of physics at Nancy, a city in the east of France. He was in his fifties, and he was good. He had measured how fast electric waves travel along wires and found that they go at the speed of light, which was one of the proofs that light and electricity are the same kind of thing. People trusted his hands.

In February 1903 he was trying to find out whether X-rays were polarised, which means whether they vibrate in one direction rather than every direction. His instrument was a tiny electric spark. He believed that when invisible rays fell on the spark it glowed a little brighter, and that if he turned the spark one way or another he could tell which way the rays were vibrating.

Then something odd happened. The rays bent when they went through a prism, the way light does. X-rays do not do that. So on March 23rd 1903 Blondlot told the Academy of Sciences in Paris that he had found something new: "a new species of light". By May he had found the same rays coming from a gas lamp, by June from the sun. He called them N rays, after Nancy.

## How you saw them

Here is the strange part, and it is the whole story. N rays could not be seen, photographed or felt. They did not make a photographic plate go dark. They did not warm anything. The only way to know they were there was to look at something faint in a dark room, a tiny spark or a spot of glowing paint, and notice that it got a little brighter when the rays fell on it, and a little dimmer when you put your hand in the way.

Blondlot was honest about how hard this was. Some people, he wrote, saw the change at once; for others it was "almost at the limit of what they are able to discern", and they needed practice. Later he wrote a page of instructions. You must not strain your eyes. You must "see the source without looking at it", glancing a little to one side. You must be silent. There must be no tobacco smoke. "The observer must play an absolutely passive part, under penalty of seeing nothing." And at the end: "Some people, in fact, never succeed."

Notice what that means. If you saw the rays, you had the knack. If you did not, you were one of the people who never succeed. There was no way for the experiment to come out wrong.

## Everybody finds them

Once you know how to look, you find what you are looking for. In November 1903 Blondlot found that a quartz lens kept giving off N rays for twenty minutes after the lamp was switched off. Pebbles picked up in the yard gave them off for four days. A brick that had been in the sun, held near your eyes, made a dim strip of paper look brighter. A bent walking stick held in front of your face made a clock across the room easier to read. A hardened steel file gave off N rays, and so did a knife dug out of a grave twelve hundred years old, "just like a modern knife".

Then a doctor at Nancy, Augustin Charpentier, found that the human body gave them off, more from a muscle when it was tensed, more from a nerve when it was working. He said you could follow a nerve under the skin by holding a glowing spot near it. Another scientist found that the rays from a brain faded when the person was put to sleep with ether, and that metals could be put to sleep too. Others found N rays in plants, in a tuning fork, in a dead body. In the first half of 1904 the Academy's weekly journal printed dozens of papers about them. Almost all of them came from France, and most of them from Nancy.

Outside France nobody could see them. Physicists in Germany, England and America built the apparatus exactly as Blondlot described it and saw nothing. One of them, Otto Lummer in Berlin, explained why in a talk in November 1903. The edge of your eye sees faint light better than the middle does. If you glance at a dim spot it looks brighter; if you stare straight at it, it fades and goes reddish. So an observer who looks at a glowing spot, then puts a hand in the beam and stares harder to see the change, will see it fade, and will see it come back when the hand is removed and the eyes relax. No rays are needed. A professor in Glasgow tried it with seven observers, who called out "bright" and "dim" at a glowing screen while nothing at all was being done to it.

Blondlot had one instrument with no eyes in it. He put a thermopile, a very sensitive heat detector, in the beam, so sensitive that a candle twelve metres away made it move. The N rays did nothing to it. He then heated a platinum wire until it glowed dull red, connected it to an instrument that would notice a change of a sixtieth of a degree in its temperature, and shone the rays on it. The wire looked brighter to the eye, as it always did. The instrument did not move. He decided this proved that N rays do not carry heat. He did not consider the other reading: that nothing was arriving.

He also measured the rays as carefully as anyone has ever measured anything that was not there. With an aluminium prism he split them into eight separate kinds, each with its own number. With a fine grating he measured their wavelength, and found it between thirty and seventy times shorter than the wavelength of yellow light. He said his errors were no more than four per cent. All of these numbers came from one source: a person in the dark saying whether a glowing slit of paint had got brighter or not.

He also tried to prove it with photographs. A spark was photographed for five seconds with the rays on it, then five seconds with them blocked, over and over, moving the plate by hand each time to the beat of a metronome. In the finished pictures the "with rays" spark looked a little bigger and darker. He did this about forty times. He showed the pictures to visiting scientists, and they were impressed.

## The visitor

Robert W. Wood was an American, a professor of physics at Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore, and an expert on light. He had tried Blondlot's experiments and seen nothing. In August 1904, at a scientific meeting in England, the German physicists who had also failed asked him to go to Nancy and look. "You are an American," one of them said, by Wood's account, "and you Americans can do anything."

He went in September 1904, in the evening, and spent three hours in the dark room with Blondlot and his assistant. He did three things, and each one was a kind of test that nobody at Nancy had ever done. The rule of all three was the same: the person looking at the screen must not know what Wood was doing.

First, the spark. Blondlot said it dimmed when a hand was put in the beam. Wood asked him to call out the moment the hand went in. Blondlot called "bright" and "dark" while Wood's hand stayed perfectly still, and when Wood moved it, the calls "bore no relation whatever to its movements".

Second, the prism. Blondlot's finest experiment was a spectrum: an aluminium prism spread the N rays out into separate lines, and a glowing thread was moved across them to find each one. Wood pointed out that the slit letting the rays in was three millimetres wide, but the lines were supposed to be a tenth of a millimetre apart, which is like reading a newspaper through a letterbox from across the street. He was told this was "one of the inexplicable and astounding properties of the rays". Then, in the dark, Wood quietly lifted the prism off the apparatus. Blondlot went on reading off the lines exactly as before. Wood put it back and asked him to say which way round it was. Three tries, three wrong.

Third, the file. A steel file held near the eyes was supposed to make a dim clock face easier to see. Wood held the file himself, and then, without saying so, swapped it for a piece of wood the same size and shape. Wood gives off no N rays. The observer saw the clock just as well.

Wood wrote it all down and sent it to the London journal *Nature*, which printed it on September 29th 1904. He did not name Blondlot or Nancy. He did not need to.

## What happened next

A French magazine translated the letter and asked French physicists what they had seen. Nearly all of them said they had never managed to see the rays. A few famous men in Paris said they had. On December 19th 1904 the Academy of Sciences gave Blondlot one of its big prizes anyway, "for the whole of his work", and added, in careful words, that not all its members had been able to see the rays for themselves, and that it wished "to affirm its confidence in the experimenter".

That was the last high point. Other French scientists tried the photographs and found nothing in them. When Blondlot's papers came out in English in 1905, with a real glowing screen bound into the book, the reviewer said the whole proof depended "on a subjective impression on the mind of the experimenter, who sees, or imagines he sees, or imagines he does not see, a slight change". A last experiment from Nancy in 1906 was tried in London and failed. After that the rays simply stopped being mentioned.

Blondlot kept his job and lived until 1930. Wood lived until 1955 and is remembered for many things, but most of all for one evening and one prism.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**If the instrument is a person, the person must not know the answer.** Every N ray experiment at Nancy was done by people who knew when the rays were on. The one time someone hid the answer from them, with a still hand, a missing prism and a piece of wood, they could not tell. That is why doctors now test medicines by giving some patients a dummy pill without telling them which is which. It is not that people cheat. It is that people see what they expect to see, and cannot help it.

**A test that cannot fail is not a test.** Blondlot wrote that some people never succeed in seeing the rays. So anyone who did not see them was simply one of those people. Dozens of papers confirmed the rays, but every one of them used the same method and the same kind of eye. One mistake repeated many times is still one mistake.

**When a machine with no eyes says nothing is there, listen.** The heat detector that could sense a candle twelve metres away sensed nothing. Blondlot read this as a fact about the rays. It was a fact about the room.

**A famous name is not a measurement.** The Academy gave the prize because it trusted the man. Trust is the very thing a good experiment is designed to do without.

So the lesson is simple. Before you believe what you see, find a way to check it that does not depend on you. If you cannot find one, you have not found anything yet.
