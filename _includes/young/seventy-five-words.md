## Seventy-five words

On August 11th 1947 a government officer on the island of Rarotonga, in the middle of the Pacific Ocean, sent a telegram to the French governor in Tahiti. It had reached him by radio from six men on a reef far to the east, and it was seventy-five words long. It said that their expedition had landed on the atoll of Raroia, that all six men and their scientific equipment were "safe and unhurt ashore", that a change of wind had forced "our prehistoric type craft" in through the reef, where they had left the raft stuck and waded to safety, and that they had food, water and radio contact. "No inhabitants seen. All well." It was signed Thor Heyerdahl.

The next day the newspapers of the world printed it: a raft called the *Kon-Tiki* had ended its voyage on a coral reef. Ten days later a newspaper in Adelaide, Australia, ran a headline that said much more: "History Believed Verified By Raft Crash".

The seventy-five words said what had happened. The headline said what it meant. This is the story of the gap between them.

## The man with the idea

Thor Heyerdahl was a Norwegian, born in 1914. He studied zoology (the science of animals) at the University of Oslo, and in 1937 he and his wife spent most of a year living in a hut on Fatu Hiva, a small island in the Marquesas, in the far east of Polynesia. Polynesia is the huge triangle of islands in the Pacific Ocean, from Hawaii in the north to New Zealand in the south to Easter Island in the east.

On Fatu Hiva, Heyerdahl said, an old man told him by a fire that the islanders' ancestors had been brought across the sea by a god and chief called Tiki, from "a big country beyond the sea". And every evening on the beach Heyerdahl watched the waves roll in from the east, pushed by a wind that always blew from the east. Far beyond the horizon in that direction lay South America, 4,300 sea miles away. Everybody agreed that the Polynesians had come from Asia, in the west. Heyerdahl came home thinking that the first of them had come from the east, from Peru.

He read everything he could find. In the old legends of Peru there was a sun-god called Kon-Tiki, the leader of a white, bearded people who had built great stone monuments, been driven out, and gone away westward across the ocean. In Polynesia there were legends of Tiki, great stone statues, and a plant, the sweet potato, that came from America and had the same name in Peru and in Polynesia: kumara. Heyerdahl wrote all this down in a long manuscript, and after the war he took it to America to show the experts.

Nobody would read it. One old scientist in a museum in New York told him why. The people of ancient South America could not have reached the Pacific islands, he said, because "They had no boats!" Heyerdahl said they had rafts, made of balsa logs. The old man smiled and said: "Well, you can try a trip from Peru to the Pacific islands on a balsa-wood raft."

So Heyerdahl did.

## What the raft was meant to prove

Heyerdahl's idea had two parts. The first part was that a balsa raft of the old Peruvian kind could carry people alive across the ocean to Polynesia. The second part was that people from Peru had actually done it, long ago, and were the ancestors of the Polynesians.

A raft could test the first part. If it got across, the experts could no longer say the voyage was impossible. But the raft could not test the second part at all. Whether people from Peru had really gone was a question for other kinds of evidence: the languages the islanders spoke, the tools they left in the ground, the plants they grew, and later the genes in their bodies. Heyerdahl knew this. When a friend asked him what he would prove if the raft sank, he said: "Then I shan't have proved anything."

## The raft

Heyerdahl could not sail. He said so himself: he was "not even an amateur sailor". He chose five companions who were not sailors either, so that nobody could say afterwards that the voyage had worked only because they were better seamen than the old Peruvians. Herman Watzinger was an engineer, and designed the raft. Erik Hesselberg was a painter who had once been to navigation school; he was the only one who could find the raft's position with a sextant. Knut Haugland and Torstein Raaby were radio operators who had worked for the resistance in occupied Norway during the war. Bengt Danielsson was a Swedish scientist who knocked on Heyerdahl's hotel door in Peru and asked to come. A green parrot came too.

Nobody wanted to pay for a voyage that the insurance companies called suicide. A Norwegian colonel in Washington lent them money, and others followed. The American army lent them equipment to test, including, Watzinger worked out, 684 boxes of tinned pineapple. The government of Peru let them build in the naval dockyard at Callao and lent them a tug.

The old Peruvian rafts were known from drawings made by the Spaniards centuries before. Heyerdahl and Watzinger went into the jungle of Ecuador, cut huge balsa trees, and brought the logs to the coast. Balsa is the lightest wood there is. At Callao nine logs were laid in the water, the longest, 45 feet, in the middle, with shorter ones on each side, and tied together with about three hundred separate pieces of hemp rope. There was not a single nail in the whole raft. On top went a deck of split bamboo, a little hut of bamboo canes, two masts of mangrove wood tied together at the top, and a square sail. At the back was a long steering oar.

And there was one more thing. Pushed down into the cracks between the logs, standing on edge in the water, were five planks of fir, each about six feet long and two feet wide. The old Spanish writers called them guaras. Heyerdahl's crew called them centreboards, and thought they were there to stop the raft sliding sideways with the wind, like the keel of a boat. That is one thing they do. They do something else as well, and nobody on board knew it.

## One hundred and one days

The tug towed the *Kon-Tiki* out of Callao on April 28th 1947, and cast her off the next morning about fifty miles from land. "Now we'll have to start the engine, boys," Raaby said. There was no engine. The wind took the sail, the great Humboldt Current took the logs, and within a day they learned the first rule of rafts: there was no going back. If they had turned the bow towards Peru, the raft would simply have gone backwards, just as fast, out into the Pacific.

Everything the experts had said would go wrong went right. The logs did not soak up water and sink. The ropes did not wear through; they wore grooves into the soft wood and sat safe in them. When a big wave came up behind, the stern lifted and the raft rode over the top. When a wave did break on board, the water ran out between the logs. The raft drifted west at about 42 miles a day, and fish came to live in its shadow.

Somewhere past the middle of the ocean they discovered what the five boards were for. One of them had come loose and slipped under the raft. Watzinger and Haugland dived down and brought it back. Then, with the oar tied fast and the raft holding a steady course, they pushed the board down into a crack near the stern. The raft at once turned several degrees, from west towards north-west, and stayed on the new course. When they pulled the board up, she turned back. When they pulled it halfway up, she turned halfway back. By raising and lowering a board they could steer without touching the oar at all. It was, Heyerdahl wrote, something "no one has understood since the Indians themselves gave up this forgotten sport."

And then they put the discovery away. They had got used to the oar, Heyerdahl wrote, so they set a general course with the boards "and preferred to steer with the oar." They never tried to find out whether the boards would let the raft sail against the wind. That was the thing the old Spanish books had actually claimed.

On July 21st Watzinger fell overboard reaching for a sleeping bag. A raft cannot stop or turn round, and Haugland had to jump in after him with a rope. The same day the first real storm hit them, and then another that lasted five days. The sail tore, the oar broke, and the ropes holding all five boards burst. After that the boards hung loose under the raft, and the men could not dive down to fix them, because sharks now followed close behind.

## Raroia

On the ninety-third day they saw their first island, Pukapuka, and could not reach it. On August 3rd they drifted all day along the island of Angatau, so close that islanders paddled out in canoes and tried to tow the raft in. They could not: a raft will not be pulled against the wind. Ahead lay forty or fifty miles of coral reef, Takume and Raroia, straight across their path.

At dawn on August 7th Raaby saw a line of palm-covered islands all along the horizon. It was the ring of Raroia, an atoll about twenty-five miles across, with the reef that joins its islands lying along its eastern side like a sea wall. The raft was drifting sideways towards it. "With fixed centreboards we should still have had some hope of steering clear," Heyerdahl wrote. But the boards were loose and the sharks were there. They put on their shoes for the first time in a hundred days, packed the films and papers in waterproof bags, and cut the boards free so the raft would wash over the reef instead of catching on it.

Raaby sat in the hut tapping out a message to a radio amateur on Rarotonga: the raft was going on to the reef, and if nothing was heard for thirty-six hours, tell the Norwegian embassy in Washington. His last words were: "O.K. 50 yards left. Here we go. Good-bye."

The order was to hold on to the raft, whatever happened. The first wave lifted the *Kon-Tiki* and carried her in. The second came over everything like a wall of green glass. The hut was smashed, the mast went, the oar went. But the nine big logs held together, and the six men held on to them. When the raft lay still on the coral, they waded across the reef to a little island. Nobody was hurt. The voyage had taken 101 days and covered 4,300 sea miles.

## What happened next

The radio was got working again, and the seventy-five words went out to the world. After a few days canoes came across the lagoon from the village of Raroia, where 120 people lived. When the villagers saw the logs they all shouted at once: "That's not a boat, it's a pae-pae!" Pae-pae is the Polynesian word for a raft. A French government schooner came to fetch the six men and tow the raft to Tahiti, where an old chief who had adopted Heyerdahl on his first visit met him on the quay and said: "now we know where our fathers came from." A Norwegian ship carried the raft and the crew to America, and in October President Truman received them in Washington.

Heyerdahl wrote a book, *The Kon-Tiki Expedition*, which has been translated into more than seventy languages. The film the crew had shot on the raft won an Academy Award. The raft was put in its own museum in Oslo, where it still is.

## What the scientists said

In 1950 Heyerdahl published his results in a scientific journal in London. About the raft, he was exact. About his idea, he wrote that the purpose of the expedition "had been fully attained": a voyage from Peru had been shown to be possible. But in the same paper he admitted something else. The raft had drifted 4,300 miles westward, but it had been "unable to push eastward to a neighbouring island within sight." And in a footnote he pointed to a book by two Spanish officers, Juan and Ulloa, published two hundred years earlier, which said that with the guaras the rafts of Ecuador could sail, tack and work "in contrary winds, as ships with a keel". The instrument that could have tested this had been on board the whole time. In 1951 Heyerdahl wrote that his crew "had never seen one before, nor a guara".

Other scientists answered the second part of the idea, the part the raft could not test. A professor in Vienna, Robert Heine-Geldern, wrote that Heyerdahl's crossing deserved "the highest admiration", and then went through the evidence. The Polynesian languages were related to the languages of Indonesia and not to any language of America. Not one South American type of stone axe had ever been found in Polynesia, while every Polynesian stone tool was of a kind known in eastern Asia. Then he wrote the sentence that matters most in this story: "the fact that he proved that sailing with a balsa raft from Peru to Polynesia was technically possible does not yet prove that Peruvians actually came to Polynesia."

Over the next seventy years the other tests came in. Radiocarbon dating showed that the islands of eastern Polynesia were settled late and quickly, between about 1025 and 1290, by people coming from the west. The sweet potato is still argued over. And then came the genes. In 2020 a team led by Alexander Ioannidis studied the DNA of 807 people from seventeen Pacific islands and from the Pacific coast of America. They found "conclusive evidence" that Polynesians and Native Americans had met, once, in eastern Polynesia, around the year 1200, and that the Americans were most closely related to the native people of Colombia. The signal was strongest in the Marquesas, including Fatu Hiva, and in the Tuamotus, where the raft had landed. In 2024 ancient skeletons from Easter Island showed about ten per cent American ancestry too.

So there was a real meeting. But it was not Heyerdahl's story. The Polynesians were already there, speaking a language from the west and using tools from the west. The Americans were few, and related to the people of Colombia and Ecuador, not to the people of the Peruvian desert. The date was seven hundred years later than Heyerdahl's. And nobody can tell from the genes which way the boat went: a raft going west, or a Polynesian canoe going east and coming home.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Showing that something is possible is not the same as showing that it happened.** The raft proved that six men could drift from Peru to Polynesia on balsa logs. That was all it could prove, and Heyerdahl said so. But the headline said "History Verified", the chief in Tahiti said "now we know where our fathers came from", and millions of people who read the book believed that the voyage had settled the question. The question could only be settled by languages, stone tools, dates and genes, and when those answers came, they said something different.

**Read the instrument you are carrying.** The five boards were the one thing on the raft that could have shown what an old Peruvian raft was really able to do. The crew found out by accident that the boards could steer the raft. Then they went back to the oar, because they were used to it. The thing the old Spanish books had actually claimed, that with the boards a raft could sail against the wind, was never tried. The answer had been printed two hundred years before.

**Be honest in the small print, and make sure the small print gets read.** Heyerdahl wrote the truth in his scientific paper: the raft could not go east, and the old raftsmen might have done better. Heine-Geldern wrote the truth in his reply: possible does not mean it happened. Both were printed in a journal that few people read. The book and the film reached the world.

**Nature often gives a smaller, stranger answer than the one you were looking for.** Heyerdahl was wrong about Polynesia. But seventy years later the genes showed that around the year 1200, near the islands where his raft came ashore, a few Americans and a few Polynesians really did meet. It was not the proof he wanted. It was something true.

So the lesson is simple. When an experiment succeeds, ask exactly what it has shown, and no more. The most spectacular result is the easiest one to misread.
