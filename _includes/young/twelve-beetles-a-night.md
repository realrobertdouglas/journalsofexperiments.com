## The beetle

Sugar cane grows along the coast of Queensland, in the hot, wet north of Australia. In the 1930s the worst enemy of the cane was a brown beetle a little over an inch long, called the greyback cane beetle. The beetle itself does not do much harm. Its young do. A beetle's young is called a grub, and the greyback grub lives in the soil and eats the roots of the cane, so that whole fields fall over and die.

The beetle spends almost its whole life underground or up a tree. When the first storms come in November or December, the adult beetles climb out of the soil and fly straight to the trees, where they eat leaves and mate for about two weeks. Then the females fly down and burrow into the cane fields to lay their eggs. The grubs eat the roots all autumn. The only time a beetle is on the ground is the few minutes when it comes out of the soil and the few minutes when it goes back in.

The scientists who studied the beetle worked for the Bureau of Sugar Experiment Stations, which the Queensland government had set up in 1900 to help the sugar farmers. The Bureau's insect laboratory was at Meringa, just outside the town of Gordonvale, south of Cairns. For forty years the farmers had poisoned the soil, paid people to collect beetles, and still lost their crops.

## A number from Puerto Rico

In March 1932 a sugar scientists' meeting was held on the island of Puerto Rico, in the Caribbean. The Queensland government sent Arthur Bell from the Bureau. There Bell saw giant toads, as long as a man's hand, hopping about the cane fields at night and eating beetles. The toads had been brought to Puerto Rico from another island, Barbados, in 1920, and the farmers loved them.

A scientist at the meeting named Raquel Dexter had done something careful. An entomologist (a scientist who studies insects) can watch a toad all night and still not know what it has eaten. So Dexter caught 301 toads, early in the morning after they had fed, opened their stomachs, and counted what was inside. She found that about half of what the toads ate was insects that harmed crops. She also found that the number of beetles a toad ate depended entirely on where the toad was sitting. The Puerto Rico beetle flies up into banana trees to feed at night and drops back to the ground every morning. Under the banana trees, eighteen toads had eaten 224 beetles in one night, which is about twelve each. Where there were no banana trees, twenty toads had eaten eight beetles between them.

Both numbers were in her paper. Only one of them travelled. The piece "[The court of final appeal](/deeper/twelve-beetles-a-night/the-court-of-final-appeal/#young)" tells how she did her counting.

## The toads come to Queensland

Another scientist at the Puerto Rico meeting, Cyril Pemberton from Hawaii, took a box of toads home with him in 1932 and let them go. Three years later there were toads all over Honolulu, and the Hawaiian sugar station was sending out thousands of young toads a day to the plantations.

The Bureau in Queensland decided to do the same. In 1935 it sent Reginald Mungomery, its entomologist at Meringa, to Hawaii to bring some back. Mungomery was thirty-four. He had grown up in a cane district, had never been to university, and was very good at his job. On the night of June 1st 1935 he, Pemberton and another man went out with torches on the lawns of Honolulu and picked up 102 toads, half of them male and half female. They packed them in damp wood shavings in two wooden boxes and put them on a ship on June 3rd.

The boxes were opened in Brisbane on June 20th. The newspaper reporters who came to look counted a hundred toads, "big, brownish fellows" six to eight inches long, and said not one had been lost. Mungomery took them north by train. They reached Gordonvale on June 22nd. One toad had died on the way. The rest were thin and dry, but when they were put in water, Mungomery wrote, "they soon swelled out."

## The pond

At Meringa, Mungomery's colleague James Buzacott had built a home for them: a round concrete pond twelve feet across, shaped like a plate, with a sprinkler in the middle, water lilies and water hyacinth in the water, ferns and taro plants around the edge, and an eight-sided cage of chicken wire over the whole thing. The toads went in on June 24th. It was winter, and they sat cold and uninterested. They walked round and round the inside of the wire with their noses pressed against it until their noses were rubbed raw, so ferns were planted thickly against the wire to stop them seeing out.

Nobody in the world had ever got this kind of toad to lay eggs in a cage. Mungomery expected to keep the hundred toads locked up until the rains came in November, and then let them go. But on June 28th the weather turned warm, on the 29th and 30th it drizzled, and on the morning of July 1st 1935 there were long strings of jelly full of jet-black eggs tangled in the water weed. They hatched on July 4th. The pond was soon full of tadpoles, fed on oatmeal and rice.

On June 26th the Cairns newspaper had printed what the Bureau knew. "It was definitely established at Porto Rico that an average of twelve cane-beetles were consumed by one toad per night." And then it printed the one thing the Bureau did not know. "Their habits will be closely studied here, as it has not yet been established when the beetle is captured." In other words: nobody yet knew whether a toad in Queensland would ever meet a greyback beetle, because the greyback does not come down to the ground every morning the way the Puerto Rico beetle does. Mungomery knew this perfectly well. He wrote it himself, in July 1935, in the Bureau's own magazine: the toads "do not ascend trees," and how much good they did would depend on "the length of time the beetles actually spend on or near the ground." He ended his article with the words "time alone will show."

That question could have been tested in a few weeks. You would wait for the beetles to come up in November, put a few dozen toads in a cage with them, or catch toads in a beetle-infested field at dawn, and open their stomachs the way Dexter had. Nobody did it. The breeding had worked so quickly that it felt like success, and the next step was to let the toads go.

## Let loose

On August 19th 1935, seven weeks after the eggs hatched, Mungomery and Buzacott took about 2,400 young toads out of the pond and released them along the Little Mulgrave River and at other places around Gordonvale. It was the dry season, the very time Mungomery had said was wrong for the parents.

A week later the big sugar scientists' meeting that had started all this came to Australia. It opened in Brisbane on August 27th 1935 with scientists from twelve countries, and Mungomery read them a short paper about breeding the toads, with two photographs of the cage. Pemberton was in the chair. On September 13th the Queensland Minister for Agriculture, Frank Bulcock, opened the new buildings at Meringa with sixty-two of the visitors looking on. The toads were the thing everybody came to see.

By the end of November the Bureau had let thousands of young toads go around Gordonvale and Innisfail.

## "No more toads are to be liberated"

One man said no. Walter Froggatt was seventy-seven. He had been the New South Wales government's entomologist for nearly thirty years, had written the standard book on Australian insects, and had started three societies for people who loved nature. He wrote to the Commonwealth government in Canberra, which had the power, under the quarantine law (the law that controls which animals may be brought into the country), to stop the toads being carried about.

Froggatt also wrote a short paper, printed in 1936. He had read Dexter. He had talked to Pemberton, who told him that Hawaii had already given away 600,000 young toads and that "some millions" were roaming the islands, so many that the street cleaners of Honolulu swept up the squashed ones every morning. Then Froggatt asked the question the Bureau had never asked in public. The toad had only ever been let loose on islands. Australia was a continent. "Granted that these toads will do all that is expected of them," he wrote, "what will become of the increasing thousands which breed up in tropical Queensland?" He said they would eat the small creatures of the ground, the eggs of birds that nest on the ground, and the frogs and lizards. He said there was "no limit to their westward range." He said they would end up on people's lawns and verandahs. And he said that the toad "may become as great a pest as the rabbit or the cactus," which were the two worst pests Australia had ever known.

At the end of November 1935 the Commonwealth's Director-General of Health, Dr Cumpston, declared that no more giant toads were to be let go. The Sydney Morning Herald reported it on November 29th. The sugar farmers were furious. The Bureau was allowed to keep the toads it had already released, in four districts around Cairns, but could not carry them anywhere new.

So the Bureau did, at last, what it should have done at the start. Mungomery's men collected toads in the field, studied their droppings, and cut some open to see what they had eaten. But the question they were answering was the Commonwealth's question, not their own. Canberra wanted to know whether the toads ate useful insects, like bees. Nobody was asking whether they ate greyback beetles, because it was the wrong season, and because the toads were already out. The Bureau sent its findings to Canberra. The Queensland Premier wrote to the Prime Minister, Joseph Lyons, and the Prime Minister overruled his own Health Department. On September 15th 1936 Bulcock announced that the toads could go anywhere his department wished. The Brisbane paper's headline was "The Giant Toad Can Roam Again."

## What the toads did

They roamed. Mungomery reported in May 1937 that toads had now been released in nine new districts, along seven hundred miles of coast from Mossman in the north to the Isis in the south, and that the first Australian-born toads were already breeding in the pools of the Little Mulgrave, "continuously since last December." And then he wrote this: "With regard to the usefulness of toads against the Greyback beetle pest, it is much too early to judge their efficacy yet." Two years after the first release, with toads from one end of the sugar coast to the other, the man who had brought them was saying that nobody yet knew whether they ate the beetle.

Froggatt never found out. He died in Sydney on March 18th 1937, eight weeks before Mungomery's report.

In 1941 the Bureau finally opened a hundred toads caught in a cane district during the beetle season. The result was printed in a corner of the Queensland farming magazine: the toads had eaten "a few greyback cane beetles." A few. The magazine called it encouraging.

## What happened after

The greyback beetle was beaten in the end, but not by the toad. After the Second World War, Mungomery himself led the work on a chemical that killed the grubs in the soil. The toad had nothing to do with it.

Did the toad help the sugar at all? Nobody checked for eighty-five years. In 2020 three scientists at Macquarie University took the Bureau's own records of how much cane each acre of Queensland produced every year from 1900 to 1949, and looked for a jump after 1935. There was none. They also worked out why. Toads eat huge numbers of ants, and ants eat beetle grubs. Toads are poisonous, and they poison the goannas (large Australian lizards) that eat them, and goannas eat cane beetles by the dozen. And toads are food for native rats, which eat cane too. So the toads probably made the beetle problem a little worse, not better.

Meanwhile the toads kept going. They spread down the coast, west across the top of Australia, and in 2009 they crossed into Western Australia, moving about fifty kilometres a year. Where they arrive, goannas, some snakes, crocodiles and quolls (small, spotted native hunters) die from eating them. Some of those animals have learned, over the years, to leave toads alone. No Australian animal has died out because of the toad, which is the one thing Froggatt got wrong. He got the lawns and verandahs exactly right.

Fifteen years after the toads, another government laboratory in Australia released another animal's enemy against another pest: a virus, against the rabbit. That story is "[A Flying Pin](/experiments/2026/10/20/a-flying-pin.html)". The two make a pair. In one, the scientists tested their weapon for years and were still surprised. In the other, they did not test it at all.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A number is only true where it was measured.** Twelve beetles a night was a real measurement. Dexter made it under banana trees in Puerto Rico, where the beetles dropped to the ground every morning in front of the toads. In the same paper she wrote down that toads away from the trees ate almost nothing. Only the big number was carried to Australia. By the time it reached the Cairns newspaper it had lost everything that explained it.

**Do the cheap test before the big step.** Breeding the toads in a pond was easy, and it worked in nine days. Finding out whether a toad would eat a greyback beetle would have taken a cage, a few dozen toads and a few weeks in November. Nobody did it, because the easy success felt like the answer. The toads were released first, and the Bureau said it would find out whether they worked "when the toad has established itself in numbers." That is backwards. Once the toads were out, there was no way to call them back.

**Ask what else it will do.** The Bureau asked only one question: will the toad eat the beetle? Froggatt asked the other one: what will the toad eat instead, and where will it go? Nobody had an experiment for his question, so his question lost. But it was the right question, and it is the one Australia has been answering ever since.

**The scientist who said "time alone will show" was telling the truth.** Mungomery was honest. He wrote down the weakness in his own plan before he carried it out. Writing a doubt down is not the same as testing it. Time did show, and it took eighty-five years.

So the lesson is simple. When someone gives you a number that proves a plan will work, ask where the number was measured, and whether the place you are going is the same. If it is not, measure again before you let anything loose that cannot be put back.
