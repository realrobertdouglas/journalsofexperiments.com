## A room full of sea water

On the island of St Thomas, in the Caribbean, there is a white concrete building at the edge of a bay. In the summer of 1965 the upstairs rooms of that building were full of sea water, about two feet deep. A young woman lived in the water. So did a dolphin.

The woman was Margaret Howe, who was twenty-three. The dolphin was Peter, a bottlenose dolphin about five years old, which for a dolphin is a teenager. From June 15th 1965 to September 1st 1965, Margaret slept on a foam bed beside the water, cooked on a gas stove above it, and three times a day sat in it with a bucket of fish and a microphone and tried to teach Peter to speak English. Every lesson was recorded on tape.

This is the story of why she did it, who paid for it, what the tapes recorded, and the question nobody built a way to answer.

## The man with the idea

The idea belonged to a scientist named John Lilly. He was born in 1915, trained as a doctor and studied the brain. In the 1950s he invented a tank in which a person floats in warm water in the dark. Then he saw his first dolphins at a marine park in Florida, and found that a dolphin's brain is bigger than a human's and just as folded.

A big brain, Lilly thought, must mean a big mind. In 1958 he told a meeting of psychiatrists that dolphins were probably as intelligent as people and had a complicated language of their own. In 1961 he wrote a book called *Man and Dolphin* that said more: that if a dolphin were raised the way a human child is raised, with a person who talked to it all day, it would learn to speak English. The book sold very well.

It also got him money. The Navy wanted to know how dolphins used sound underwater. NASA, the space agency, was wondering how it would ever talk to creatures from another planet, and a dolphin seemed like good practice; in 1962 it gave Lilly's institute about eighty thousand dollars for a year. The National Institute of Mental Health gave him a five-year award, and other agencies gave more: at the peak, one historian says, about half a million dollars a year. With it he built a laboratory on the bay at the eastern end of St Thomas, and called it Dolphin Point.

## Three dolphins and one assistant

The laboratory had a sea pool at the bottom, washed clean by the waves, and rooms above it. A hoist like a lift carried dolphins up and down. In 1964 there were three dolphins. Sissy was the biggest, a loud, pushy adult female who, Margaret said later, "sort of ran the show." Pamela, called Pam, was a shy adult female who had been hurt before she came and kept away from people. Peter was the young male.

Margaret had no scientific training. She was living on the island, heard there were dolphins at a laboratory, drove out to look, and was told by the scientist in charge that she could stay and write down what the dolphins did. She started on February 7th 1964. By the end of that year the other scientists had left, and she was running the laboratory by herself with two workmen. Lilly lived in Miami and came for about five days a month.

She did the lessons. A dolphin makes clicks and whistles. It can also, if it puts its blowhole up out of the water, make loud noises in the air, and some of those noises sound a little like human speech. Lilly called them "humanoid" sounds. The lesson went like this: Margaret said a word, Peter made a noise, and if the noise was a humanoid noise he got a fish. If it was a click or a whistle he got nothing. Over months, the clicks and whistles went away and the humanoid noises took over.

## Why flood the room

Margaret thought the lessons were too short. Every evening the people went home and the dolphins were left alone with their big brains all night. So she asked Lilly to let her plaster and seal the upstairs rooms, fill them with sea water, and live there with one dolphin, day and night, the way a mother lives with a baby.

First she tried it for a week, in March 1965, in a tank with Pam, in sixteen inches of water. She learned that sixteen inches was too shallow for a dolphin's back, which dried and cracked, that you can sleep in a wet bed but wake up stiff, and that a dolphin in the next room can wake you whenever it likes.

Then she had the two upstairs rooms made watertight, an inside room forty feet by twenty and a balcony twenty by twenty, and filled them to twenty-two inches, later twenty-four. Nothing electric could be near the water, so she cooked on a propane stove. Her bed was a foam pallet that soon soaked through. There was a mirror at Peter's level, which he liked to talk to, a television and a telephone. One corner was hung with carpets to stop echoes; that was the recording studio, with the microphones in plastic bags and the tape machines in a dry room next door.

She chose Peter because he had had the least training. She wrote herself a timetable: up at half past seven, a recorded lesson at eight, another at noon, another at three, each with five pounds of fish; play, cleaning, notes and bills in between; bed at ten. Six days a week. Saturdays she got out, listened to the week's tapes, and wrote a report. The words to teach were written down in advance: the numbers one to five, the names Peter and Margaret, hello, ball, toy fish and a few more.

## One clear word

She moved in on June 15th 1965. The first nights were awful. Walking through two feet of water all day was exhausting. Peter nipped her feet, so she carried a broom. He had forgotten how to listen, and yelled every time she opened her mouth.

And in her very first report she wrote down something that turns out to be the whole story in one line. Peter had said, for the tape, one clear word: "BALL." But he had said it in the middle of a long ramble to himself, "and it could contain no meaning." Then she added: "But it is good pronunciation."

Think about that. A sound had been recorded that sounded like a word. It meant nothing. It was pronounced well. Which of those two things was the experiment measuring?

The honest answer, which Margaret gave herself, was that nobody could tell. The judge of whether Peter's noise was the start of a word was Margaret, who had just spent all day waiting to hear it. She noticed that when she listened to the tapes on Saturday, alone in the dry room, she was always more encouraged than she had been in the lesson itself. Lilly had written that the people in the room with the dolphin should not be the only judges: other people, who had never met the dolphin, should listen to the tapes and decide. Nobody ever did.

## The letter x

When Lilly printed Margaret's reports in a book in 1967, he also printed some of the tapes written out. He used a code. The letter c stood for any click or whistle. And the letter x stood for a humanoid sound "which is not a clear enough humanoid to accurately describe phonetically." In other words: a noise that sounded a bit like speech, but not enough to write down as a word.

The transcripts show the clicks disappearing. In November 1964 Peter's line is mostly c. By June 1965 it is almost all x. By August 10th 1965, after sixty-three days and nights in the flooded room, there are no clicks at all, and when he speaks out of turn Margaret can hush him. On October 8th 1965, after the experiment was over, she worked on the M in her own name, and Peter, who had learned to roll so that his blowhole was just under the water, gave her "Mxx xxx," and she clapped.

That is real progress of a kind. A wild animal learned to make noises in the air instead of underwater, to make the kind of noise that earned a fish, to match the number of sounds it heard, to copy the rise and fall of a voice, and to take turns. But it is a line of x. Nowhere in the record is there a word that someone who had not been in the room agreed was a word.

## What it was like

Margaret's notes are the best part of the record, because she wrote down what did not work. Her bed had three inches of water in it that would not come out. She ran a fever for a day. Her shins were bruised up and down from Peter's nose. After three weeks she had to get in the car and drive round the island just to be away from the laboratory for an hour.

She wrote that living with a dolphin was "more like a shadow than a roommate." He never left her. If she answered the phone, he walked her to it, pushing and nibbling, and talked loudly in humanoid for as long as the call lasted. She had become, she wrote, the other dolphin in his life.

Peter was a teenager, and in the fifth week he began to behave like one, pressing himself against her legs until she could not work with him. She wrote about this as a problem to be solved, because that is what it was, and she solved it in the way that seemed to her least disruptive to the lessons. Fifty years later she said it had not been sexual on her part, and that it had made the two of them closer. Out of all the hundreds of pages in the record, that is the part the world chose to remember; in the late 1970s a men's magazine printed a story about it. It is not what the experiment was about, and not what it found.

She also wrote down the loneliness. Some nights, after showering knee-deep in sea water and climbing into a clammy bed, she cried. Then Peter would call out in humanoid, loud and clear, and toss a ball into her bed until she got up and played. She said the loneliness "cannot be ignored." She was right. The person whose ears the whole result depended on was describing the mood in which she was listening.

## The other experiment

While Margaret was sealing the rooms in the spring of 1965, Lilly was at a conference about the drug LSD, telling doctors that he had been injecting it into dolphins since 1964 to see what it did. He said they breathed faster and made more noise. He also told the conference about "that girl in St. Thomas" who had spent a week in sixteen inches of water with a dolphin. Margaret said later that she insisted Peter must not be given the drug, and that Lilly agreed; a tape in his papers is labelled as Peter after LSD, recorded in April 1965. The two accounts do not agree, and nothing in print settles it.

By 1966, Margaret said, Lilly's interest in the talking dolphin was fading. "It didn't have the zing to it that LSD did at that time. And in the end the zing won."

## What happened

Margaret moved out of the wet room on September 1st 1965. She wrote her conclusions honestly. The tapes showed that Peter had learned to listen, to take turns, to be hushed and to make good attempts at copying her. Most of her conclusions were about how to build a better house. Lilly wrote that the work had been "eminently worthwhile," that Margaret had done "a magnificent job," and that she had married the photographer who took the pictures. Peter went back down to the sea pool. "Some person other than Margaret may continue the work," Lilly wrote. Nobody did.

The money was already going. A board of grant examiners visited at the end of 1965 and gave the laboratory bad marks. In the autumn of 1966 it was announced that it would close. Margaret's last job was to pack it up and ship the three dolphins to Lilly's other laboratory, in an old bank building in Miami, where the tanks were small and there was little sunlight. She could not keep Peter. "If he'd been a cat or a dog, then maybe. But not a dolphin."

A few weeks after the move, Lilly telephoned her. Peter was dead. A dolphin has to decide to take every breath, and Lilly's word for what Peter had done was suicide. Margaret said, fifty years later, that she was not terribly unhappy about it; she had been more unhappy about him living in those tanks. Lilly himself wrote in 1975 that in 1967 and 1968 he decided to stop working with dolphins because, in his words, "I no longer wanted to run a concentration camp for my friends," that five of his eight dolphins died soon after, and that he let the other three go.

## After

Lilly spent the rest of his life on the drugs and the tank, and became famous for them in a different way. He died in 2001, aged eighty-six. Margaret stayed on St Thomas, married John Lovatt, moved back into the empty laboratory, turned it into a house, and brought up three daughters in it. In 2014 she told her story to a film-maker, Christopher Riley, for a BBC documentary called *The Girl Who Talked to Dolphins*. Nobody has tried to teach a dolphin to speak English since.

The tapes still exist. Lilly's papers are at Stanford University in California, and nearly fifty boxes hold reels of tape of his dolphins from 1961 to 1968, some labelled with Peter's name. Nobody has published a study of them. The one thing the experiment never had, a listener who had not been in the room and had not given the fish, is still possible.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**If you only count the thing you reward, you will find that it goes up.** Peter got a fish for humanoid sounds and nothing for clicks. So the humanoid sounds increased and the clicks went away. That proves the reward worked. It does not prove that the humanoid sounds were words. The count was measuring Margaret's hand, not Peter's mind.

**The person who wants the result should not be the only judge.** Lilly wrote the rule himself: people who had never met the dolphin should listen to the tapes. It never happened. The only judge was Margaret, who was lonely, tired, hopeful, and honest enough to say so. That was not her fault. The experiment was designed so that her hope and the result could not be told apart.

**The part you can check is often smaller than the part you claim.** Lilly's one solid paper showed that a dolphin can match the number of sounds it hears, ninety-two times out of a hundred. That is true and interesting. It is not language. The flooded room was built as if it were.

**An experiment that cannot fail cannot teach.** Margaret's motto was "no matter how long it takes." After a month she wrote that she was thinking "in terms of forever." Every problem in the ten weeks became a reason to build a bigger house, not a reason to doubt the idea. What stopped it was the money, not a result.

**Write down what went wrong.** The best thing in the whole record is Margaret's notes. She wrote down the wet bed, the bruises, the tears, the days she could not concentrate. Because she did, we can see exactly where the result came from. Most people only write down the good days.

So the lesson is simple. Before you start, decide who will judge the result, and make sure it is not the person who wants it most. Then make sure that what you count is the thing you are really asking about, and not just the thing you are rewarding.
