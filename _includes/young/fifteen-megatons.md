## The needle at the end of the scale

On the afternoon of March 1st 1954, on a tiny island called Rongerik in the middle of the Pacific Ocean, a radiation meter ran out of numbers. It was a recording meter: a pen that drew a line on a moving strip of paper, and the line showed how much radiation was in the air. It had been built to measure very small amounts, and its scale stopped at one hundred. At ten minutes to three the pen went past the top of the paper and stayed there.

Twenty-eight American weathermen lived on Rongerik. They had been told that if the meter did anything unusual they should radio a man called Mr Breslin on the command ship, so at a quarter past three they sent a message: "over 100". Nobody on the ship could tell whether that meant 101 or 10,000, and the first guess was that the meter had broken. The weathermen went back to work. A fine grey dust was settling on the tables in their dining hall. They put on long sleeves.

That morning, 135 nautical miles to the west, the United States had set off the biggest explosion it ever made. The test was called Bravo. The scientists had expected the bomb to be as powerful as six million tons of ordinary explosive. It was as powerful as fifteen million. This is the story of that difference, and of how it was measured: not by the scientists' instruments, but by the islands and the ships and the people who happened to be downwind.

## Who ran it

In 1954 the United States was testing hydrogen bombs, which are far more powerful than the atomic bombs of 1945, at two ring-shaped coral islands in the Marshall Islands called Bikini and Enewetak. The tests were run by a group called Joint Task Force 7: more than ten thousand soldiers, sailors, airmen and scientists, commanded by an Army general, Percy Clarkson, with Alvin Graves of the Los Alamos laboratory as his scientific adviser. In Washington the Atomic Energy Commission, the government body in charge of bombs and reactors, was led by Lewis Strauss.

The Marshall Islands were not part of the United States. They were a trust territory: islands the United Nations had put in America's care after the Second World War. The people of Bikini had been moved off their atoll in 1946 so that it could be used for tests. East of Bikini were other atolls where people still lived. On Rongelap, about 100 nautical miles away, there were 82 people that week. On Rongerik, the weathermen. On Utirik, about 300 miles away, 154 people. Nobody had moved them, because the plan said the fallout would not go their way.

## Two questions, one of them never asked

Fallout is the dust from a nuclear explosion. When a bomb goes off on the ground, it sucks up earth, or coral, or water, and turns it into a cloud of radioactive dust. The heavy pieces fall back quickly. The fine pieces blow away on the wind and come down later, sometimes a long way off.

The scientists were trying to answer one question: would a new kind of hydrogen bomb work? The first hydrogen bomb, fired in 1952, had used liquid fuel kept colder than anything on Earth, and it had weighed as much as a building. The new bomb, nicknamed Shrimp, used a solid fuel made with lithium, a light metal. Natural lithium is mostly one kind of atom, lithium-7, with a little of another, lithium-6. The designers thought only the lithium-6 would take part in the explosion, and that the lithium-7 would just sit there. They expected about six megatons (a megaton is a million tons of ordinary explosive). They also worked out the most it could possibly be: fifteen.

The second question was where the dust would go. Nobody treated that as a question; they treated it as a calculation. When the 1952 bomb was fired, almost none of its dust was ever found: only about 5 per cent of it could be accounted for. The scientists thought the rest had been thrown so high that it had stayed up in the stratosphere, the calm layer of the sky above the weather, and would drift down slowly over the whole world, too thin to hurt anyone. So when they drew the map of where Bravo's dust would land, they drew the cloud as a column fifteen miles across, standing over the bomb up to the bottom of the stratosphere. The top of the cloud, the part they had never been able to find, was left off the drawing.

## What they built

The bomb was built on a little artificial island of dredged coral on the reef at the edge of Bikini, so that it could be surrounded by instruments. Everybody except a small firing party was taken off Bikini onto ships, which sailed about thirty miles away to the south-east. The firing party stayed in a concrete bunker twenty miles across the lagoon, to send the signal that would fire the bomb.

The safety equipment outside Bikini was thin. Patrol planes searched the sea for ships before the test, but only in the direction the dust was expected to go, away from the islands. Radiation meters, like the one on Rongerik, had been left on a few islands, with scales that stopped at one hundred because nobody expected more. A single plane would fly after the explosion to find the cloud.

There was a "danger area" drawn on the sea charts, about 335 miles long and 150 wide. Its eastern edge had been drawn so that Rongelap would be just outside it, because if Rongelap were inside, the people would have had to be moved. The danger area was really there to keep foreign ships away, so that they could not collect dust and learn how big the bomb was.

On the night before the test the weather officers gave their forecasts at six o'clock, at midnight and at half past four in the morning. At midnight some of the winds high up were blowing slowly toward Rongelap, but the officers worked out that the dust would take twelve to fifteen hours to get there, and would be harmless by then. The general decided to go ahead.

## Over 100

At a quarter to seven on the morning of March 1st 1954 the bomb went off. The fireball was nearly three miles wide. In five minutes the cloud was more than 115,000 feet high, far up in the stratosphere, and had spread out at the top like a giant mushroom; ten minutes after the explosion the cap was 75 miles across. Half an hour later a camera plane saw dust pouring out of the bottom of the cap, like rain falling from a cloud. Where the little island had been there was a hole in the reef a mile across.

The dust began to fall on everything. First on the bunker, where the men inside watched the radiation outside climb to 250 roentgens an hour, a rate at which a few hours in the open would be deadly. Then on the ships thirty miles away, which found themselves in a snow of white grit at eight in the morning and ran south with their fire hoses going. Then on Rongelap, where at about ten o'clock a white powder began to drift down on the village. "At 11:30 the classes were dismissed and the students and I went out and were greeted by the powder-like particle as it began to fall on the land," the schoolteacher, Billiet Edmond, wrote later. The powder whitened people's hair and stuck to their skin. The island's health worker, Jabwe, closed the wells and told everyone not to drink the water. Nobody had told the Marshallese what fallout was.

Then on Rongerik, where the meter went off the scale at ten to three.

On the command ship, the message from Rongerik arrived at the same time as a report from the plane that was supposed to be tracking the cloud. The plane said there was no radiation near Rongerik. So the officers believed the plane and not the meter. What they did not know was that the plane had never been near Rongerik at all: a mix-up had kept it circling on the wrong side of Bikini for six hours. That night, the official history says, the headquarters "did not know where the cloud was, nor where it had been".

The next morning an Air Force radiation expert, Captain Louis Chrestensen, flew to Rongerik, measured the radiation on the ground, and without waiting for orders began flying the 28 weathermen out. His readings reached the command ship at about one o'clock on March 2nd. Only then did the general understand how bad it was. A warship, the *Philip*, sailed for Rongelap that night and took everyone off at dawn on March 3rd, about fifty hours after the powder fell. On the ship the children were given milk and ice cream. The next day another warship took the 154 people off Utirik, ferrying them through the surf on rubber rafts.

## The boat nobody knew about

There was one more thing downwind, and nobody in the task force knew it was there. A Japanese fishing boat, the *Fukuryu Maru* No. 5, which means Fortunate Dragon, was about 85 miles east of Bikini, just outside the danger area, with 23 men aboard catching tuna. Before dawn they saw a red light rise in the west, which turned yellow-white and then faded. Seven or eight minutes later they heard two bangs. About three hours after the flash, white ash began to fall on the deck, and it kept falling until noon. The men worked on through it.

For a week they sailed home feeling sick and strange. Then their skin began to burn and blister wherever the ash had touched it. "On the first night we were unable to eat our supper," the radio operator, Aikichi Kuboyama, said afterwards. They did not call for help, because they were afraid of what the Americans might do to a boat found near Bikini. They reached their home port of Yaizu on March 14th 1954, and within two days the story was in every newspaper in Japan. The story of the boat is told in the companion piece [*The Fortunate Dragon*](/deeper/fifteen-megatons/the-fortunate-dragon/#young).

## What happened to the people

At the Navy base on Kwajalein the people from Rongelap were washed and examined. Over the next weeks their hair fell out and burns appeared on their skin, worst on the tops of their feet, where the powder had settled. The doctors worked out that the people on Rongelap had received about 175 rad, a unit of radiation dose: not enough to kill, but enough to make them ill, and enough to cause harm years later. Three days after the evacuation the military set up a medical study of the exposed people, which had never been part of the plan. One of the doctors at Los Alamos wrote: "Purely by luck, good or bad, we did manage to get some unusually good results on humans at Rongelap."

For ten days the government said nothing. Then it announced that 28 Americans and 236 islanders had been moved "as a precautionary measure" and were "reported well". At the end of March, Lewis Strauss gave a statement at the White House. He said the bomb had been "about double" what was calculated, and that "the wind failed to follow the predictions" and had blown the dust onto the islands. He said the fishing boat "must have been well within the danger area". Both of those things turned out to be wrong. The task force's own report, two weeks later, said the wind forecast had been "essentially correct", and the Japanese government showed that the boat had been outside the line. The scientists on the task force already knew the real reason the islands had been hit: the dust had come from the top of the cloud, the part that had been left off the drawing.

The radio operator, Aikichi Kuboyama, died in hospital on September 23rd 1954. The Japanese doctors said radiation had killed him; the American doctors, who were not allowed to examine him, thought the treatment might have. In January 1955 the United States paid Japan two million dollars. In February 1955 the Commission finally told the public what Bravo had shown: that a bomb like it, exploded on the ground, could make about 7,000 square miles of land so radioactive that people would have to leave or shelter to survive.

## After

The people of Rongelap were taken home in 1957, and doctors came every year to examine them. Nine years after the test a twelve-year-old girl was found to have lumps on her thyroid, a gland in the neck that is easily harmed by radioactive dust. In time about a third of the people who had been on Rongelap that day developed thyroid tumours, most of them people who had been children in 1954. A boy named Lekoj Anjain, who had been one year old when the powder fell, died of leukaemia in 1972, aged nineteen. In 1985 the people of Rongelap decided they could not trust their island and left it again. They have not gone back.

In 2013 the United States government published a long report on Bravo. It said that the wind had not shifted, and that the bomb's size was not really the cause either: a six-megaton bomb would have made nearly as big a cloud, and the dust would still have reached Rongelap. The real mistake was in the drawing: "The error in forecasting the BRAVO fallout was that the contribution from the stratosphere was not adequately considered." One of the scientists who had been there wrote of the hours after the explosion: "To call the result 'Chaos' is putting it mildly."

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**If you only test one of your two guesses, the other one can hurt you.** The scientists tested whether the bomb would work, with every instrument they had, and it worked. They did not test where the dust would go. They worked it out on paper from one old explosion whose dust had never been found, and left the top of the cloud off the drawing. The part you leave off the drawing is the part that gets you.

**When a meter goes off the end of its scale, do not read it as the top number.** "Over 100" could have meant a thousand. The officers treated it as about a hundred and waited. The meter was telling the truth; it had simply been built too small to tell all of it.

**If you do not put instruments where the harm might go, whatever is there becomes the instrument.** Nothing of the task force's was placed to measure fallout on Rongelap or on a fishing boat, because the plan said there would be none. So the measurement was made by a weather station's pen, by a ship's deck, and by people's skin. Those are bad instruments: they report late, and they cannot be read until the harm is done.

**A line drawn for one reason gets used for another.** The danger area was drawn to keep foreign ships away, and its edge was placed to leave Rongelap outside so its people would not have to move. Then everyone treated the line as if it were the edge of safety: the fishermen stayed outside it and thought they were safe, and the chairman of the Commission thought anyone who was burned must have been inside.

**The number everybody talks about is not always the one that matters.** Everyone remembered "fifteen megatons" and "the wind shifted". The task force's own scientists knew within weeks that the wind had been forecast correctly, and that a smaller bomb would have done nearly the same. The lesson was in the drawing and in the lost messages, and that lesson was never read out loud.

So the lesson is simple. Before you run a big experiment, ask what could go wrong for the people who are not part of it, and put a real instrument there to find out, one with a scale big enough to tell you the truth.
