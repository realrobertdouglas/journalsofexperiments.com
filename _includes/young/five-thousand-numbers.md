## The chair

On December 30th 1972, in Santiago, the capital of Chile, the President of the country sat down in a white plastic chair with an orange cushion and pressed a button on the arm. The chair was one of seven, set in a ring in a six-sided room with wooden walls. There was no table and no paper. On the wall in front of him were four screens. Behind the wall were slide projectors, and when you pressed a button the projectors were supposed to throw a chart onto the screens showing how the country's factories were doing.

It was midsummer, the machinery behind the wall had got too hot, and the projectors did the wrong thing. "The projectors went crazy," one of the designers said later. The President, Salvador Allende, who had been a doctor before he was a politician, said something like: it would be good to have this working; keep at it.

Eight days later a newspaper in London told its readers that Chile was being run by computer. It was not. What existed was about a hundred telex machines in factories (a telex: a typewriter joined to a telephone line, which printed what you typed on a matching machine far away), a program on a government computer that read the numbers the factories sent in each day, a half-built model of the economy, and the room. All the numbers that came in each day added up to fewer than 5,000.

Those numbers were the point. The experiment was whether a government could read them, and then leave the factories alone.

## Who ran it

In 1970 Chile elected Salvador Allende president. He was a socialist, and his government set out to take the copper mines, the banks and the big factories into public ownership, by law and by vote, without a civil war. Within two years the state owned or ran about two hundred businesses. Many of the old bosses had left, sometimes taking the company records with them, and the government put in its own managers, called interventors.

The man in charge of the factories day to day was Fernando Flores, an engineer of twenty-eight, who worked for CORFO, the state development corporation. As a student he had done odd jobs for a British consulting firm whose boss was Stafford Beer.

Beer was an Englishman, born in 1926, who had worked in the steel industry and become famous for applying a new science called cybernetics to running companies. Cybernetics is the study of how things steer themselves: how a body keeps its temperature steady, how a thermostat keeps a room warm, how a firm or a government notices trouble and corrects it. Beer believed the rules were the same for all of them.

In July 1971 Flores wrote to Beer. He said he was now in a position to try cybernetics on a whole country, and asked for help. Beer wrote back that he would drop any of his other contracts for the chance, "because I believe your country is really going to do it." The two met in London in August. Beer flew to Chile in November 1971 and went to work within an hour of landing. On November 12th 1971 he went alone, with an interpreter, to the presidential palace to explain the plan to Allende. He drew his model on a sheet of paper: five levels, copied from the human nervous system, with the brain at the top. When he got to the top level, Allende smiled and said: "At last: the people." Allende's conditions were that the system must leave the factories free, must not become a bureaucracy, and must involve the workers. Over the next two years Beer flew between London and Santiago ten or eleven times.

## What they wanted to find out

Beer said a government running factories has two questions. What is going on in all those plants? And how soon will we know about today's results? Government statistics, he said, were six months out of date. He wanted a government that knew about today, today.

So the idea had two parts.

First, measurement. Every state factory would send a handful of numbers to Santiago every day: how much came in, how much went out, where the bottleneck was, how many workers were absent. A computer program would compare each number with the factory's normal and decide, using statistics, whether a change was real or just noise. Only real changes would be reported, and only to the factory itself.

Second, freedom. If the centre knew that it would be told about real trouble, it could leave every factory alone until then. Each factory would have a set time to fix a problem; only if the time ran out would the warning go up a level. Beer called this "effective freedom" and believed it could be calculated.

Notice that the second part depends on the first. The freedom only works if the factory managers read the warnings and act on them. So the real instrument of the experiment was not the room. It was the daily numbers and the reports the computer sent back, and the test was whether anyone read them.

## What they built

The project was called Cybersyn in English and Synco in Spanish. It had four parts.

The network. Chile had fewer than fifty computers in the whole country, and the government could spare time on only one. So Beer proposed telex machines. CORFO owned one or two and had no money to buy more. Then an army officer on Flores's staff found that the state telephone company had 400 telex machines sitting in a store, bought by the previous government and never used. The team called it their Aladdin's cave. The machines were lent to factories, and by March 1972 numbers were coming in. By October 1972 ninety-nine machines were connected, from the far north of the country to the far south. The figure of 500 telexes that people often give today was the number CORFO expected to add in 1973.

The filter. A London consulting firm wrote the first version of the computer program, called Cyberstride, by March 1972. It used a new statistical method for spotting real changes in a series of numbers. By July 1972 it was checking thirty numbers a day. The government's best computer was so busy that the day's figures could wait two days in the queue, so a Chilean engineer, Isaquino Benadof, rewrote the program to run on a less-used machine.

The model. A second British team wrote a computer model of the whole Chilean economy, called CHECO, so that the government could try out decisions before making them. Tested against the country's real statistics, it got the wrong answers. Beer said later that it never worked.

The room. Chile's new state design group built the room described at the start: six walls, seven chairs, buttons in the armrests, screens with slide projectors behind them, a wall for the five-level model, a wall of red lights for alarms, and a wall for the model of the economy. It was ready to sit in at the end of December 1972. Beer called it a set for a futuristic film. The companion piece "[The room](/deeper/five-thousand-numbers/the-room/#young)" describes it in detail.

The whole thing was cheap for a government. By September 1973 Chile had paid Beer about 79,000 pounds for everything. Fewer than a hundred scientists worked on it.

## Two thousand telexes

In October 1972 the people who owned Chile's lorries went on strike against the government. By October 10th 12,000 lorry owners had stopped work, and soon it was 40,000. Shops shut, most of the buses in Santiago stopped, and doctors, lawyers and engineers joined in. Nothing moved: no food, no fuel, no raw materials for the factories. Roads were blocked.

Flores did not use the room or the model. He set up an emergency headquarters in the presidential palace, using a private telephone list and the telex machines that were already in the factories. The Cybersyn telex room at CORFO became the command centre for industry. Alongside the production figures, the machines now carried plain messages. A factory said: we have no fuel. Another said: we have fuel. The room passed one to the other. The government had about 200 lorries it could rely on against the 40,000 that had stopped, and the network was how it put them where they were needed. Beer, following from England by telex, reckoned 2,000 messages a day went over the network. Twenty machines clattering in one room, he said, and "the noise was indescribable."

The strike ended on November 2nd, when Allende brought army generals into his government. Beer always said afterwards that the telexes had saved the government. The Chileans who ran the network were more careful: it helped a lot, one of them said, but so did the people in the neighbourhoods and the factories who kept things going, and so did the generals. Flores, who was made a minister as the strike ended, said that most people in the government never thought of the telex network as part of Cybersyn; they just thought it was a clever way of getting messages around. And he had begun to doubt the rest. "The room was not going to stop tanks and planes and bigger strikes," he said thirty years later.

## Big Brother

The team planned to announce the project on February 14th 1973, when Beer was to give a lecture in Brighton, in England. But on January 7th 1973 the *Observer* newspaper in London printed the story first, under the headline "Chile run by computer", and said the system had been "secretly brought into operation". An American newspaper reprinted it with a cartoon of a giant computer watching a small man in a sombrero through binoculars. A Chilean magazine ran a story called "Mr Beer's Big Brother", after the all-seeing ruler in George Orwell's novel *1984*. Beer wanted to show the room on television to prove it was not sinister. The Chileans said no, because then someone might sabotage it.

Beer gave his lecture anyway. The next morning a science magazine, the *New Scientist*, printed two articles by a reporter named Joseph Hanlon. One described the system carefully: the two computers, the fewer than 5,000 numbers a day, the one report a week to each manager. The other called Beer a technocrat (someone who wants experts and machines to run things) and said the system was "designed by a small group of scientists and imposed from the top in secret." Hanlon also made a prediction: managers would soon learn to feed the computer the numbers it wanted, whatever was happening in the factory. In March a right-wing Chilean magazine ran a story called "Secret plan Cyberstride: Popular Unity controls us by computer", and an American computer expert wrote to the *New Scientist* that it was impossible for Chile to have built such a thing at all. The companion piece "[Fifteen February](/deeper/five-thousand-numbers/fifteen-february/#young)" tells that story.

## What it looked like from inside

Inside, it looked different from both the dream and the nightmare.

At the computer centre, Benadof's office got the factories' numbers by telex each afternoon, ran the program, and telexed the results to the control room at CORFO. They did not tell the factory managers, and they did not use Beer's careful system of warnings and time limits. When the program spotted a problem, the news went to the factory, the CORFO telex room and the project's director all at once. Getting the factories to send their numbers every day was a constant struggle.

In the factories, the system looked like a visit from young engineers. The workers, who were supposed to help draw the charts of their own plants, were mostly not asked. In one factory where the manager believed in the system, the workers used the numbers to argue for bonuses; when that manager left, the work stopped.

In the government, the one thing that was read every day was a report written at CORFO from the telex messages and carried by hand to the palace. Its figures were about three days old. That was much better than six months. But it was not the clever statistical filter at work. It was a summary of telexes.

In April 1973 one of the young engineers wrote to Beer: "The cybernetic adventure is apparently coming to an end, or is it not?" Managers took the tools, he said, "but just them." The big idea was "not accepted, not even understood." In May 1973 Allende told Congress that about a quarter of the state's factories were connected to the system in some degree. In August Beer wrote to Allende, sadly, that the workers were running their factories their own way, without the models prepared for them. The letter was never answered.

## What happened

On September 8th 1973 Allende asked for the room to be moved into the presidential palace. On September 11th 1973 the army, navy and air force overthrew the government. The palace was bombed by the air force, and by two in the afternoon Allende was dead inside it. The project's director walked out of the CORFO building with a colleague carrying four parcels of the project's papers, to save them.

The army tried to understand the room. Officers questioned the team. One soldier took a knife and stabbed every one of the slides the designers had made. Then the army lost interest and the room was taken apart. The chief designer escaped Chile with the only colour photograph of it. Flores was arrested on the day of the coup and held for three years in prison camps. Beer never went back to Chile. Within two years the new military government had decided to sell the factories back to private owners, so a system for running state factories had nothing left to run.

Fifty years later, in 2023, the room was built again, full size, with working buttons, for an exhibition under the same palace, with the original designers helping.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**An instrument is only an instrument if someone has to read it.** The daily numbers were collected, filtered and reported, exactly as designed. But the reports went to the wrong place, the managers mostly did not know they existed, and the workers were never told. In the one factory where the manager read the numbers, they were used. Everywhere else they were just numbers arriving. Measuring something is the easy part. Making sure someone reads the measurement, and acts on it, is the experiment.

**The part that worked was the part nobody designed.** The telex network was built to carry statistics. In the strike it carried messages instead: this factory has no fuel, that road is open, send the lorry here. Two thousand messages a day, read by people who had to act on them at once, kept a government going for three weeks. Beer said "communication is indeed control", and he was right, but not in the way he meant. Plain messages between people did the work. The clever filter did not.

**People read what they can see, not what matters.** The newspapers saw the room: seven chairs, buttons, screens, something out of a science-fiction film. They called it Big Brother. Nobody looked at the actual instrument, the fewer than 5,000 numbers a day, or asked whether anyone was reading it. The one reporter who found the real weakness asked a simple question: what would a manager do with one of these reports?

**A model of a country is not the country.** The model of the economy never worked. And the numbers that were measured, how much a factory made, were a small part of what was happening in Chile: prices rising fast, shortages, strikes, a hostile Congress, and workers who were people with opinions, not parts of a machine. None of that was in any chart. And Cybersyn ran for only twenty-two months of a planned five years before a coup ended it, so nobody knows whether the managers would have started reading the reports in year three. The honest answer to "did it work?" is "nobody found out."

So the lesson is simple. Before you build a machine to measure something, decide who will read the measurement and what they will be obliged to do with it. Otherwise you may end up with a beautiful room, a frightened press, and five thousand numbers a day that nobody looks at.
