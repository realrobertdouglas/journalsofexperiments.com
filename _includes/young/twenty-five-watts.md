## The factory

The Hawthorne Works was an enormous factory on the edge of Chicago, in a town called Cicero. It belonged to the Western Electric Company, which made the telephones, cables and switchboards for the whole American telephone system. In 1926 the company told its workers that the Works had more than 100 buildings. Somewhere between 30,000 and 40,000 people worked there. Many of them were immigrants, or the children of immigrants, from Poland, Bohemia and Scandinavia. Thousands of the workers were young women, in their teens and early twenties, who sat at long benches putting together small parts.

Two of those jobs matter in this story. One was assembling relays. A relay is a small switch used in a telephone exchange, made of about 35 tiny parts held together by four screws. It took about a minute to make one, and a good assembler made about 500 a day. The other job was winding coils: wrapping fine wire around a spool, using a foot pedal to turn the machine.

## Why anyone tested the lamps

In the 1920s the companies that made electric light bulbs wanted factories to buy more of them. They wanted proof that brighter light made workers produce more. So in 1923 the General Electric Company gave 50,000 dollars to a committee of the National Research Council, a scientific body in Washington, to find that proof. Thomas Edison, who had invented the light bulb, was the committee's honorary chairman. Its real chairman was Dugald Jackson, the head of electrical engineering at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and he sent a young engineer called Charles Snow to the Hawthorne Works to run the tests.

At the factory the tests were looked after by George Pennock, a superintendent, with a young man from the piece rates office, Homer Hibarger, as Snow's assistant. The question they were asked to answer was simple. If you put brighter lamps over the benches, how much more work gets done?

## Three winters

The first test began in November 1924 and ran until April 1925. Snow chose three departments: the relay assemblers, the coil winders and the women who inspected small metal parts. About 64 women took part. The old lamps were measured first, and then new, brighter lamps were put in, in steps: 100 watts, then 200, then 300. Light was measured in foot-candles, which is the unit engineers used then. The relay room went from about 5 foot-candles up to 44. Output was written down five times a day. The lamps were always changed on a Sunday, because Sunday was the only day the factory was shut, so every new condition began on a Monday.

The results were a muddle. In one department output "bobbed up and down" without any connection to the light. In another it went up all through the winter, bright or dim. Snow wrote that the test had shown how many other things, apart from light, were pushing output up and down at the same time. He also wrote something that would matter later. The women knew they were in a test, because it had been explained to them, and their output was being written down far more often than usual. That, he said, was a kind of extra supervision, and it could raise output by itself.

The second test, in the spring of 1926, was cleverer. Thirty-two coil winders were split into two groups of 16, in different buildings so they could not compete. One group kept its old lamps. The other got brighter and brighter ones. Both groups improved by almost exactly the same amount. Brighter light had made no difference that anyone could measure.

The third test was the strangest. Daylight from the windows had spoiled the first two tests, so two identical little rooms were built inside the coil winding department, with the glass roofs papered over and the gaps stuffed with burlap. Sixteen women worked in each. From September 1926 one group had 11 foot-candles all the time. The other group's light was turned down, step by step: 9, 7, 6, 5, 4, and then 2.7 foot-candles for six weeks.

## Twenty-five watts

On Monday, February 28th 1927, the lamps over the second group were changed for bulbs of only 25 watts. The light at the benches was 1.4 foot-candles, about an eighth of what the other group had. The company would only allow this for one day. By the end of that day the women in the dim room had wound 13.4 coils an hour each. The women in the bright room had wound 12.3. The next day the full light came back, and the two groups swapped rooms.

Snow wrote in his report that the women would keep up their usual output "even in the face of insufficient illumination and the attending discomfort." In the summary he published later, he said that at the very lowest light the women protested that they could hardly see, and output fell.

Hibarger then tried two tests of his own. He took two willing assemblers into a locker room that could be made completely dark, and turned the light down over a fortnight to the brightness of a moonlit night. One account says they kept up their output and said their eyes felt better; another, from the same records, says output dropped and the women complained. He also had an electrician come in, in front of the coil winders, and change the bulbs for bulbs exactly the same, while telling them the light was going up, and then that it was going down. The women said they liked the brighter light and disliked the dimmer. Their output did not change.

Snow left the factory in April 1927. He had written four-fifths of his final report, and it was never finished, because the answer it gave was not the one the lighting industry had paid for. In December 1928 Pennock ordered all the records of the lighting tests destroyed. But Snow had kept carbon copies of everything, and he kept them for thirty years.

## The test room

While the last lighting test was still running, Hibarger started a new experiment. He asked two experienced relay assemblers who were friends to choose four more women, and in May 1927 the five assemblers and a sixth woman who fetched their parts were moved into a small room walled off in a corner of the fifth floor. It was the same room that had been used for the lighting tests. At each woman's right hand was a hole in the bench, and every finished relay dropped through it into a machine that punched a hole in a moving paper tape. The company could tell how many relays each woman had made in any minute of any day for five years.

The question now was about rest. Would short breaks, a free mid-morning lunch, or a shorter day make the women produce more? Over the next year and a half the test room tried two five-minute breaks, two ten-minute breaks, six five-minute breaks, a long break with lunch, finishing half an hour early, finishing an hour early, and no work on Saturday mornings. Output went up and up. Then, in September 1928, every one of these things was taken away for twelve weeks and the women went back to a 48-hour week with no breaks at all. Their weekly output was the highest yet.

That was the "startling" result, as the company's officials called it. But inside the room the story was more complicated. The women talked while they worked, and the men running the test kept trying to stop them. In August 1927 four of them were called into the office for laughing and talking "about 75 per cent of the day." When they were told off, they said, "We thought you wanted us to work as we feel." In January 1928 two of the five, the two who talked most and whose output had fallen, were sent back to the main department and replaced by two others. And in September 1928, when the breaks were taken away, the women quietly kept their output down so that the breaks would be given back, until Hibarger promised them the breaks would return whatever happened. One of them, Operator 3, told him: "Give me back the rests and see how my output goes up."

One of the operators, Theresa Layman Zajac, said many years later: "I had no idea there would be so much happening and so many people watching us."

## The story that grew

In 1928 the company called in a professor from the Harvard Business School, Elton Mayo. Mayo decided that the real change in the test room was not the breaks but the people: the women had become a friendly team, with a kind observer instead of a bossy foreman, and that was why they worked harder. He said so in a talk to economists in December 1929 and in a book in 1933. In 1939 two of his colleagues, Fritz Roethlisberger and William Dickson, published a book of 600 pages about the whole programme. Only four of those pages were about the lighting tests, because they had no report to work from, only Snow's short magazine article.

Then the lighting story took on a life of its own. In 1950 a psychologist called John French wrote that the most interesting finding at Hawthorne was "what we might call the 'Hawthorne effect'": people behave differently when they know they are being studied. Textbooks picked up the phrase and improved the story. By the 1980s students were being taught that every time the light was changed, up or down, output rose, and that when the experimenters turned the light back down to where it started, output "perversely" rose again. In 2004 a team read the 21 best-selling textbooks on the psychology of work. Every one of them told the lighting story.

## The sheets found again

Nobody had checked, because everyone believed the data were gone. Then, one at a time, people found them.

In the 1970s two researchers, Richard Franke and James Kaul, borrowed the test room's records from the factory and put them on microfilm in a library in Milwaukee. Their sums, published in 1978, found that the biggest single cause of the rise in output was not friendliness at all. It was the removal of the two talkative women in January 1928 and their replacement by two who worked faster. In 1992 an economist, Stephen Jones, went through the output of every woman for all 270 weeks and found "slender or no evidence" that being studied had made any difference.

In 2009 two more economists, Steven Levitt and John List, followed a clue in Franke and Kaul's paper to a microfiche room in that Milwaukee library, and then to the Baker Library at Harvard, and found the lighting data: the factory's own graphs of output and light, for all three rooms, day by day. They read the numbers off the curves. The first thing they saw was the calendar. Every change of lamps had begun on a Monday, and output was always higher on Mondays than on the Fridays and Saturdays before them, whether or not the lamps had been changed. The famous jump after every change was the jump after every weekend. Output also fell every summer, when the tests stopped, and that fall had been read as the effect of the experiment ending. When they allowed for the days and the seasons, changing the light made no difference they could find. They wrote that the textbook descriptions had proved to be "entirely fictional," and that the real lesson of Hawthorne was "the power of a good story."

In the same year a team at Cornell University opened the boxes Snow had kept, which had come to the university's library in 1980. His draft reports held the tables: every period, every lamp, every foot-candle, and the average output of every group. Run through modern statistics, they showed a small effect of light in the first half of the first test, none in the second test, and in the third test an effect the wrong way round: the dimmer the test group's light, the further it pulled ahead of the control group. The Cornell team thanked Snow, "who disobeyed orders and preserved the history of the illumination experiments."

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**If the people who ran an experiment don't write it up, somebody else will.** Snow's report was never finished because the lighting companies did not like the answer. So the only account of the tests was a short magazine article, and people who had never been there filled the gap with a better story. Once the story was in the textbooks, nobody went back to check it, because everyone thought the real records were gone.

**Look at the days before you look at the lamps.** The lamps were changed on Sundays, so every new test started on a Monday, and Mondays were always good days. For eighty years the Monday jump was read as the workers reacting to the experiment. It was the workers reacting to the weekend. The boring check, a Monday with no change in it, was the one nobody did.

**Being watched does change people, but that is not the same as being the whole answer.** Snow saw in 1925 that measuring the women's work five times a day was itself a kind of supervision, and that it muddled his results. That was a true and useful thing to notice. The textbooks turned it into something much bigger: that attention is all that matters and light does not matter at all. The sheets support the small claim, not the big one.

**A story that explains every result explains nothing.** Output up with more light, up with less light, up with breaks, up without them. If a theory fits all of those, there is no result that could ever prove it wrong, and a theory like that is not a finding, it is a habit.

**The numbers were always there.** The sheet that said 13.4 against 12.3 was in a filing cabinet, then in a carbon copy in a box, then on microfilm in a library. It waited eighty-four years for somebody to divide one number by another.

So the lesson is simple. Before you believe a famous experiment, find out whether anyone has actually looked at its data. Very often, nobody has.
