## The test

On January 27th 1967, three American astronauts climbed into a spacecraft on top of a rocket at Cape Kennedy, in Florida. The rocket was not going to fly that day. There was no fuel in it. The test was a practice run of launch day. The men would sit in the spacecraft, the countdown would run, and at the end the cables that fed the spacecraft from the ground would be unplugged, to prove that it could run on its own power. Everyone called it the plugs-out test.

The spacecraft was called a command module: a cone, about four metres across at the bottom, with three couches side by side inside it. Its number was 012. It was to carry the first Apollo crew into orbit around the Earth a few weeks later, the first step toward landing men on the Moon before 1970, which President Kennedy had promised in 1961.

Before a test began at the Cape, someone had to decide whether it was dangerous. The company that built the spacecraft, North American Aviation, wrote out a list of every test that would be done to it in Florida, and its safety office went through the list and marked the dangerous ones. The rules for marking them were simple. A test was dangerous if the rocket was full of fuel, or if it used the fierce chemicals that burn on contact, or very cold liquids, or tanks under high pressure, or explosives, or a chamber that could be pumped down to a vacuum. There were 104 tests on the list. Fifty-four were marked dangerous. The plugs-out test was not one of them.

## Three men

Gus Grissom, the commander, was forty. He was born in a small town in Indiana, flew a hundred combat missions in Korea, and was one of the first seven American astronauts, chosen in 1959. In 1961 he made the second American flight into space, fifteen minutes up and down. When his capsule splashed into the sea, its hatch blew off early and it sank. In 1965 he commanded the first Gemini flight with two men aboard.

Ed White, the senior pilot, was thirty-six. He came from a military family in Texas, went to West Point, and flew fighter jets in Germany. In June 1965 he became the first American to float outside a spacecraft in orbit, on the end of a line.

Roger Chaffee, the pilot, was thirty-one. He was born in Michigan, studied engineering, and flew photographic planes for the Navy, including flights over Cuba in 1962. He had never been in space. This was to be his first flight.

## Why pure oxygen

Here is the part that matters. In the spacecraft, the men breathed pure oxygen. The air we breathe is only about one-fifth oxygen; the rest is mostly nitrogen. In space, the Apollo cabin was to hold pure oxygen at a low pressure, about a third of the pressure of the air at sea level. That had been decided in 1962, after a lot of tests. Pure oxygen at low pressure was light, simple, and safe for the lungs, and it meant the astronauts could not get the bends, the sickness that divers get when nitrogen bubbles out of their blood.

But on the ground, before launch, the cabin was filled with pure oxygen at a higher pressure than the air outside. Nobody had decided that in the same careful way. It went back to 1960, when a test pilot in a Mercury capsule passed out in a test chamber because nitrogen from the cabin had leaked into his suit. From then on, every American spacecraft was flushed out before launch until it held nothing but oxygen, so that no nitrogen could get into the suits. The pressure was set a little above the air outside so that the air could not leak in and so that the hatch would be pressed tight shut.

Pure oxygen is dangerous around fire. Things that will barely burn in ordinary air burn fiercely in pure oxygen, and the more pressure, the fiercer. NASA knew this. In 1964 it had published a book about fire in space cabins, written by a doctor named Emanuel Roth, which said that a spark in an oxygen cabin "is always a danger" and that once a fire starts in pure oxygen "it proceeds much more vigorously and at higher temperatures." Anyone could buy the book for a dollar.

But nothing had ever caught fire. Six Mercury astronauts and ten Gemini crews had sat on the pad for hours in sealed cabins of oxygen, and nothing had happened. Spacecraft 012 itself had been filled with oxygen above the outside pressure four times already, in a test chamber at the Cape in the autumn of 1966, with crews inside, for more than six hours in all. Those chamber tests had been marked dangerous, because of the chamber. In the rules, the oxygen was not what made them dangerous. The oxygen was just what was always there.

## The hatch

To get out of the spacecraft, the crew had to open three hatches, one behind another. The inner one opened inward, into the cabin. Even when everything went well, opening it and climbing out took at least a minute and a half, and the crew had never managed it that fast in practice. And the inner hatch could not be opened at all if the pressure inside was higher than outside. Even a quarter of a pound per square inch of extra pressure held it shut.

The designers had chosen that hatch on purpose. Grissom's Mercury hatch had blown off by itself in 1961. For a two-week flight to the Moon, they thought a hatch that could blow open by accident in space was a worse risk than a hatch that was slow to open on the ground. For the same reason there was no fire extinguisher in the cabin. The plan was to make sure nothing could ever start a fire, and then a slow hatch would not matter.

The crew had asked for one more thing in this test. They wanted to practise getting out of the spacecraft in a hurry, because the next test, a few weeks later, would be with a rocket full of fuel, and that one really was dangerous. So the escape practice was put at the end of the plugs-out test, which was safe.

## January 27th 1967

The spacecraft was switched on at eight in the morning. The three men climbed in at one in the afternoon, Grissom first. His first words were that his oxygen smelt sour, like buttermilk. The count stopped while a sample was taken, then went on. The hatches were closed and the cabin was flushed with oxygen.

Then the radios went wrong. A microphone at Grissom's seat stuck on and could not be switched off, and the people in the blockhouse near the pad, in the control room miles away, and in the spacecraft could not always hear each other. At twenty past six in the evening the countdown stopped at ten minutes to go, so that the engineers could sort out the radios. The men had been in the spacecraft for more than five hours.

Outside the hatch was a small room called the white room, at the end of a swing arm from a tower. Five men from North American were in it, led by Donald Babbitt, the pad leader. There were no firemen on the pad and no doctors, because the test was not marked dangerous, and there was no plan for a fire inside the spacecraft. The masks in the white room were made for poisonous gas, not for smoke.

## Seventeen seconds

At four seconds and seven tenths past 6:31, a voice from the spacecraft said one word. The tape is not clear. Most people who listened to it thought it was "Hey" or "Fire", and most thought it was Grissom. Two seconds later another voice, probably Chaffee's, said clearly: "Got a fire in the cockpit."

The fire had started low down on the left side of the cabin, beside Grissom's couch, probably from a spark where a wire had been damaged. For a few seconds it was small. Then it ran up the nylon netting that was strung under the equipment to catch dropped screws, across the ceiling, and along the left wall, throwing off drops of burning nylon. In pure oxygen it went like a blowtorch.

Each man had a job in an emergency. Grissom was to open a valve to let the pressure out. White was to reach up behind his head and open the hatch. Chaffee was to keep talking to the ground. The investigators worked out afterwards that White did reach for the hatch; people watching a television picture of the window saw an arm go up, and his harness was still buckled, as the procedure said it should be. But the fire was heating the oxygen, and the pressure inside was rising fast, and the hatch could not open against it. The valve would not have helped either. The investigators calculated that opening it would have delayed what came next by less than one second.

A second message came at seventeen seconds past the minute. Nobody can make out all of it. Some heard "We've got a bad fire, let's get out, we're burning up." It ended in a cry.

At nineteen seconds past 6:31, the cabin burst. The pressure inside had reached at least twice what the hull was built to hold. The wall split open low down on the right, and the hot gas rushed out through the hole, under the couches and over the men, spreading the fire through the whole cabin in about five seconds. Then the oxygen was used up, and the fire turned to thick smoke and poisonous gas. The investigators reckoned that the air inside was deadly by thirty seconds past the minute. The men lost consciousness within a few more seconds. From the first word to the last was seventeen seconds.

## The men in the white room

Babbitt had turned to call the blockhouse when flame came over his head and burned the papers on his desk, four metres from the spacecraft. He and his men ran out onto the swing arm, and then they came back. One found a fire extinguisher. Two of them forced off the outer hatch with a special tool, working by touch, because the smoke was so thick they could see only a few inches. They passed the tool to the next men, who took off the second hatch and unlatched the third. Each man worked until he could not breathe, went out for air, and came back. It took about five minutes to get all three hatches open.

When the inner hatch came free, heat and smoke poured out. One of the men called to the crew. He remembered afterwards "an unusual silence". Another could see the two small lights on the couches, inches away, "but they appeared to be small candles very far away." Babbitt looked in, and then told the blockhouse, on the radio that everyone was listening to, only that he would not describe what he had seen.

The firemen came about nine minutes after the fire began, and none of them had been shown how the hatches worked. The doctors came about fourteen minutes after it began. There was nothing they could do. By the investigators' reckoning, the three men were beyond saving about four minutes after the fire started, before the first hatch was off.

Twenty-seven men on the pad were treated for breathing smoke. Later that year, the five North American men and a NASA inspector who had gone into the white room with them were given a medal for bravery.

## What the Board found

That night NASA set up a Review Board, led by Floyd Thompson, the director of one of its research centres, with an astronaut, Frank Borman, among its members. For two months, 1,500 people took the burned spacecraft apart piece by piece and examined every wire under a magnifying glass for the tiny pit that a spark leaves. They never found the exact spark. They found the conditions instead.

The Board's report, in April 1967, listed six of them: a sealed cabin full of oxygen; combustible material all over the cabin; wiring that could be damaged; pipes carrying a coolant that could burn; no quick way for the crew to get out; and no way for anyone outside to rescue them. The Board wrote that the test conditions "were extremely hazardous", and that the people who planned the test "failed to identify it as being hazardous." It said that the Apollo team, "in its devotion to the many difficult problems of space travel," had not paid enough attention "to certain mundane but equally vital questions of crew safety." Mundane means ordinary.

Then Congress held hearings. A senator asked a North American manager, Dale Myers, whose decision it had been that the test was safe. Myers answered with the number: 104 tests, 54 marked dangerous, under rules that "did not cite full oxygen tests as a hazardous test. And we all are fully aware that in retrospect it should have been." The director of the Kennedy Space Center, Kurt Debus, said: "It is for me very difficult to find out why we did not think deeply enough or were not inventive enough to identify this as a very hazardous test." The Senate's own report said that nobody could explain how hundreds of highly trained people had missed it, and that the only explanation was that years of success had made them overconfident.

## What changed

NASA kept the name Apollo 1, which had been approved before the fire and which the astronauts' widows asked to keep. The nylon in the cabin was replaced with cloth made of glass fibre, which does not burn. The pipes were made of steel instead of aluminium. The wires were covered. The three hatches became one hatch, hinged to swing outward, which a man could open in a few seconds. Firemen, doctors and drills came to the pad, with a wire to slide down to the ground. And a new rule was written: every test in pure oxygen is dangerous. On the pad the cabin was from then on filled with a mixture of oxygen and nitrogen, like air, and the nitrogen was flushed out only after launch.

It took twenty months. The first Apollo crew, Grissom's backup crew, flew in October 1968, in a spacecraft rebuilt from top to bottom. Nine months after that, men walked on the Moon, before 1970, as the President had promised. ## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A list of dangers is really a guess about where danger comes from.** The list at the Cape said danger came from fuel, chemicals, pressure and explosives. It had always been right before. But the list had never been asked about oxygen, so it never said anything about oxygen. Fifty-four out of 104 sounds careful. It is only as good as the questions that made it.

**Doing something safely many times does not prove it is safe.** The oxygen had never caught fire before, in dozens of tests and flights. That proved only that nobody had been unlucky yet. It said nothing about what would happen when someone was, and that was the question that mattered, because the men could not get out.

**Decide how to stop before you start.** The crew wanted to practise escaping, and they put the practice at the end of a test they thought was safe. For five hours they sat in a spacecraft they could not have escaped from in time. The way out should be the first thing checked, not the last thing practised.

**Written warnings do nothing by themselves.** A book about fire in oxygen had been on sale for three years. A general had written that he had no confidence in the company. An inspector had written fifty-seven pages. None of them said "this test will burn," but together they said enough. Nobody's job was to put them side by side before three men were sealed in.

So the lesson is simple. When you decide that something is safe, ask what your rule for "safe" is actually looking at, and then ask what it is not looking at. The thing that hurts you is usually the thing that was always there.
