## The bird that nests in winter

The emperor penguin is the biggest penguin in the world. It lives in the Antarctic, the frozen continent at the bottom of the earth, and for a long time nobody knew where it laid its eggs.

In 1902 a British ship called the *Discovery* was frozen in for the winter near the Antarctic coast, with Captain Scott in command. In October of that year two of her officers found a crowd of emperor penguins on the sea ice at a place called Cape Crozier, under the cliffs where a huge sheet of floating ice, the Great Ice Barrier, meets the land. The penguins had chicks. The ship's doctor, Edward Wilson, who was also a zoologist (a scientist who studies animals) and a painter of birds, went there himself the next year and worked out something strange. The chicks were so big in September that the eggs must have been laid at the beginning of July, which is the middle of the Antarctic winter, when the sun never rises and the cold is worse than anywhere else on earth. The emperor penguin laid its egg in the dark, on the sea ice, and kept it warm on top of its feet, under a flap of skin, for two months.

Wilson wanted something else from those eggs. In 1907 he wrote a report about the birds of the expedition, and in it he explained why. Many scientists then believed that the emperor penguin was the most primitive bird alive: the one closest to the first birds, which had come from reptiles millions of years before. They also believed that an unborn animal, growing inside its egg, goes through the shapes of its ancestors as it grows. So an emperor penguin embryo (a chick in the earliest stage of growing inside the egg) might show the moment when a reptile's scales turned into feathers. To find out, someone would have to collect eggs just after they were laid, in July, in the dark, and that meant walking about a hundred miles across the Barrier in the winter night. Wilson wrote that it "would not be quite impossible."

## Who went

In 1910 Scott went south again, in a ship called the *Terra Nova*, to try to reach the South Pole. Wilson went with him as the head of the scientists. Before they sailed, in an office in London, he said to a young man named Apsley Cherry-Garrard, "I want to go to Cape Crozier in the winter and work out the embryology of the Emperor penguins, but I'm not saying much about it—it might never come off."

Cherry-Garrard was 24. He was so short-sighted that the expedition's doctors had nearly refused to take him, because he "could only see the people across the road as vague blobs walking." In the cold his spectacles froze over and were useless. The third man was Henry Bowers, a lieutenant in the Royal Indian Marine, who looked after the stores. Scott called him "the hardest traveller that ever undertook a Polar journey."

Scott did not want them to go. He walked with Wilson twice that winter and tried to talk him out of it. In the end he agreed, on one condition: that Wilson brought all three of them back unharmed.

## What they took

Bowers weighed everything. Two small sledges, one tied behind the other. Six weeks of food, which was only pemmican (dried meat mixed with fat), biscuit, butter and tea. Six one-gallon tins of oil for the stove. A tent. Three reindeer-skin sleeping bags with eiderdown bags to go inside them. Ice axes, a rope, a pickaxe. A big sheet of green canvas and a plank, to make the roof and door of a hut of stones they planned to build near the penguins. And a wooden box of bottles and chemicals for preserving the embryos. It all weighed about 750 pounds, which was about 250 pounds for each man to pull. When Scott saw the oil tins he said, "Bill, why are you taking all this oil?"

The food was an experiment of its own: each man ate a different mixture of fat, meat and biscuit, to find out what a body needs in extreme cold.

## Into the dark

They left the hut at Cape Evans on June 27th 1911, five days after midwinter. It was pitch dark at midday. Cape Crozier was 67 miles away.

On the second day they climbed up on to the Barrier and met a stream of cold air pouring down off it. The temperature dropped from 26 degrees below zero to 47 below in a few hundred yards. Cherry-Garrard took his hands out of his mitts for a moment to haul the sledges up, and all ten of his fingers were frostbitten. Big blisters came up on every one.

Then the snow changed. In the windless bay of the Barrier the snow is like sand, and the sledges would not move. From June 30th they had to drag one sledge forward, walk back for the other, and drag that one too. For every mile they gained they walked three, in the dark, finding the second sledge by candlelight and steering by the planet Jupiter. On a good day they gained three miles. On a bad day, one and a half.

Everything froze. Their sweat froze inside their clothes, and their breath froze inside their sleeping bags, until clothes and bags were like boards. It took four hours every morning to get from waking up to walking. "It was the darkness that did it," he wrote. Wilson kept them going slowly, and asked every so often, "Shall we go on?" The answer was always yes.

On July 6th, their tenth day, Bowers swung the thermometer at the lunch camp and it read 77 degrees below zero, with Wilson checking it. That is 109 degrees below freezing. Cherry-Garrard called it "the day on which I found out that records are not worth making." They had gained a mile and a half.

Then came fog, and a blizzard, and three days of lying in the tent while the temperature rose to 9 degrees above zero. The ice in their clothes melted and they slept. They also settled their food: Cherry-Garrard, who had been eating mostly biscuit, was always hungry, and he and Wilson agreed on a middle mixture that worked.

On July 15th, after nineteen days, they reached the Knoll, the hill at Cape Crozier, and camped 800 feet up its side. "We had arrived." They spent the next days building a hut of rocks and hard snow, with a sledge across the top for a beam and the green canvas for a roof. By then they had used four of their six tins of oil, and they needed penguin fat for the stove.

## Six eggs

On July 19th they tried to get down to the penguins and failed. The ice between the hill and the sea was piled up in ridges sixty feet high, split with crevasses, and in the dark they could not find a way through. They could hear the penguins calling, but they had to turn back.

On July 20th they tried again, by a different slope. Under the cliffs Wilson found a hole between the ice and the rock just big enough to crawl through. "Well, here goes!" he said, and went in head first. On the far side they came out on to a ledge of ice twelve feet above the frozen sea. Below them stood the penguins. There were only about 100. Nine years before there had been about 2,000.

The light was going. Cherry-Garrard, who could not see, stayed on the ledge holding the rope. Wilson and Bowers jumped down. The penguins shuffled away with their eggs on their feet, and when they were hurried they dropped them. Twice Wilson picked up what he thought was an egg and found it was a lump of ice, rounded and dirty, which a penguin had been keeping warm on its feet because it had no egg. He put a real egg down next to one of these birds, and it dropped the ice and took the egg.

"We collected six eggs," Wilson wrote, "and killed and skinned three birds." Then they climbed back up the ice ledge, which was so hard that Bowers put one leg through a crack into the sea, and ran for the sledge in the dark with two eggs each in their fur mitts. Cherry-Garrard kept falling, and both of the eggs in his mitts broke. When they got back to the hut that night they had three eggs left.

That night the blubber stove spat boiling fat into Wilson's eye. He lay awake all night in pain, thinking he had lost it. "Things must improve," he said next day. "I think we reached bed-rock last night."

## The blizzard

They hadn't. On July 22nd a blizzard came down from the south. At half past six in the morning Bowers put his head out and the tent was gone, poles and all, torn off the ground by the wind.

Then the wind started on the hut, sucking at the canvas roof and dropping it with a crash, over and over. July 23rd was Wilson's 39th birthday. At the main hut, 67 miles away, the expedition's wind gauge that day read 82 miles an hour, the highest it ever recorded. About noon the canvas roof ripped into hundreds of little pieces in a few seconds, and the top rocks of the walls fell in on them. They dived into their sleeping bags. "We're all right," Bowers shouted, and the others said yes, and none of them believed it.

They lay in their bags under the open sky for a day and a half while the snow drifted over them. Wilson and Bowers sang hymns. Cherry-Garrard thought they would die: without a tent, with one tin of oil left and half a cooker, there was no way to get home across the Barrier.

On the morning of July 24th the wind dropped. They cooked a meal with the tent's floor-cloth pulled over their heads, their first food for 48 hours. Then they went out to look for the tent, and Bowers shouted from the top of a ridge. It had gone up into the air shut like an umbrella, and come down in a hollow a quarter of a mile away, almost unharmed. "Our lives had been taken away and given back to us," Cherry-Garrard wrote. "We were so thankful we said nothing."

Bowers wanted one more try at the penguins. Wilson said no. They went home.

## Home

The journey back took eight days. One of them was the worst of all: Bowers fell into a crevasse and hung in his harness in the dark, with his head frozen into a block of ice so that he could not look down, until the other two got a loop of rope down to his foot and pulled him up inch by inch at 46 degrees below zero.

After that they fell asleep as they walked. On July 31st they came down off the Barrier, and on the night of August 1st they pulled up to the door of the hut at Cape Evans without anyone seeing them. The door opened. "Good God! here is the Crozier Party," someone said. They had been away 36 days. Their helmets were frozen on to their heads, and someone suggested a can-opener.

As they packed up for the last time that afternoon, Wilson had said quietly, "I want to thank you two for what you have done. I couldn't have found two better companions—and what is more I never shall."

## What the journey found

Wilson opened the three eggs in the hut and found embryos inside. Scott wrote that the journey had proved when and where the emperor penguin lays its eggs, and that even "if our information remains meagre concerning its embryology," the three men had shown what the Barrier is like in winter. Now there were numbers, written down by Bowers at every camp with a pencil on paper that iced over when he breathed on it. Bowers's figures showed that in winter the Barrier is 20 to 25 degrees colder than the sea ice a few miles away. Scott used the food mixture and the double tent the three men had tested to plan his journey to the South Pole.

Wilson and Bowers went to the Pole with Scott that summer, and died with him on the Barrier on the way back, in March 1912.

## The three eggs

The eggs went home to England in 1913, and Cherry-Garrard, the only one of the three still alive, took them to the Natural History Museum in London. The man at the door said, "This ain't an egg-shop." The chief custodian took the eggs without a word of thanks and told him he needn't wait. Cherry-Garrard said he would like a receipt, and sat in a corridor most of the day until he got one.

The embryos went to a scientist at Cambridge named Richard Assheton, who cut them into thin slices for the microscope, and died in 1915 before he could write a report. They went next to Professor Cossar Ewart in Edinburgh, who studied them and wrote in 1922 that the tiny bumps that become feathers appear before the bumps that become scales, and in different places, so feathers do not seem to come from scales at all. He died in 1933. In September 1934, 23 years after the journey, the Museum published its own report on the embryos. It was ten pages long and cost two shillings. It said that the specimens were badly preserved, that the three embryos were almost exactly the same age, so that they could not show the series of stages that was wanted, that "nothing decisive could be expected" from them, and that feathers and scales are different things from the very beginning.

Later science took the other idea away too. Penguins are not primitive birds. Fossils found in New Zealand in 2006 show that penguins already existed 60 million years ago, and that they came from birds that could fly. Where feathers came from was answered by other fossils: dinosaurs with feathers, dug up in China from 1996 onwards.

The three eggs are still at the Natural History Museum, in its bird collection at Tring, with holes in their shells where the embryos were taken out, and one emperor penguin egg from Scott's expedition is on show in the Museum's Treasures gallery.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Check what you are assuming before you set out.** The whole plan rested on the idea that the emperor penguin was the most primitive bird alive. Nobody could test that with an egg, and when the embryos were studied, the idea underneath turned out to be wrong anyway. A very brave experiment can be built on a guess that nobody has checked.

**Three samples of the same age are not a series.** Wilson wrote in 1907 that he needed "a series of early embryos," at different stages. What came back was three eggs laid within a day or two of each other, chosen in a few minutes of twilight, and the number that survived was the number that did not break in a mitt. The journey was planned for years, and the sample was decided by the dark.

**The useful result may be the one nobody asked for.** The eggs could not answer their question. But Bowers's thermometer readings, taken at every camp, told the expedition exactly how cold the Barrier is in winter, and Scott used that within three months. And that is the hard part of this story: the food and the tent that passed the test in July were the food and the tent that Wilson and Bowers died with in March.

**A result needs someone to read it.** The three eggs waited at the Museum for more than twenty years, while the two scientists who were given them died. Carrying a result home is only half of an experiment; the other half is someone looking at it.

So the lesson is simple. Before you risk everything to measure something, make sure it can answer your question, and make sure someone will be waiting to read the answer when you get back.
