## A disease with four Ds

A hundred years ago a disease called pellagra was killing people all over the southern United States. Doctors called it the disease of the four Ds: dermatitis, which is a rash; diarrhoea; dementia, which means the mind goes; and death. It came back every spring. It filled the hospitals for the insane. In South Carolina alone it killed about 1,200 people in the first ten months of 1915, and the government guessed that across the country there were about 75,000 cases that year and about 7,500 deaths.

Nobody knew what caused it. Most doctors thought it was catching, like typhoid or measles: a germ that passed from a sick person to a well one, perhaps by a fly, perhaps through dirty privies, perhaps through food touched by the sick. A big study paid for by two rich New Yorkers, the Thompson-McFadden Commission, had spent years going house to house in the cotton-mill towns of South Carolina and had decided in 1913 that pellagra was "in all probability a specific infectious disease." Some people thought it came from spoiled corn.

## The man who was sent

In 1914 the United States Public Health Service, the government's disease-fighting service, gave the problem to Joseph Goldberger. He was forty years old. He had been born in Hungary in 1874, had come to New York as a boy of nine, and had grown up above his parents' grocery on the Lower East Side. He trained as a doctor and joined the Service in 1899. He was the kind of doctor who went where the disease was. He had chased yellow fever through Mexico and the South and caught it. He had caught dengue fever in Texas and typhus in Mexico. He had found out that a mysterious itch among poor city people came from a tiny mite living in their straw mattresses. In 1906 he married Mary Farrar, from a rich family in New Orleans.

Goldberger noticed something that other people had seen but had not thought about. In the asylums, which were hospitals for people with mental illness, the patients got pellagra, sometimes after living there for ten or twenty years. The nurses and attendants, who lived in the same wards, ate at the same tables and sometimes slept in the same rooms, never did. At the asylum in Jackson, Mississippi, nearly a hundred patients had died of pellagra in four years. More than a hundred nurses had worked there in that time, and not one had caught it.

If pellagra was catching, he asked, why not the nurses? A germ does not know who is a patient and who is a nurse. The one thing that was different was the food. The nurses got the best of what the kitchen made, and could buy more outside. The patients got what they were given: corn bread, grits, biscuits, syrup, and very little meat, milk or eggs.

So Goldberger had a guess. Pellagra was not caught. It was eaten, or rather, it came from something missing from what people ate. The poor of the South lived on corn meal, pork fat and molasses, and whatever was missing from that was missing all winter.

## Three tests

A guess is not proof. Goldberger set up three tests.

The first was to feed people better and see if pellagra went away. In September 1914 he and his colleagues took over the kitchens of two orphanages in Jackson, Mississippi, where more than two hundred children had had pellagra that summer, and two wards of an asylum in Georgia. The children got a cup of milk twice a day, an egg at breakfast, fresh meat three or four times a week, and beans or peas nearly every day. Nothing else was changed. The crowded rooms, the dirty privies and the water were left exactly as they were. A year later, pellagra had not come back in a single child at one orphanage, and in only one child at the other. At the asylum, none of the seventy-two women on the new food got it again, while nearly half of the women in the ordinary wards did.

The second test was the hard one: to feed healthy people badly and see if they got pellagra. For that he needed people who would agree to be made sick. He went to the Governor of Mississippi, Earl Brewer, in January 1915 and asked for convicts. The Governor would offer a pardon, which means being let out of prison for good, to any man who would eat the diet for six months. Twelve white men from the state prison farm at Rankin, eight miles east of Jackson, said yes. Half of them were murderers serving life. One of them, when the Governor read him the list of food, said: "Gov, I'm eating that diet now. Sure I'll do it."

The third test was to try to catch pellagra on purpose. That one had to wait a year.

## The pellagra squad

The twelve men moved into a small screened cottage at the prison farm in the first week of February 1915, with their own guard, five hundred feet from the building where the other convicts slept. For two and a half months they ate ordinary prison food, so that the doctors could be sure none of them had pellagra already. A young doctor from the Service, George Wheeler, lived at the farm and examined them, stripped, over and over. No one at the farm had ever had pellagra.

At noon on April 19th 1915 the food changed. From then on the men ate biscuits, fried mush, grits, brown gravy, syrup and sweet coffee for breakfast and supper, and corn bread, greens, sweet potatoes, grits or rice and syrup for dinner. There was no milk, no eggs, no beans or peas, and only one meal in six months with any meat. It was the winter diet of a poor Southern family, well cooked and plentiful. Goldberger weighed one week of it and printed the figures: about 256 pounds of food for the squad, about three and a third pounds a man a day, nearly 3,000 calories, which is a working man's ration. The men were not hungry. They did the same work as the other convicts, whitewashing fences and sawing lumber, and had more rest.

The other sixty or so convicts were the control group: the people in an experiment who do not get the treatment, so that you can see what difference the treatment makes. They drank the same water, worked the same fields and used the same privies. Twenty of them were examined as carefully as the squad. If pellagra was something you caught at Rankin, they would catch it too. If it was something you ate, only the squad would.

On July 1st one man was let out of the squad because he was ill with something else. That left eleven. Through the summer they grew weaker and thinner. One of them, Guy James, who was serving life for murder, wrote to the Governor begging to be let out. The Governor said no. Another man said later that he had "been through a thousand hells."

Then, between September 12th and September 24th, five months after the diet began, the rash came. It came first in a place the textbooks did not expect, on the skin of the scrotum, and only later on the backs of the hands of two men and the neck of one. Goldberger brought in the head of the Mississippi State Board of Health, the former head of the state asylum, and two professors of skin disease from Memphis and St Louis to look at the men and make sure it was pellagra and nothing else. Six of the eleven had it. Not one of the other convicts did.

## November 1st 1915

The diet ended on October 31st. The next day Goldberger wrote to the Governor: "The test has been completely successful, inasmuch as not less than six of the men have developed pellagra." He added that the men were all sick and should stay at the farm for two weeks to be fed properly, or they might be badly harmed. That afternoon the eleven men walked into the Governor's office in Jackson, still in prison stripes. A newspaper reporter wrote that they were "all pale, weak and emaciated," that two or three could hardly walk, and that tears ran down nearly every face. Each man got his pardon, a suit of clothes and five dollars. None of them stayed the two weeks. They went home that day, Goldberger wrote, "like scared rabbits."

The *Jackson Daily News* printed Goldberger's letter on its front page that same afternoon under the headline "Dr. Goldberger Produces Pellagra Among Convicts." Within two weeks the government in Washington announced that the cause and the cure of pellagra had been found.

## The doctors say no

A week later the doctors of the South met in Dallas, Texas. Most of them did not believe it. One said he would need "much more evidence." One said that telling people pellagra was not catching was "pernicious," and the room applauded. One pointed out that the rich never got pellagra, which proved nothing about germs, he said, and offered his own rule: the way to prevent pellagra was "having two hundred dollars in the bank." The health officer of South Carolina was angry that he had read the news on a train, in a newspaper, before any doctor had seen it in a medical journal. The companion piece "[What Dallas said](/deeper/eleven-convicts/what-dallas-said/#young)" has what they said.

In March 1916 the laboratory chief of the Thompson-McFadden Commission, W. J. MacNeal, attacked the experiment in the biggest medical journal in America. He said the first report of it had been a newspaper. He said the rash on the scrotum was not the typical rash of pellagra, and that the book Goldberger had quoted actually called it unusual. He said the claim "should be regarded with suspicion." Goldberger answered that "typical" meant the shape and the edges of the rash, not where it was, and that the full report would follow. It did, but not until 1920.

## The filth parties

Goldberger had planned one more test, and now he ran it. If pellagra was catching, the surest way to catch it would be to take into your own body everything that came out of a sick person. In the spring of 1916 he and fifteen of his friends and colleagues did exactly that. Thirteen were doctors. One was his wife, Mary.

On April 25th 1916, at the Service's pellagra hospital in Spartanburg, South Carolina, blood was taken from a woman with pellagra and injected into Goldberger's arm and Wheeler's. A swab from her nose and throat was rubbed into theirs. Three days later Goldberger swallowed a pill of flour mixed with the scraped skin, the urine and the liquid diarrhoea of patients in an asylum. He had a week of diarrhoea himself. On May 7th six people, Mary among them, were injected with blood from three patients; the five men also swallowed the skin scales and a pill of urine and faeces. Mary had the blood injection only. There were seven of these parties in all, between April and June, in four cities. Goldberger was at every one. He said afterwards that they had "feasted on filth," and that if anyone could get pellagra that way, "we three should certainly have it good and hard!" The companion piece "[The seven experiments](/deeper/eleven-convicts/the-seven-experiments/#young)" gives every dose.

Nobody got pellagra. Several had upset stomachs for a week or two. That was all. In November 1916 Goldberger stood up in front of the Southern doctors in Atlanta and told them so. "Of 11 convicts subsisting on a one-sided diet, not less than 6 developed definite evidence of pellagra, while of over 30 controls not one showed any evidence that would justify even a suspicion of the disease."

## What happened next

The proof was in print by 1920. The South did not want it. If pellagra was a germ, it was nobody's fault, and a mill owner could fight it with window screens and better privies. If it came from what poor people ate, then the cure was higher wages, land of their own, and a cow, and that was a bill nobody wanted to pay. In 1921, when the price of cotton collapsed, Goldberger warned that there would be 100,000 cases and 10,000 deaths that year. The warning made the front page of the *New York Times*. Southern congressmen and newspapers called it an insult. One paper said the South would bear its troubles in a "manly and courageous way." The cases came anyway.

Goldberger never found out exactly what was missing from the food. He got close. He found that the disease could be prevented with milk or meat, and then, more cheaply, with a few spoonfuls of dried brewer's yeast, which the Red Cross handed out when the Mississippi River flooded in 1927. He died of cancer in Washington on January 17th 1929, aged fifty-four. In 1937 a chemist named Conrad Elvehjem found the missing thing. It was a vitamin, niacin. There is plenty of it in milk, meat, eggs and beans, and almost none in corn meal, pork fat and syrup. By the 1940s it was being added to flour and bread, and pellagra, which had killed about 100,000 Americans, faded away.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**The answer was on the menu.** Goldberger printed one week of the convicts' food, weighed to the hundredth of a pound, with the calories. That one table answered most of the arguments. The men were well fed, so it was not hunger. The corn was good, so it was not spoiled corn. And the diet was the ordinary diet of the poor South, as one convict told the Governor. The doctors argued about the rash and the newspaper. Nobody argued with the table, because the table said something nobody wanted to be true.

**A control group is the heart of an experiment.** The sixty convicts who ate the ordinary food, drank the same water and used the same privies were as important as the eleven who ate the bad food. Without them, someone could always say the squad had caught pellagra from the farm. With them, that excuse was gone.

**Tell the scientists before the newspapers.** Goldberger wrote to the Governor the day the diet ended, and the letter was on the front page the same afternoon. He had good reasons. People were dying. But the doctors who needed to believe him heard the news from a train, and the first word they heard was "successful," before any of them had seen a patient. They treated the result as a boast instead of a finding, and it took him years to win them back.

**Proving the cause is not the same as curing the disease.** The experiments were right. The South still refused for twenty years, because the cause was poverty and the cure cost money. In the end what beat pellagra was not the proof but a cheap yeast, a vitamin in the flour, and farms that grew food instead of cotton. An experiment can settle a question of fact and still leave the hard part to politics.

**Think about the people in the experiment.** The convicts were called volunteers, but they chose five months of a disease they had seen kill their neighbours over the rest of their lives in prison, and when one of them begged to stop, he was not allowed to. Goldberger himself put the word "volunteers" in quotation marks in a letter to his wife. He was honest with the men about the danger. He could not be honest about the bargain, because the bargain was the point.

So the lesson is simple. When you want to know what causes something, change one thing and keep everything else the same, and keep a group where you change nothing at all. Then write down what you did, in numbers, and show the people who doubt you before you show anyone else.
