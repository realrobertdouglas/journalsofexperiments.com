## The man in the basket

On the morning of August 16th 1960, a United States Air Force pilot named Joseph Kittinger was rising through the sky over the New Mexico desert in an open metal basket hung under a balloon. The basket was about the size of a large barrel, with no roof and a gap in one side to step out of. The balloon above him was made of plastic thinner than a sandwich bag.

He was wearing a pressure suit: a tight laced suit with tubes and air bags built into it, which pump up as the air gets thinner and squeeze the body, so that a person can stay alive where there is almost no air to breathe. The suit had a sealed helmet, and gloves with air bags of their own.

At 43,000 feet, about eight miles up, his right hand began to feel wrong. He looked at it. The air bag in the right glove had not pumped up. Without the pressure, the hand would swell, and keep swelling, and hurt more and more the higher he went.

Down on the ground, at Holloman Air Force Base, a doctor and a team of engineers were listening to his radio and watching him on radar. He had a recorder on his back that wrote down fourteen different measurements about his body and his equipment. None of the fourteen was the glove. The only way the ground could find out about it was if he told them.

He decided not to.

## Who ran it

The experiment was run by a laboratory called the Aero Medical Laboratory, at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Ohio, where the Air Force studied how to keep pilots alive. Its commander was Colonel John Paul Stapp, a doctor who had become famous by riding a rocket sled at more than 600 miles an hour and stopping in about a second, to find out how much a human body could take. His own story is told in the entry [*Eighteen G*](/experiments/2026/10/20/eighteen-g.html).

Joseph Kittinger was born in Tampa, Florida, on July 27th 1928, and grew up in Orlando. He joined the Air Force at twenty, flew jet fighters in Germany, and in 1953 became a test pilot at Holloman Air Force Base in New Mexico, where he met Stapp. In 1957 he made the Air Force's first flight to the edge of space, sealed in a metal capsule under a balloon, to about 96,000 feet. When his oxygen began to run out and the ground told him to come down, he tapped back in Morse code, "Come up and get me." He came down anyway, and landed in a creek.

In 1958 Stapp put Kittinger in charge of a new project called Excelsior, a Latin word meaning "ever upward". The third important person was a civilian named Francis Beaupre, a technician at the laboratory, who had designed the parachute that the whole project was built to test.

## The problem

By the late 1950s, aircraft were flying higher than 60,000 feet, and a rocket plane called the X-15 was being built to go far higher. If a pilot had to bail out up there, nobody knew whether he could get down alive.

The problem was not the parachute. It was the fall before it. High up, the air is so thin and so cold that a parachute opened at once would snap the pilot unconscious or freeze him before he came down. So the pilot had to fall a long way first, to where the air was thick and warm enough, and only then open it. But a person falling through thin air does not fall straight. They tumble, and then they spin, faster and faster, like a propeller. One officer who tried the long fall from 41,000 feet in the 1940s never opened his parachute at all.

So for years the Air Force dropped dummies instead of people: life-sized, dressed like pilots, carrying instruments. Balloons took them up from Holloman and let them go, sixty-seven dummies on forty-three flights between 1954 and 1959. They spun. One, dropped from 89,000 feet, reached 200 turns a minute. A person spinning that fast is unconscious in seconds.

## What they wanted to find out

Beaupre's idea was a parachute that opened in stages. First the pilot would fall for sixteen seconds to pick up speed; then a spring would throw out a tiny parachute, only eighteen inches across. That would pull out a small one, six feet across. The small one would not slow the fall much; its job was to act like the tail on a kite, to hold the pilot feet-down and stop him spinning. Then, much lower, where the air was thick, a device that measured air pressure would let the big main parachute open. Everything happened by clockwork and air pressure, with no cord to pull, because a pilot thrown out of a wrecked aircraft might not be able to pull anything. Behind all of this, on the chest, was a reserve parachute that would open by itself if the rest failed.

The dummies had shown that the idea worked on dummies. The question the experiment was built to answer was really two questions:

1. Would the parachute keep a real person from spinning, all the way from the top of the sky?
2. Could a real person, in an ordinary Air Force pressure suit, survive the climb, the cold and the fall, and do useful work on the way?

A dummy cannot answer the second question. Only a person can. That person was Kittinger.

## What they built

The basket was about four and a half feet across, with sides about four feet high, a seat, an instrument panel, an oxygen tank and a sun shade. A camera was fixed to its frame to film the gap where the pilot would step out. The basket had a parachute of its own, so that it could be cut loose from the balloon after the pilot had gone. The balloons were huge: on the ground they looked like tall thin tubes, and at the top of the climb they swelled into a ball about 170 feet across.

Kittinger wore the standard Air Force pressure suit, with heated gloves and boots and thick winter flying clothes over the top. He carried oxygen bottles, the recorder, a box of instruments and cameras that he sat on, and two parachutes. He weighed about 150 pounds. With everything on, he weighed more than 300.

The flights took place from Holloman, next to the White Sands Missile Range, a hundred miles of empty desert.

## The first jump: two and a half seconds

The first flight, Excelsior I, went up on November 16th 1959. Almost everything that could go wrong on the way up did. The sun glared off the instruments so that he could not read them. His visor fogged. When he tried to stand up to jump, he was stuck: bottles of water he had put under his seat for warmth had frozen and swollen and jammed his instrument box into the seat. It took him about eleven seconds to break free.

By then the balloon was at 76,400 feet, more than three miles higher than planned. Standing in the gap, he pulled the cord that started the parachute's clock. It took three pulls to come free. What he did not know was that the first pull had started the clock.

So the tiny parachute came out only two and a half seconds after he stepped off, long before he was falling fast enough for it to work. It flapped about in the thin air and wrapped itself around his neck. Without it, none of the other parachutes could open. He began to spin. The spin reached about 120 turns a minute, and he blacked out, high in the sky, with the parachute round his neck.

What saved him was the reserve parachute. At 10,000 feet it opened by itself. It tangled too, because he was still spinning, but Beaupre had designed it so that a small cord would break and let it clear itself, and at about 6,000 feet it filled. Kittinger woke up hanging under it, a few thousand feet above the desert, with no memory of the fall.

Four days later the Air Force announced the jump to the newspapers as a world record, the longest parachute jump in history. The story said that his parachute "opened automatically at 10,000 feet". That was true. It did not say which parachute, or that he had been unconscious, or that the parachute being tested had failed. Kittinger told reporters the jump had not scared him because he was "too busy to worry". He said, "This was a research job."

Inside the laboratory the failure was studied carefully. The knobs that started the clock were redesigned so that they could not be mixed up. The visor heater was reset in a colder test chamber. A cardboard shade went round the instruments. The seat was changed so nothing could freeze into it. Kittinger was afraid the senior officers would cancel the project. Stapp told him the second flight was approved. All the fixes took less than a month.

## The second jump

Excelsior II went up on December 11th 1959 and reached 74,700 feet in about an hour. This time everything worked. Fourteen seconds after he stepped off, the tiny parachute fired, the small one followed and held him steady, and at 18,000 feet the main parachute opened. He was on the ground twelve and a half minutes after leaving the basket.

The parachute now had one clean success, from fourteen miles up. But the project's target height was 100,000 feet, nineteen miles, and nobody had yet tested the suit, or the man, that high.

## The third jump: 43,000 feet

Excelsior III was launched at half past five in the morning on August 16th 1960, from a dirt airstrip near the village of Tularosa. The basket carried two signs. One said, "This is the highest step in the world." The other was a cardboard licence plate that his four-year-old son Mark had cut from a cereal box, because he thought the basket ought to have one.

The climb took an hour and a half. At 43,000 feet his right hand began to feel wrong, and he found the glove had not pumped up. He thought it through. The hand was not needed to fly the balloon. If he told the ground, the doctor would almost certainly order him to stop the flight and come down. So he kept climbing and said nothing about it.

The hand swelled. By the time he reached the top it was about twice its normal size and hurt badly. At 50,000 feet he passed through the coldest part of the sky, 94 degrees below zero. At the top, 102,800 feet by the ground radar, the sky above him was black, even though it was morning. He was not yet over the landing area, so he drifted for eleven minutes while he worked through a checklist of forty-six items. He told the ground what he saw: "There is a hostile sky above me."

Then he dropped the radio antenna, as the plan required, and from that moment the ground could not hear him at all. He unplugged his hoses and cables, stood up in the gap, said a prayer, and stepped out. The camera on the basket took a picture as he went.

## The fall

He fell first on his right side, then rolled onto his back and looked up. He said afterwards that it was the most beautiful sight he had ever seen: the bright balloon against the black sky, racing away from him. He could not feel himself falling. There was no wind noise, because there was almost no air, and nothing went past him to show how fast he was going.

After about sixteen seconds the tiny parachute sprang out and pulled out the small one, and the small one held him steady, feet down, exactly as Beaupre had said it would. Near 90,000 feet he reached his top speed, about 614 miles an hour, nearly the speed of sound. Then the air began to thicken and he slowed. Hanging under the small parachute he experimented, and found that by pointing one foot out a little he could turn himself to face Texas, and then turn back to face New Mexico.

At 21,000 feet he fell into a solid layer of cloud. He had never fallen through cloud before, and he pulled his feet up, the way you do when you think you are about to hit something. Just below the cloud, four minutes and thirty-eight seconds after he had stepped off, the main parachute opened. He said, "Thank you, Lord." He came out of the cloud with two helicopters circling him, and landed hard on the desert thirteen minutes and forty-five seconds after leaving the basket. "I'm very glad to be back with you all," he told the crew. Then they took off his helmet and his suit, and saw the hand.

## What happened next

The hand went back to normal in about three hours, with no lasting harm. The Air Force's numbers took longer to settle: on the first day it said he had fallen at about 450 miles an hour, and two days later 614. Some people have since claimed he broke the speed of sound. Kittinger himself always said he did not.

On October 3rd 1960 President Eisenhower gave him the Harmon Trophy at the White House, with his wife Pauline and his two small sons in the room. His seven-year-old son Joe told a reporter, "I want to go higher than he did."

Beaupre's parachute went into service. Kittinger said in 2014 that the small steadying parachute "is still in use today; every ejection seat has it".

Kittinger went back to flying fighters, was shot down over Vietnam in 1972 and spent eleven months as a prisoner. In 1984 he became the first person to fly a balloon alone across the Atlantic. In 2012 he sat at the radio and talked a skydiver named Felix Baumgartner through a jump from about 128,000 feet, which broke his height and speed records, but not his record for the longest free fall. He died in 2022, aged ninety-four.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**An experiment can have two questions in it, and answer them differently.** The first question was about a parachute, and the parachute answered it: three jumps from three heights, with a camera and a recorder to prove it. The second question was about a man, and only the man could answer it. When part of his suit failed, the only instrument that could report it was him.

**When the person being measured can switch off the measurement, you are not measuring what you think.** The people on the ground had the power to stop the flight if anything went wrong. Kittinger knew that. So when something went wrong, he did not tell them. The fourteen channels of the recorder all said the system was working. The experiment was supposed to find out whether a man could survive up there. What it really found out was that a man will keep quiet about a failure if telling the truth would end the test.

**Being right does not make the problem go away.** Kittinger was right. The hand recovered, the jump worked, and the project proved what it set out to prove. It was brave. But a design that depends on a person deciding which of their failures to report has a hole in it, and the hole is there even when the person chooses well.

**Look at what is not being said.** When the first jump nearly killed him, the Air Force's announcement said the parachute had "opened automatically at 10,000 feet". It did not say which parachute. The project kept quiet about its failures because it feared being shut down. Then the man in the basket kept quiet about his, for the same reason. When you read a report that only tells you what worked, ask what the people writing it were afraid of.

So the lesson is simple. If someone's own body is your instrument, and they know you will stop the experiment on bad news, do not be surprised when the bad news does not arrive. Build a way for the instrument to report that does not depend on its courage.
