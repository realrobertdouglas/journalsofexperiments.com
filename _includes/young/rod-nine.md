## A reactor for the Arctic

In the 1950s the United States Army built a line of radar stations across the far north of Canada and Alaska, to watch for Soviet bombers coming over the Pole. Every station needed heat and electricity, and every drop of the diesel oil that made them had to be flown or shipped in across the ice. In 1955 it asked the Atomic Energy Commission, the government body in charge of all things nuclear, for a small nuclear reactor: a reactor is a machine that makes heat by splitting uranium atoms. This one had to be small enough to fly to a radar station in pieces, simple enough for soldiers to put together, and able to run for three years without new fuel.

The reactor was designed by Argonne National Laboratory near Chicago and built in the desert of eastern Idaho, at the National Reactor Testing Station, a huge piece of government land where a dozen other reactors were being tried out. It was called SL-1, for Stationary Low-Power Reactor Number One. It made its first heat in August 1958 and its first electricity that October. From February 1959 it was run by a company called Combustion Engineering, which had a few engineers at the site. But the people who actually worked the reactor, day and night, were soldiers, sailors and airmen, trained for a few months and sent to Idaho in teams.

That was the experiment. The Army wanted to find out whether a nuclear reactor could be run like a diesel generator, by enlisted men on their own, with no scientist in the building. To make the test real, the night shifts had no engineer from the company watching over them. There was an unwritten agreement about that, and in November 1960 the company asked the Commission to put it in writing.

## How it worked, and the one rod

SL-1 stood inside a round steel building forty-eight feet high. The reactor itself was a steel tank, about four and a half feet across and fourteen feet high, filled with water. At the bottom of the tank sat the core: forty bundles of thin metal plates with uranium inside them. When it ran, the water boiled and the steam turned a turbine to make electricity.

A reactor is controlled with rods. SL-1 had five of them, cross-shaped sheets of a metal called cadmium, which soaks up the tiny particles called neutrons that keep the atoms splitting. With the rods pushed all the way down into the core, the reactor was off. Pull them up, and it came on. The rods were numbered 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9, and rod 9 was in the middle of the core. Electric motors moved them very slowly.

Because the reactor had to run for three years without new fuel, it was loaded with much more uranium than it needed, and the rods had to hold all of that down. The middle rod, rod 9, held down more than any other. In the very first tests in August 1958 the scientists measured something: with the four outer rods all the way down, pulling rod 9 up by itself, about fourteen inches, was enough to turn the reactor on. Everyone who ran SL-1 knew this. One of the soldiers said later that they often talked about what they would do if the Russians came to their radar station: "We'd yank it out."

To hold the extra uranium down, the designers had also fixed thin strips of a metal called boron to the fuel bundles. Boron soaks up neutrons too, and it was meant to burn away slowly over the years as the uranium was used up. But the strips bent and flaked off. In August 1960 an inspection found big pieces of them missing, and flakes of boron lying in the bottom of the tank. Pulling out the fuel bundles to look at them seemed to make things worse, so the inspections were stopped, and the reactor kept running. With less boron, the rods had to work harder, and in November 1960 the company slid extra strips of cadmium into the core to help.

The rods were giving trouble too. Sometimes, when a rod should have dropped down to shut the reactor off, it stuck. In the first twenty-two months, about one drop test in forty went wrong. In the last month before Christmas 1960, about one in eight did. On the morning of December 23rd 1960, when the reactor was shut down for the holidays, the log said: rod 1, no drop; rod 3, dropped half an inch and stuck; rod 7, no drop. Rod 9, the middle one, was the best behaved of all. The company's managers told the investigators later that they had not read these entries in the log.

## The night of January 3rd

Over the holidays the crews did maintenance. To put new measuring wires into the core, they had to take the motors and their housings off the top of the rods, and take some of the heavy plugs out of the lid of the tank. On January 3rd 1961 the day shift finished that job. The night shift's job was to put everything back together so the reactor could start up the next day.

The night shift was three men. Richard Legg, a Navy electrician aged twenty-six from Michigan, and John Byrnes, an Army specialist aged twenty-two from New York, were both qualified reactor operators. Richard McKinley, an Army specialist aged twenty-seven from Ohio, was a trainee, due to qualify the next month. Byrnes and McKinley lived in Idaho Falls, forty miles away, with their wives. It was a bitter night, heading for seventeen degrees below zero. They came on at four in the afternoon, and the gate was locked behind them.

To reconnect a rod to its motor, a man had to stand on the lid of the tank, screw a lifting tool onto the top of the rod, and lift the rod a little, just a few inches, so that a washer and nut could be fitted. The written procedure said to lift it "not more than 4 inches." It did not say why. The reason was that rod 9 only had to come up about sixteen inches to turn the reactor on, and nobody was supposed to get anywhere near that. No instruments were switched on. Nobody was in the control room.

At about nine o'clock, rod 9 came up about twenty inches.

Nobody knows why. What happened next is known exactly, from the wreckage, and it took less than a second. The reactor turned on. In four thousandths of a second, its power rose to about 19,000 megawatts, six thousand times what it was built for. The metal plates in the middle of the core got so hot they boiled, the water around them flashed into steam, and the steam shut the reaction down again. But the steam also shoved the seven feet of water above the core upward like a hammer. The water hit the lid of the tank at a hundred and sixty feet a second, with a force of ten thousand pounds on every square inch. The heavy plugs in the lid shot out like bullets. The whole tank, weighing many tons, jumped nine feet into the air, tore every pipe off itself, hit the ceiling, and fell back. From the first pull on the rod to the tank landing again took between two and four seconds.

Two men were standing on the lid. One was thrown against a concrete block and killed. The other was carried up by one of the plugs and pinned to the ceiling, thirteen feet above the reactor, and killed. The third man was standing a little way off; he was thrown back against another block and hit his head. Water from the tank, full of the broken core, sprayed over the whole room.

## The rescue

At 9:01 p.m. a heat alarm from the reactor building went off at the fire stations. Nine minutes later a fire engine was at the locked gate. The firemen found no fire and no one. In the support building next door they found three lunch pails on a table. At the bottom of the stairs up to the reactor floor, their radiation meter went off the top of its scale.

Radiation is invisible; the men carried meters to measure it. On the reactor floor there was enough to kill a person in an hour or two. At 10:35 two of the company's supervisors went up the stairs in breathing masks and looked for less than two minutes. They saw two men. One of them was moving. They carried the live man out in three minutes. He died in an ambulance in the desert. At 11:14 p.m. a doctor said so. Another team found the third man, dead, and left him where he was. The rescuers had each taken a dose of radiation that a worker was normally allowed over about five years.

The dead man in the ambulance was so radioactive that there was nowhere to put him. At four in the morning, by the headlights of two cars, at twenty below zero, a team cut his frozen clothes off him. They were allowed one minute; they took a minute and seventeen seconds. The second man was brought down the next evening by soldiers who ran in two at a time, with a stopwatch, for one minute each. The third man was the hardest. He was on the ceiling above the open reactor, and anything that fell from up there might drop into the core. Soldiers from an Army unit in Utah practised on a full-size copy of the reactor top, rigged a net on a crane under the body, and freed it in relays, no two men inside for more than sixty-five seconds. The companion piece "[Sixty-five seconds](/deeper/rod-nine/sixty-five-seconds/#young)" tells the whole six days. The last man was brought out at 2:37 in the morning of January 9th 1961.

The three bodies were buried in coffins lined with lead, to stop the radiation, and were flown to their families. McKinley was buried at Arlington National Cemetery, near Washington, on January 25th 1961. They were the first people ever killed by a reactor in the United States.

## Finding out what happened

The Atomic Energy Commission set up a board to investigate. At first the Commission thought it might have been a chemical explosion. Then scientists found that a screw in one man's cigarette lighter, the buckle of another man's watch, and a ring on the third had all become radioactive in a way that only neutrons can cause. The reactor had turned on.

The board's report came out in June 1961. It said the most likely cause was that someone had moved the middle rod "unusually rapid and extensive" distance, perhaps because it had stuck and then come free suddenly while a man was pulling on it. But it was honest. "We cannot say, however, with any certainty, what initiated the SL-1 explosion, and it is possible that we may never know." It blamed the company for letting the reactor run in such poor condition, and it blamed the Commission's own offices, all the way up, for not checking. It did not blame the three men. The companion piece "[The rod log](/deeper/rod-nine/the-rod-log/#young)" goes through everything the reactor's own records had been saying for two years.

A Commissioner told Congress that "it is all too clear that the accident could and should have been prevented." The first thing on his list was the design: a reactor that could be turned on by pulling a single rod.

The real proof came from the wreck, and the companion piece "[Twenty-six and a half inches](/deeper/rod-nine/twenty-six-and-a-half-inches/#young)" tells how it was found. In 1961 and 1962 the General Electric company lifted the tank out with a crane and took it to pieces in a shielded workshop forty miles away. The part that mattered was the mechanism of rod 9. When the water hammer hit the lid, the tube around the rod had been crushed onto it so hard that it took two and a half tons of force to move it in a testing machine. Inside, the rod and the tube had matching scratches. The scratches showed exactly where the rod had been: about twenty inches up. It was the only rod of the five that was not all the way down.

Then the scientists did a simple experiment. They hung a spare rod from a real rod mechanism in a pool of water, gave several men the very lifting tool from the accident, and timed them. A man pulling as hard as he could raised the rod from the danger point to twenty inches in about one twentieth of a second. A man pulling casually took a little longer. A computer showed that the pull which best matched the explosion was slower than even a casual pull. In other words, nobody had to do anything remarkable. Any ordinary lift that went too far would have done it. As to why the rod went up twenty inches instead of two, General Electric's report said only that it had made no attempt to guess.

## What came after

The building was cut up and buried in pits near the site, behind a fence with warning signs. The Commission checked every other reactor in the country, and a new rule was written that still stands today: a reactor must never be built so that one rod on its own, pulled out or stuck, can turn it on. In 1962, at another reactor on the same site, scientists blew up a core on purpose, with everyone standing far away, to measure what SL-1 had done. The Army went on with its small reactors for a few more years and then gave up on them in 1965. The radar stations kept burning oil.

People have argued about the three men ever since. Did the rod stick and come loose? Was it a mistake, a joke, or something worse? The reports leave that space empty, and nobody has been able to fill it with evidence. The site's own historian wrote that all the science at the station "was unequal to this most perplexing question."

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**If one hand can start the machine, one hand will.** SL-1 was built so that pulling a single rod would turn it on. Everybody knew it, and everybody lived with it, because the rule said to lift the rod only four inches. A rule is not a safeguard. The safeguard that came afterwards was a design rule: build the reactor so that one rod can never do it, however far it goes. That is the same lesson as [The Demon Core](/experiments/2026/10/19/the-demon-core.html), where a scientist held two halves of a bomb core apart with a screwdriver until the day it slipped.

**Say why.** The procedure said "not more than 4 inches" and gave no reason. If the paper had said "because at sixteen inches the reactor turns on," every man on that lid would have known what the four inches were for.

**Old trouble can hide new trouble.** The rods had stuck a little from the beginning, and everyone had a fix for it. When the sticking got much worse in the last month, it looked like the same old problem. The boron flaking off looked like a problem for later. Each thing was written in a log. Nobody's job was to add them up.

**An empty control room hears nothing.** The Army wanted to prove that soldiers could run a reactor alone at night, so there was no engineer, no one at the instruments, and the instruments were off. The first sign of trouble was a heat alarm, after it was over.

**Be honest about what you do not know.** The investigators proved where the rod was, and proved that a man could have put it there. They could not prove why, and they said so in every report. The stories told since have more certainty and less evidence.

So the lesson is simple. Do not build a thing that one slip can destroy and then rely on nobody slipping. Build it so that the slip does not matter, and tell the people who work on it why the rules are what they are.
