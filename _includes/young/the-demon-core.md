## The drawing

On the afternoon of May 21st 1946, in a small laboratory in a canyon below Los Alamos, New Mexico, a guard named Patrick Cleary saw a blue light and felt a wave of heat. He ran out of the door, down a ramp, through a gate and about 1,000 feet up a hill. Then one of the men called everyone back down to the building. "As soon as we got there," Cleary wrote later, "we drew a diagram to figure out approximately where everyone was when it happened. The only other conversation was that everyone was wondering who had gotten most of the radiation."

The drawing still exists. It shows a table with a ball of plutonium on it, and eight dots with names and distances. Louis Slotin was a foot and a half away. Alvin Graves was three feet away. The others were six, eight and sixteen feet away. That drawing was the best measuring instrument anyone had. Slotin died nine days later.

## Who they were

Louis Slotin was born in Winnipeg, Canada, in 1910. He was a very good student, a boxer, and a man who liked to let people believe exciting stories about him that were not true. He went to university at sixteen, got a doctorate in London, and then went to Chicago to help build a machine for studying atoms. In 1942 he joined the secret project to build the atomic bomb. One of his colleagues remembered that when Slotin wanted to fix an experiment at the bottom of a tank of water that shielded a nuclear reactor, and was told to wait until the weekend, he took off his clothes and dived in. "He was like a cowboy," the colleague said, "but a good experimental scientist."

In December 1944 Slotin went to Los Alamos, the laboratory in New Mexico where the bomb was being designed. In July 1945 he was the man who put together the plutonium core of the first atomic bomb ever tested. People called him the "Chief Armorer of the United States." After the war he wanted to go back to Chicago and study living things, but the Navy wanted him for more bomb tests at Bikini, in the Pacific. "I am one of the few people left here who are experienced bomb putter-togetherers," he wrote to a friend in March 1946.

Harry Daghlian was born in Connecticut in 1921. He was twenty-four. He had come to Los Alamos in 1944 to do the same kind of work as Slotin, and he helped prepare the plutonium for the first test.

## What they were trying to find out

A ball of plutonium the size of a bomb core does not explode by itself. Too many of the tiny particles called neutrons, which carry the chain reaction from one atom to the next, escape out of the surface. But if you put something around the ball that bounces neutrons back in, a tamper, more of them stay, and at some point the chain reaction keeps itself going. That point is called critical. The scientists' job was to find exactly where it was, for each core and each tamper. Every core that went into a bomb had to be checked first.

They did this by hand. They put a tamper around the ball a little at a time and listened to counters that clicked faster as the reaction grew. A colleague named Raemer Schreiber said: "The counters go up; you move something off and the counters goes down. It's a tantalizing sort of business. But if you go past critical – I don't know what the multiplication rate is, but it's far faster than human reactions."

Here is the real experiment in this story. The work could have been done by a machine, with the people far away. The scientists chose their own hands instead. They were afraid a machine might fail, and they believed that a careful person standing right next to the assembly could do it safely. Hundreds of times, they had. The Army's own history of the laboratory warned that these experiments "seem so safe when properly carried out that they lead to a feeling of overconfidence on the part of the experimenter."

## The core

The ball of plutonium was the third one ever made. The first was exploded in the test in July 1945 and the second over Nagasaki, in Japan, in August 1945. The third was cast in a single night for a third bomb, in case Japan did not surrender. Japan surrendered on August 15th 1945, and the core stayed at Los Alamos. It weighed 6.2 kilograms and was about nine centimetres across, small enough to hold in one hand, in two halves plated with nickel.

## Tuesday the 21st, 1945

On August 21st 1945, Harry Daghlian was building a tamper around the core out of heavy bricks of tungsten carbide, each weighing 4.4 kilograms. He had done two experiments that day. The next one was planned for the following morning. Instead, after dinner, he went back to the laboratory alone, which was against the rules. The only other person in the building was a guard, sitting at a desk about ten feet away with his back turned, reading a newspaper.

At five minutes to ten at night Daghlian had four layers of bricks in place and was holding the last brick over the top when the counters told him it would push the assembly past critical. He pulled his hand back. The brick slipped and fell onto the middle of the stack. There was a blue glow. He pushed the brick off with his right hand, and then he took the stack apart by hand, staying close to it for at least ten minutes. His hands were swollen and numb by the time he reached the hospital. He died on September 15th 1945.

The man who investigated the accident was Louis Slotin. He talked to Daghlian the next day. He asked for photographs of the stack to be taken. His name is on the report. A famous physicist, Enrico Fermi, is said to have told Slotin that he would not last a year if he kept doing that experiment. Nobody has ever found that warning written down.

## Tuesday the 21st, 1946

Nine months later Slotin was about to leave for the bomb tests at Bikini, and Alvin Graves was going to take over his job. On the afternoon of May 21st 1946, Graves asked Slotin to show him the experiment. No experiment had been planned for that day. There were eight men in the one-room laboratory.

Slotin's tamper this time was two bowl-shaped shells of a metal called beryllium. The bottom shell sat on a table with the plutonium ball inside it. The top shell had a hole at the top. Slotin put his left thumb in the hole, lifted the top shell, took out the little metal spacers that were supposed to hold the two shells apart, and let the top shell down until one edge touched. Under the other edge he held the blade of a flat screwdriver. He worked the screwdriver out, bit by bit, letting the gap close, and watched the counters.

At about twenty past three the gap closed all the way. The official reports say the screwdriver slipped. The room filled with a blue glow and a wave of heat. Slotin flipped the top shell off with the hand that was in it. "Went clattering on the floor," Schreiber remembered. "Everything went off scale, there was a blue flash, etc. He knew he had had it at that time. He kept his head." The chain reaction had lasted less than half a second.

Outside, Slotin set about measuring what had happened. The men drew the diagram. He had them empty the coins from their pockets, because silver that has been hit by neutrons becomes slightly radioactive, and he held a counter to the fillings in their teeth. The film badges that were supposed to measure radiation were in a lead box in another room, about a hundred feet away. Slotin asked for them to be put on the assembly. Schreiber did it, at great risk, and the badges recorded almost nothing useful, because the neutrons had already gone. Then they drove to the hospital.

## In the hospital

Slotin told Graves: "I'm sorry I got you into this. I'm afraid I have less than a 50 per cent chance of living. I hope you have better than that."

For the first few days his hands hurt and he felt sick, but he could talk and hope. His parents were flown in from Winnipeg on an Army plane. On about the fourth day his stomach and intestines stopped working. His fever rose. The white cells in his blood, which fight infection, almost disappeared. On the eighth day he became delirious. He died at 11 in the morning on May 30th 1946. His friend Philip Morrison, a physicist who helped work out the doses, wrote to friends: "It was a pure and simple case of death from radiation." By a mistake, Slotin had been put in the same hospital room as Daghlian. "They died in the same bed," Morrison wrote.

The physicists measured the dose from the men themselves. Neutrons passing through a body turn a little of the sodium in the blood radioactive, and that can be counted. Morrison's first answer for Slotin was about 800 units, give or take 200. Graves, at three feet, had taken about 200. The other six had taken less than 100 each.

## What happened next

About 3,000 people came to Slotin's funeral in Winnipeg on June 2nd 1946. The general in charge of the bomb project wrote that his "bravery and quick action" had saved seven lives. Not everyone agreed. One physicist wrote privately that "the whole affair was a scandal," and that Slotin himself had insisted that automatic safeguards were not needed.

For a long time people said the core was exploded in the Bikini tests that summer. It was not. In the summer of 1946 it was melted down and the plutonium was made into a new core. Only later did people start calling it the "demon core."

Graves recovered, though he was weak for six months and lost his hair. He went on to direct America's bomb tests in Nevada. He died of a heart attack in 1965. One of the men in the room, Marion Cieslicki, died of leukaemia, a cancer of the blood, nineteen years later, and so did Daghlian's guard. Doctors followed them all for thirty years and said the numbers were too small to prove what caused what.

## The end of hands-on work

After Slotin's death the laboratory stopped all experiments of this kind done by hand. It said no machine could think as well as a trained man, but a machine could only do what it was told, and could be fitted with safety devices. Schreiber took over Slotin's group and wrote the safety rules. One of his ideas was this: when a person gets so used to a dangerous job that he loses respect for it, he should be moved to a different job.

The group built a new laboratory a quarter of a mile up the canyon from the control room, with cables running to it and early television cameras so the scientists could watch from far away. The design rule was simple: the moving part of any assembly came up from below, so if anything slipped, the pieces fell apart instead of together. In April 1947 one of the first things they did was to repeat Slotin's experiment by remote control. It did not go critical. Nobody else at Los Alamos was ever killed doing a critical assembly.

The number for Slotin's dose kept changing as the scientists learned more. In 1948 it was 386. In 1967 it was 1,000. A review in the year 2000 gave 2,100. The number changed by five times over thirty years. The order of the men in the drawing never changed.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**A procedure that depends on one steady hand has only one way to fail.** Both accidents happened because something fell down onto the core: a brick, then a shell. Schreiber saw the answer straight away. Hold the top still and raise the bottom, and then if something slips, the pieces fall apart and the reaction stops. The laboratory built that rule into its machines a year later. Nothing about the physics had changed. What changed was that the design decided which way a slip would go.

**A warning you write down and file is not a fix.** After Daghlian died there was a report with Slotin's name on it, photographs, and a repeat of the experiment to measure the dose. None of it changed the way the experiment was done, because the person who wrote the report was the person doing the experiment. Being very careful is not the same as being safe.

**When you know a job so well that you stop respecting it, it is time to change jobs.** That was Schreiber's rule. Slotin had done this experiment many times. The scientists had done hundreds of them without harm. That is exactly when the danger is greatest, because it no longer feels like danger.

**A quick rough measurement can be worth more than a slow exact one.** The proper instruments, the film badges, were in a lead box in another room. A drawing on the floor, some coins and a counter told the doctors within the hour who had taken the most radiation, and that was what they needed to know. The exact number took thirty years and changed by five times. The order never changed.

So the lesson is simple. Do not build an experiment that relies on nobody ever slipping. Build it so that when someone slips, the pieces fall apart, not together.
