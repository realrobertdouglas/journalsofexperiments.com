## The bridge

The Tacoma Narrows is a strip of sea in the state of Washington, in the north-west corner of the United States. It is about a mile wide, very deep, and the tide runs through it like a river. For years the people of Tacoma wanted a bridge across it, so that they would not have to take a ferry to the land on the other side.

In 1938 the federal government agreed to help pay. The state's own bridge engineer, Clark Eldridge, had already drawn a bridge. It had a roadway hung from two great cables, like the Golden Gate Bridge, and along each edge of the roadway ran a stiffening truss: a wall of criss-cross steel 25 feet deep, to keep the deck from bending and swaying. It would have cost about eleven million dollars.

The government would not pay for that. It said the state must hire a famous outside expert, and the state hired Leon Moisseiff of New York. Moisseiff had helped design nearly every big suspension bridge in America. He believed that a long bridge did not need deep trusses, because the weight of the deck and the pull of the cables did most of the stiffening on their own. So he redrew the bridge with the trusses replaced by plain steel plates, called plate girders, only 8 feet deep. That saved thousands of tons of steel and nearly five million dollars. The new bridge would cost six million four hundred thousand dollars.

It would also be the narrowest big bridge ever built. The main span, the part between the towers, was 2,800 feet long, and the deck was only 39 feet wide: two lanes of traffic and two narrow sidewalks. The width was one seventy-second of the length. The Golden Gate Bridge, the longest in the world, was one forty-seventh. Nobody had ever built a bridge as thin as this.

## The warnings

Before the bridge was built, three sets of engineers looked at the plans. All three noticed how narrow it was. All three decided it would be all right, because Moisseiff was such a famous engineer.

One of them, T. L. Condron, who worked for the government lender, came closest to saying no. He wrote in September 1938 that he hesitated to criticise Moisseiff, but that the narrowness of the bridge was "open to criticism, particularly, since it was without precedent," which means that nothing like it had ever been tried. He suggested making the bridge 52 feet wide instead of 39. Then he wrote that the design was probably sound anyway, and the bridge was built as drawn.

## Galloping Gertie

The bridge was built in nineteen months, and the building was a triumph. The piers went down through 140 feet of rushing water, deeper than any before. The towers rose 420 feet above them. The two cables, each as thick as a man's body, were spun from fourteen thousand miles of wire. The bridge opened on July 1st 1940, the third longest suspension bridge in the world.

But while the men were still pouring the concrete roadway, in May 1940, the deck began to move. In a light wind it rose and fell in slow waves, a few feet high, rolling along the bridge like the swell on the sea. The workmen felt seasick. Drivers crossing it saw the car in front sink out of sight and rise again. People gave the bridge a nickname: Galloping Gertie.

The engineers had half expected this. Another bridge by Moisseiff, in New York, had done the same thing in a small way. So before the bridge opened they fitted two cures. They tied the cables to the deck at the middle with diagonal cables, so the deck could not slide about underneath. And they put big oil-filled shock absorbers at the towers. Neither made any difference.

## The measuring

So the state paid the University of Washington to find out what was wrong. A professor named F. B. Farquharson built a model of the whole bridge, 50 feet long, with every part made to the right stiffness and the right weight. Along it were a hundred electromagnets that could push and pull the deck in time, to stand in for the wind. On the model he could make the same waves the real bridge made, and try cures on it.

And the real bridge was measured too. From August 1st 1940 a surveyor's telescope, called a transit, stood on the roof of the toll house, pointed at nine targets fixed to the lamp posts along one side of the bridge. Every time the deck moved, a man read how high each target rose and fell, how fast the waves came, and how hard the wind blew. In four months they wrote down about 250 readings.

The readings told them strange things. The deck could rise and fall four feet in a wind of only three or four miles an hour, and then lie perfectly still in a wind ten times stronger. Seven different kinds of wave were counted, with one, two, three, four or more still points along the deck. By November the government's engineer on the job, L. R. Durkee, could write a careful list of everything they knew. The waves were sometimes five feet from top to bottom. They did not get bigger when the wind got stronger. And in four months, no damage of any kind had been found.

Every one of those 250 readings was the same kind of movement: both edges of the deck going up together and coming down together. The deck never twisted. That was not something anyone decided. It was simply what the bridge did, and so it was all the targets ever showed.

The cures followed the readings. In October the engineers tied the side spans, the parts of the bridge between the towers and the shore, down to concrete blocks and wooden piles. That quieted the side spans, but not the main span. Then, about five weeks before the end, Farquharson tried something new. He built a short piece of the deck at one twentieth of its real size and put it in a wind tunnel, a machine that blows a steady wind over a model. The wind tunnel showed that the shape of the deck itself was the trouble. A solid steel wall 8 feet high on each edge caught the wind like a sail, and when the wind came at a slight angle, the deck fed on the wind and made its own waves. The cure was simple: fit curved wooden vanes along the outside of the girders, to smooth the wind round them. The vanes made the model deck behave.

Farquharson finished his report on November 2nd. On Wednesday November 6th the engineers met and agreed to fit the vanes to the south side of the bridge at once. Eldridge sat down on Thursday morning to draw them.

## November 7th 1940

That morning the wind blew hard up the Narrows from the south. At half past seven a surveyor measured it at 38 miles an hour. By about half past nine it was 42. The bridge was doing its usual gallop, 36 waves a minute, each about three feet high. Eldridge drove across at half past eight and thought it was moving less than on many other days. Farquharson arrived from Seattle with two cameras, to film the bridge for his records. A Coast Guard ship passed underneath, and her captain noticed that one of the big cables was sliding back and forth, a yard or two at a time, through the steel band that held it at the middle of the bridge.

Just after ten o'clock, the surveyor at the transit saw the targets at the middle of the bridge slide out of his view. Farquharson, standing on the roadway, saw it too. He wrote that day that the change came "without any intermediate stages and with such extreme violence that the span appeared to be about to roll completely over."

The bridge had started to twist. Instead of both edges going up and down together, one edge went up as the other went down. The deck tilted 45 degrees one way, then 45 degrees the other, fourteen times a minute. At the quarter points the cables moved through 28 feet. Lamp posts snapped off. Chunks of concrete burst out of the sidewalks. This was the motion the targets had never once shown in four months.

There were still people on the bridge. A newspaper editor, Leonard Coatsworth, had just driven on with his daughter's dog, Tubby, a black cocker spaniel, in the back seat. The road tilted and threw his car against the kerb. He climbed out of a window, tried to go back for the dog, was thrown down again, and crawled on his hands and knees 500 yards to the shore. A student named Winfield Brown had paid ten cents to walk across for fun. He was flung over the railing again and again, and when he reached the toll gate he remembered that he owed another ten cents for the way back. "He said, 'Skip it'," Brown told the newspaper. A delivery van tipped over near the far tower just after the two people in it had jumped out; two painters backed a truck out onto the bridge and rescued them.

Farquharson stayed. He stood by the tower and filmed. During a lull he walked out to Coatsworth's car to try to save the dog. "The animal, sick and terrified, bit me on a knuckle," he said afterwards. He had to leave it. Eldridge came out onto the bridge, saw it "twisting about a neutral point at the center of the span in somewhat the manner of a corkscrew," and went back to telephone for men and cables, still hoping the wind would die.

At about eleven o'clock a section of the deck 600 feet long tore loose from its cables, turned over in the air and fell 190 feet into the water. The rest of the main span went ten minutes later, piece by piece, and Coatsworth's car and the dog went with it. The part of the bridge under Farquharson's feet dropped thirty feet when the weight came off the cables. He fell and broke one of his cameras, knelt on the roadway, and finished the film. At 11:10 it was over. He was the last man off the bridge. Nobody had died but Tubby.

## What the experts found

The federal government appointed three experts to find out why: Othmar Ammann, who had built the George Washington Bridge in New York; Theodore von Kármán, the leading expert on how air flows round things; and Glenn Woodruff, a bridge engineer from San Francisco. They visited the wreck, studied the four months of readings, tested models in the wind tunnel at von Kármán's laboratory, and reported in March 1941.

They found that nobody had made a mistake, in the usual sense. The bridge had been designed properly for all the forces engineers then designed for, including a steady wind of 100 miles an hour pushing sideways. The steel was good and the work was good. What nobody had designed for was a wind that made the bridge move, and then fed its movement. The bridge was so narrow and so bendy that, once it started to twist, a wind of only about 25 miles an hour could pump energy into the twist faster than the bridge could soak it up. The report said plainly that even if the cable band had never slipped, the bridge "would have failed under wind velocities far below that of 100 m.p.h. for which it was designed."

The experts also explained why the readings had never shown the twist. The diagonal cables at the middle of the bridge, put in to cure the up-and-down waves, had been holding the two edges of the deck in step. The twist could not start until something gave way. On November 7th the steel band holding those cables slipped, the two edges were free to move against each other, and the twist began. Until that morning there had been no twist to measure.

And they found one more thing. The wooden vanes that were about to be fitted would have helped with the up-and-down waves. But in the wind tunnel, a deck with vanes started to twist at a lower wind speed than a deck without them. The cure the engineers were about to try was a cure for the wrong motion.

## After

The towers and the cables were cut up for scrap. Eldridge, who said the state had been forced to accept a design it thought unsound, went to Guam to build for the Navy and spent the war in a Japanese prison camp. Moisseiff died in 1943. Farquharson got a new laboratory and spent ten years testing bridges in a wind tunnel.

A new bridge was built on the old piers and opened on October 14th 1950. It is very like the one Eldridge first drew: four lanes wide, with open steel trusses 33 feet deep along its edges, and slots in the roadway to let the wind through. It is still there. Since 1940, every big suspension bridge in the world has been tested in a wind tunnel before it is built.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**An instrument only answers the question it was built to ask.** The targets on the lamp posts measured how far one side of the deck went up and down. They did that perfectly, 250 times. They could not see a twist, because a twist was not what they were pointed at. Four months of readings that never showed a twist did not mean the bridge could not twist. It only meant it had not twisted yet.

**A model is only as good as the forces you put into it.** The big model with the electromagnets copied the real bridge's waves exactly. But magnets are not wind. The model showed what the bridge did when it was pushed, not what the air did to the bridge. The wind tunnel, which did blow real air, came only five weeks before the end, and even then the wind could only blow straight at the model.

**Fixing the problem you can see can make the problem you cannot see worse.** The engineers were one day from fitting vanes to cure the bouncing. The experts found afterwards that the vanes would have made the twisting start sooner. Nobody could have known that, because nobody had measured twisting. That is exactly why it mattered.

**A famous name is not a check.** Three sets of engineers saw that the bridge was narrower than any ever built, said so in writing, and then trusted that Moisseiff knew best. The government lender's own expert suggested making it wider, and then said it was probably fine. Nobody ever actually worked out whether a deck this thin would stand the wind. They worked out that the man who drew it was very good.

So the lesson is simple. When you measure something to find out whether it is safe, ask what your instrument cannot see, because the danger may be hiding exactly there.
