## A man in London

On June 22nd 1972 a scientist from America stood up to give a talk at the Royal Society of Medicine in London. His name was John Calhoun, and he worked for the United States government at the National Institute of Mental Health. The first thing he said was strange. "I shall largely speak of mice, but my thoughts are on man."

On that same day, in a loft above a metal building on a farm in Maryland, the mice he was talking about were counted. There were 122 of them. Four years earlier there had been eight. Two and a half years earlier there had been 2,200. For more than two years no baby mouse had lived. Nobody had taken the mice away and nothing had got in. They had all the food and water they could want, and they were dying out.

When Calhoun began to say what this meant for people, the man in charge of the meeting stopped him and told him to be careful. He had been talking about mice.

The mouse pen was called Universe 25. Since then its story has been told again and again, nearly always the same way: a scientist built a paradise for mice, filled it with mice, and watched it go wrong, and the moral is that the same thing will happen to people in crowded cities. This is the story of the other experiment, the one that was actually measured. Its instrument was a count of mice, taken by hand, which added up to about a million separate observations. The question is what happened to them.

## Who ran it

Calhoun was born in Tennessee in 1917 and spent his boyhood collecting birds' eggs. He became an ecologist, which is a scientist who studies how living things fit into the places they live. In 1946 he went to work in Baltimore on a project to get rid of the city's rats, and found something odd. Poison killed most of the rats on a block, but within a few years there were exactly as many as before. Something besides food was deciding how many rats a block could hold.

To find out what, he built a pen in the woods behind his house, put five pairs of wild rats in it with all the food they wanted, and watched them for over two years from a tower. The pen was big enough for about five thousand rats. The number stopped at about two hundred. Later, working for the National Institute of Mental Health, he ran rat experiments in a barn at Rockville, Maryland, and wrote about them in a magazine in 1962. He invented a phrase there, "the behavioural sink," for what happened when rats got into the habit of eating only where other rats were eating and crowded into one pen while the others stood empty. The rats are a story of their own, told in "[Rat city](/deeper/a-million-observations/rat-city/#young)".

By 1968 Calhoun had a new laboratory on a farm near Poolesville, Maryland, which the Institute called its Animal Center. He did not work alone. A colleague called Halsey Marsden ran some of the experiments. Two chemists, Julius Axelrod and Larry Ng, measured the chemicals of stress in the mice's bodies. And there were the people who caught every mouse at every count and wrote down what they saw, whose names are not recorded anywhere.

## What they were trying to find out

In the wild, animals die of five things: they are driven out, they run short of food, the weather kills them, they get sick, or something eats them. Calhoun's idea was to build a place where none of those five things could happen, and see what, if anything, would stop the population from growing. He called it "a Utopian environment constructed for mice." A utopia is a perfect place.

He had two guesses that could be tested by counting. The first was that the bigger the pen, the less crowded it would be when the population stopped growing. The second was that the bigger the pen, the slower the population would start. He built five pens of different sizes to test them and put the same eight mice into each. The first guess turned out to be wrong. The second turned out to be right. Almost nobody has ever mentioned either.

He had a third idea too, which no count in a pen could test: that whatever happened to the mice would happen to people.

## What they built

The pen was a square of metal walls about two and a half metres on a side and a metre and a third high. Mice could climb the lower part of the walls but not the smooth strip at the top. Each wall was split into four sections, sixteen in all. In every section four wire tunnels ran up the wall, and from each tunnel four doors led into nest boxes, 256 boxes altogether. Calhoun called them "walk-up one-room apartments." Fifteen mice could sleep in one box, so there was room for 3,840 mice. Each section had a food hopper that 25 mice could eat from at once, enough to feed 9,500, and water enough for 6,000. There was no disease and no predator.

On July 9th 1968, four pairs of young white mice were put in.

The instrument was the count. Every mouse was marked with its own pattern of dye colours, and every part of the pen had a code. At regular intervals every mouse was caught, identified, looked over, and written down. By the autumn of 1970 that had added up to more than half a million observations. By 1972 Calhoun said it was about a million. A mouse became a fact when somebody held it in a glove and read its colours.

## Day 315

For the first 104 days no mice were born; the eight fought until they had settled who lived where. Then the babies came, and the number of mice doubled every 55 days: about 20, then 40, 80, 160, 320, and 620. On day 315 the doubling suddenly slowed right down, to once every 145 days.

The pen was not full. Four out of five nest boxes were empty. What had filled up, Calhoun thought, was something else. The mice had sorted themselves into fourteen groups, each with one strong male, his females and their young, each holding a stretch of wall. The group in the south-west corner had had 111 babies. The group in the opposite corner had had 13. The fourteen groups held about 150 grown mice, and Calhoun decided that 150 was the number of places the pen had for grown mice to be. Around them were 470 young mice, well brought up, about to grow up into a pen with no place for them.

In the wild, young animals that find no place leave. In the pen there was nowhere to go. The young males that lost their fights gave up, stopped doing anything, and gathered in heaps in the middle of the floor. The strong males did not bother to attack them. Instead they attacked each other, in sudden bursts, and the ones attacked did not even run away. The young females that lost gave up too, but they went upstairs, into the high nest boxes that mothers did not want.

That was the second stage, from day 315 to day 560, and the worst of it happened to the mothers. The strong males, tired out from pushing the young away, defended less and less. The nursing mothers took over the fighting, and then turned on their own babies, biting them and driving them out of the nest days before they were ready. Fewer mothers got pregnant. Whole litters that were alive at one count were gone by the next. The population was still growing, slowly, because 1,600 more young were weaned in this stage. But as a society, Calhoun wrote, it was already dead.

On day 560, which was January 20th 1970, the count was 2,200. It never went higher. The last baby that lived was born on March 1st 1970. After that there were pregnancies, fewer and fewer, and none of the babies survived. The last pregnancy began about day 920, in January 1971.

## What it looked like from inside

Nobody lived inside, but people climbed in. On April 1st 1970 a television crew came to the loft and asked how many mice were in the pen. "It's between twenty-two hundred and twenty-five hundred mice, that is bodies, moving bodies," Calhoun said. "But there are really only uhh about a hundred and fifty mice in here. Real mice, that is mice who are in the social sphere. All the others have been rejected in one way or another."

He showed the camera the two kinds of rejected mice. On the floor were the males who fought each other, scarred and with their tails chewed. Hidden in the boxes were the others, the last few hundred born, who had grown up in the crowd and never learned to fight or to court. "We call them the beautiful ones," he said, "because they are so excellent physically, they are unstressed." He climbed over the wall, found one in a can of nesting paper, and lifted it out for the camera: no wounds, in perfect shape. The reporter asked whether, as far as being a mouse went, it was a nothing. "He's a total nothing."

That winter a photographer took the one picture of the pen that everybody has seen since: Calhoun crouching in the middle of the floor with a mouse in his hand, and across from him a pale heap of mice, all giving up together.

In the summer a newspaper columnist called Stewart Alsop drove out from Washington. "It was a lovely day," he wrote, "much too lovely to spend in an office. In fact, it seemed a perfect day to visit Dr. John Calhoun's mousery." Calhoun picked up two of the beautiful ones and two of the floor males and held all four in one hand, so that Alsop could see the difference: the floor males scruffy and bitten, one with half its tail gone, the beautiful ones sleek and still. In the autumn a writer for the government came, and Calhoun told her the last mice born "cannot learn. They're all washed out."

## What happened

The number went down. On day 736 there were 2,056. After that Calhoun could draw the rest of the curve ahead of time, as a dotted line, because nothing new was being born to change it. On the day of the London talk there were 122. On November 13th 1972, when he finished writing his paper, there were 27: 23 females and 4 males, the youngest of them nearly three years old. From the rate at which they were dying he worked out that the last male would die on May 23rd 1973.

The pen was not the whole experiment. A smaller pen had run beside it, and Calhoun opened up 148 of its females when they were nearly a year old. Four out of five had never been pregnant. Marsden took small groups of mice out of the crowded pens and put them in empty ones, with plenty of room and with healthy partners that had grown up uncrowded. They did not recover. They had never learned how to be mice, and it was too late to learn. And the four smaller pens all did the same thing: every one grew, stopped, and died out, and in every one the last half of the mice born never learned to fight, court or look after young. That was the real test, and it is the only time the experiment was ever repeated.

Calhoun's paper came out in January 1973 under the title "Death Squared." In it he took the step the chairman had warned him against: "For an animal so complex as man, there is no logical reason why a comparable sequence of events should not also lead to species extinction." But in the discussion afterwards he said something else about his own work, which is worth remembering. It was not a proper balanced experiment, he said. "It was observation and reconstruction of a process; it was historical."

## After

The story about people had been written before the pen was built. Calhoun's rats had been in a magazine since 1962, and in 1968 the writer Tom Wolfe had used "the behavioural sink" as the title of a chapter about New York. What the mice added was a photograph, a number, and a name. Alsop's column asked whether young people who dropped out of society were "the beautiful ones, already." A magazine said that people might "face a psychic fate similar to the one that befell Dr John Calhoun's white mice." In 1972 a television crew came back to film the last survivors: "The result was chilling."

Scientists went the other way. The people in the room in London had objected on the day. Within a few years the scientists who studied real crowded cities had found that crowded people did not behave like crowded mice, and they stopped quoting Calhoun.

Calhoun himself read the experiment a third way. He never thought it showed that people were doomed. He thought new ideas came from people under pressure, and that the human answer to crowding was not fewer people but more ideas and better ways of sharing them. "If we try to make everybody totally happy," he said in 1970, "we'll destroy mankind." His programme was ended in 1983, and he died in 1995, aged seventy-eight.

And the million observations? They were put on magnetic tape for analysis, and no fuller analysis was ever published. His papers, 170 boxes of them, are in the National Library of Medicine, where anyone can read them. In 2024 a biologist who did reported that nothing like the behavioural sink or the beautiful ones has ever been seen in wild animals of any kind, and that textbooks no longer mention the mice at all.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**The names travelled and the numbers stayed home.** Calhoun gave his discoveries names that people would remember: the behavioural sink, the beautiful ones, walk-up apartments. The names got into magazines, the Senate, and a children's book. The numbers, a million of them, got onto a tape. An experiment has two results, the measurements and the story, and the story is the one that gets passed on. If you name something for the public, expect the name to become the result.

**The experiment measured what happens when animals cannot leave.** In the wild, young mice with no place to go would have left and died somewhere else, and nobody would have counted them. The pen kept them in, so for once they could be seen: heaps on the floor, and hidden in the top boxes. That is what a closed box can show. But a city is not a closed box. Cities have doors. The people in London said so on the day, and the story everyone tells leaves that out.

**The part that was tested is the part nobody remembers.** Does a bigger pen mean less crowding in the end? No. Does it mean a slower start? Yes. Five pens were built to find this out, and they did. Nobody quotes it. The claim about people was never tested, could not be tested in a pen, and is the only thing most people know about the experiment.

**Everyone read the result they came for.** The public read a warning about cities. The scientists read a man going too far, and stopped reading. Calhoun read a recipe for saving the world. The only thing that could have settled it was the counts themselves, and nobody published them.

**Check the story before you learn from it.** The peak is 2,200 in the paper and "between twenty-two hundred and twenty-five hundred" on television. The last mouse died on May 23rd 1973 in every account you will find, but in Calhoun's paper that date is a guess, worked out from the death rate when 27 mice were left, and no record says what day it really happened.

So the lesson is simple. When someone tells you what an experiment proved, ask what was actually counted, and whether anyone ever read the count.
