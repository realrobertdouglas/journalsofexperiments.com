## The boy in the orchard

One summer day in 1784, in a garden at Passy, just outside Paris, a boy of about twelve was led out of a house with a thick bandage over his eyes. The bandage had been specially made so that no light could get in at all. The boy was taken to a tree and told to hug it for two minutes. Then to a second tree, a third and a fourth.

At the first tree he said he was sweating. He coughed and said his head hurt a little. At the second he felt dizzy. At the third the dizziness and the headache got worse, and he said he thought he was getting close to the tree. At the fourth tree he fainted. His arms and legs went stiff, and he was carried to the grass, where the doctor who had brought him ran over and brought him round.

The doctor's name was Charles d'Eslon. Before the boy came out, d'Eslon had gone alone to the far end of the orchard and "magnetised" an apricot tree that stood by itself. He believed that an invisible fluid flowed out of his hands and his iron rod, into the tree, and that anyone who touched the tree would feel it. But none of the four trees the boy hugged was the apricot tree. The fourth one, where he fainted, was about twenty-four feet away from it.

The men watching were a committee appointed by the King of France. The house belonged to Benjamin Franklin, who was seventy-eight and too ill to travel into Paris, so the experiments came to him. The committee wrote afterwards that the result "is entirely contrary to the theory of the animal magnetism."

This is the story of that committee, of the blindfold, and of what happened to what they found out.

## What animal magnetism was

In 1778 a doctor from Vienna called Franz Anton Mesmer came to Paris. He said he had discovered an invisible fluid that filled the whole universe, flowed through every living body, and could be sent from one person to another. He called it "animal magnetism". When the fluid was blocked, he said, people got ill. When a trained person set it flowing again, they got better. Mesmer said this fluid could cure almost anything.

Rich people in Paris flocked to him. In his treatment rooms stood a big round wooden tub, called a baquet, with iron rods sticking out of the lid. Patients sat in rows around it, each holding a rod against the part of them that hurt, joined together by a cord. Someone played a piano. Men walked round with iron wands, pointing them at the patients and pressing their hands on their stomachs. After an hour or two, people began to have what Mesmer called "crises": they shook, cried out, coughed up water, or fainted. Once one person started, the others followed. The committee later wrote that "he that has not had it can have no idea of it".

Mesmer's most important pupil was d'Eslon, a doctor of the Paris medical school and physician to the king's brother. He ran a treatment of his own and taught the method to many other doctors. In 1784 he asked the government to examine it. Mesmer was furious and refused to take part. So the committee examined animal magnetism as d'Eslon practised it, not as Mesmer did, and said so on the first page of their report.

## The committee

On March 12th 1784 the king named four doctors to look into it, one of them Joseph-Ignace Guillotin, whose name was later given to the guillotine. They asked for help from the Royal Academy of Sciences, and the king added five scientists, among them Franklin, famous for his work on electricity, Antoine Lavoisier, the greatest chemist of the age, and Jean-Sylvain Bailly, an astronomer. A second committee, from the Royal Society of Medicine, worked at the same time.

Franklin had already made up his mind what the problem was. In March a doctor had written to ask what he thought of Mesmer's cures. Franklin answered that he could not believe in the fluid "till I can see or feel some Effect of it", and that many illnesses get better on their own, while people are very ready to fool themselves and each other.

Bailly wrote the report. Lavoisier, as far as we can tell from the notes he left, designed the experiments.

## The plan

The committee began by deciding what question to ask. Mesmer's claim was really two claims: that the fluid existed, and that it cured people. They decided to test the first one first. As they put it, the fluid "may indeed exist without being useful, but it cannot be useful if it do not exist."

Then they made a decision that surprised d'Eslon. They refused to count cures as evidence. D'Eslon wanted them to follow his patients and watch them recover. The committee said no, because people recover all the time, with or without treatment, and you can never be sure what did it.

Lavoisier wrote down the plan before the big experiments. The crises might be caused by the fluid, he said, or by the patient's own imagination: the belief that something was being done to them. The only way to find out was to separate the two, by doing two kinds of experiment: magnetising people without their knowing it, and telling people they were being magnetised when they were not. And he added a rule: "a single conclusive experiment is more precious than a great number of others which do not go directly to the end." You can read his whole plan in the companion piece "[Lavoisier's plan](/deeper/twenty-four-feet/lavoisiers-plan/#young)".

## Building up to the tree

First the committee tried it on themselves. Once a week they went to d'Eslon's house, sat round a private tub for two and a half hours, and let themselves be magnetised. Not one of them felt anything. Then they had seven poor sick people brought to Franklin's house and magnetised in front of them. Four felt nothing; three felt a little. Then d'Eslon magnetised Franklin himself, two of his relatives, his secretary and an American officer who had a fever. None of them felt anything.

The committee noticed something. The only people who felt anything were poor people who were being paid to come and who probably wanted to please a room full of gentlemen. A sick child of six felt nothing. A girl of nine who had fits all the time felt nothing. So the committee had a suspicion: perhaps the effects came from what people expected to feel.

To test that, they needed the blindfold. The report describes it as carefully as a scientific instrument: two cups of rubber filled with eiderdown, sewn into cloth, tied at the back, letting the person breathe freely but letting in not "the smallest particle of light".

One of the first people they blindfolded was a woman who worked in a doctor's house and who felt heat wherever a magnetiser's hand went. Blindfolded, she felt heat in the wrong places. Then the magnetiser was quietly sent out of the room, and she was told the treatment was going on. She felt exactly the same heat, though nobody was doing anything. The committee wrote that the answers people gave "were determined by the questions that were put to them". A learned gentleman, blindfolded and told the treatment had been going for ten minutes before it had even started, said he felt a heat in his back like a stove.

## The tree, and after the tree

None of those people had had a crisis, though. Could belief alone cause one? That is why the committee asked d'Eslon to magnetise a tree. He agreed, and said it would work if the person was sensitive enough. So the committee let him choose the person, and he chose the boy. They let him magnetise the tree alone. They would have liked him to leave, but he said it would not work unless he pointed his cane at the tree from a distance, so they let him stay, far off, with committee members standing between him and the boy so that he could not signal.

The boy fainted at the wrong tree. D'Eslon said that all trees are a little magnetic and that his being nearby had made them more so. The committee answered that if that were true, a sensitive person could not take a walk in a garden without having a fit. And they pointed out the most important thing: if the boy had felt nothing at all, even at the right tree, d'Eslon could have said he was just not sensitive that day. But he had a full crisis at a tree nobody had touched. The crisis came from his own mind.

They did it again, at Franklin's house, with two of d'Eslon's most sensitive patients, both women, while d'Eslon was in another room. One was blindfolded and told d'Eslon had come in to treat her. Three committee members sat with her and talked to the empty chair as if he were there. Nobody touched her. In three minutes she was shaking; then she went stiff, clapped her hands, jumped up and stamped her feet. The other woman was not blindfolded, but was sat facing a closed door and told d'Eslon was magnetising her through it. In three minutes she had a full crisis and bit her own hand so hard that the tooth marks showed.

The last experiment was the cleverest. A doorway between two rooms was covered with paper. The second woman, a seamstress, was sent for on the pretence that a lady wanted some sewing done, and the only chair in the room stood in the papered doorway. On the other side, without her knowing, a committee member who was a doctor magnetised her for half an hour exactly as d'Eslon taught. She chatted happily and said she felt fine. Then he came round into her room and magnetised her openly for twelve minutes, but on purpose did it the wrong way, the way that the theory said could not possibly work. In three minutes she was gasping, and by twelve she had a full crisis. Then he told her it was time to stop, and the crisis faded away in three minutes.

The committee wrote: "the imagination singly produces all the effects attributed to the magnetism, and that, where the imagination ceases to be called forth, it has no longer the smallest efficacy." The whole series is told in the companion piece "[The other blindfolds](/deeper/twenty-four-feet/the-other-blindfolds/#young)".

## What the report said

The committee signed its report in Paris on August 11th 1784. It said the causes of everything they had seen were pressing on the body, imagination, and imitation, which means people copying each other. "This agent, this fluid, has no existence." Their last sentence sums up the method: they had shown "that the imagination without the magnetism produces convulsions, and that the magnetism without the imagination produces nothing".

Then they said something else. They had just proved that imagination was powerful enough to throw people into fits and bring them out again. They called it "that active and terrible power". But instead of saying this was worth studying, they said it was dangerous. Fits were bad for people, they said, and should never be caused on purpose, and the treatments should stop.

There was also a second report, which was kept secret and written for the king alone. It said that nearly all the patients who had crises were women and nearly all the magnetisers were men, that the treatment was a danger to morals, that some of the crises were probably faked to get the others going, and that nobody was really being cured. The chief of police had asked d'Eslon, in front of the committee, whether it would be easy to take advantage of a woman in a crisis, and d'Eslon had said yes. None of this was printed for forty years. You can read about it in the companion piece "[The secret report](/deeper/twenty-four-feet/the-secret-report/#young)".

One examiner refused to sign. Antoine-Laurent de Jussieu, a botanist on the second committee, had spent more hours in d'Eslon's rooms than anyone, trying the method himself. He agreed that imagination did most of it. But he thought he had seen a few things it did not explain, and he thought thirteen men had judged too fast on too few experiments. He is the subject of the companion piece "[The one who did not sign](/deeper/twenty-four-feet/the-one-who-did-not-sign/#young)".

## What happened next

Franklin wrote to his grandson two weeks after the report came out: "Some think it will put an End to Mesmerism. But there is a wonderful deal of Credulity in the World, and Deceptions as absurd, have supported themselves for Ages." He was right.

D'Eslon wrote a reply. He said the committee had been too hasty to decide so much from one boy at one tree, and that they should have repeated it. That was a fair point. He also said the practice could not be stopped now, because Mesmer had trained 300 pupils and he himself had trained 160 doctors. Mesmer complained to the law courts. Cartoonists drew the committee waving their report while the magnetisers flew away on broomsticks.

But the practice did not stop. It changed. Within a year, magnetisers had found that instead of giving people fits they could put them into a kind of sleep, in which they would talk and obey. Franklin wrote to a friend in 1785 that there was "now a fresh Folly", the magnetic sleep, which crowds went to see every day. Forty years later another committee of French doctors looked at the sleepers and said they could read with their eyes shut. An American doctor who reprinted Franklin's report in 1837 pointed out why: the new committee had not covered the sleepers' eyes "with the same caution" as the king's committee had. The blindfold had to be invented all over again, and each time it gave the same answer.

Franklin went home to America in 1785 and died in 1790. D'Eslon died in 1786. Bailly and Lavoisier were both executed in the French Revolution, by the machine that carried Guillotin's name.

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**If you want to know whether something works, you have to separate it from people's belief in it.** In the treatment rooms, everything happened at once: the tub, the music, the hands, the crowd, and the expectation that something would happen. Nobody could tell what was doing what. The committee took it all apart. They magnetised a woman secretly and nothing happened. They pretended to magnetise her and she had a fit. Those two results together told them more than a thousand fits around the tub.

**Decide in advance what each result will mean.** Lavoisier wrote down the plan before the experiments. The committee knew that if the boy felt nothing at the right tree, d'Eslon could say he was not sensitive that day. So they arranged things so that the boy could fail in a way that could not be explained away, and he did. A test is only a test if you have agreed beforehand what failing it would look like.

**Do it more than once.** One boy at one tree on one afternoon is not much. The committee did repeat the idea in other ways, and every time it came out the same. But d'Eslon was right that the tree itself should have been done again. Even a result that looks completely clear should be checked.

**Do not throw away a real finding because it came from the wrong place.** The committee proved that belief alone could make people shake, faint and recover, in minutes, on a word. That was a real discovery about people. Instead of studying it, they called it dangerous and told everyone to stop. The magnetisers kept using it for another hundred years, because it worked, even though their explanation for why it worked was wrong.

**Answering one question is not answering all of them.** The committee asked whether the fluid existed. The patients wanted to know whether they felt better. Some of them did, and even the second committee admitted it: they had hope, they took exercise, and they stopped taking their medicines. Nobody tested that.

So the lesson is simple. The committee built the right instrument, a blindfold, and read it correctly: there was no fluid. But they put the reading away and did nothing with the thing it had actually found, and so the practice went on for a century without them.
