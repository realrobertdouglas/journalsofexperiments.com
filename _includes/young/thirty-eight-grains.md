## A test in a glass

In August 1854 a London doctor named John Snow was walking the streets south of the River Thames with small glass bottles in his pockets. At each house where someone had died of cholera, he asked for a little of the water from the tap, wrote the address on the bottle, and took it home. At home he added a few drops of a chemical called nitrate of silver. If the water had salt in it, the chemical made a white cloud. One kind of water made a small cloud. The other made a cloud so thick that, he said, the two "could be at once distinguished without any further trouble."

The water with almost no salt came from the Lambeth Company, which pumped it out of the Thames far upstream at a village called Thames Ditton, above the reach of the tide and of the sewers. The water with a lot of salt came from the Southwark and Vauxhall Company, which pumped it at Battersea, in the middle of London, where the tide pushed sea water up the river and where the sewers of two million people emptied. A gallon of the Ditton water held about one grain of salt. A gallon of the Battersea water held about thirty-eight. That is why this story is called Thirty-Eight Grains.

The bottles were not the experiment. The experiment had been running for two years over about three hundred thousand people, and nobody had planned it. The bottles were how one man read the answer.

## Who ran it

John Snow was born in York in 1813, the son of a farmer. At fourteen he was sent to Newcastle to learn to be a surgeon, and when cholera reached the coal mines near Newcastle in 1831 he was sent, at eighteen, to look after the sick miners. In 1836 he walked most of the way from Liverpool to London to study medicine there. By 1854 he was a well-known London doctor. He had given chloroform, the new drug that sends people to sleep for operations, to Queen Victoria when her son Prince Leopold was born.

In 1849 Snow had written a short book saying that cholera was not caught by breathing bad air, as almost every doctor believed. He said it was caught by swallowing something that came out of a cholera patient's body, and that one way it travelled was in drinking water. Very few people believed him.

Three other people matter in this story. John Joseph Whiting was a medical man whom Snow paid to help him knock on doors. William Farr ran the statistics at the General Register Office, where every death in England was recorded, and he wrote a weekly report on deaths in London. John Simon was the chief medical officer of the General Board of Health, the government body in charge of public health.

## Two pipes in one street

In the 1830s and 1840s the two water companies south of the river had fought each other for customers. Each had laid its pipes down the same streets, and salesmen from both had knocked on the same doors. So in a big part of south London, in Lambeth, Southwark and Newington, one house took its water from one company and the house next door took it from the other. Rich houses and poor houses, big and small, were mixed up the same way. Which company a house had depended on what a landlord had decided twenty years earlier.

Until 1852 both companies pumped dirty water from the middle of London. Then the Lambeth Company moved its pumps upstream to Thames Ditton, where the river was clean. The Southwark and Vauxhall Company stayed at Battersea. A new law said it had to move by 1855, and it had promised to be ready by September 1854. It was not.

Snow saw what this meant. Three hundred thousand people, in his words, "were divided into two groups without their choice, and, in most cases, without their knowledge." One group drank water with London's sewage in it. The other drank clean water. They lived in the same streets, breathed the same air and earned the same wages. If the clean-water houses had fewer cholera deaths, the water had to be the reason, because nothing else was different. "No experiment could have been devised," Snow wrote, "which would more thoroughly test the effect of water supply on the progress of cholera than this, which circumstances placed ready made before the observer."

All he had to do was find out, for every house where someone died of cholera, which company's pipe came into it.

## Knocking on doors

That turned out to be hard. Cholera came back to London in July 1854. Snow got the addresses of the dead from Farr's office and began, in the middle of August, in a part of Lambeth called Kennington. At most houses nobody knew which company they paid. Poor families paid rent each week to a landlord, and the landlord paid the water bill. Even people who paid it themselves had to go and find the receipt.

So Snow used the salt. When nobody could tell him, he took a bottle of water home and tested it. He also learned at what time of day each company's men turned the water on in each street, and what the water looked like as it came in. But he only trusted the test and the receipt.

The first forty-four deaths in Kennington told the story at once. Thirty-eight of the houses had the Battersea water. Four had the Ditton water. Two had their own wells. Snow took the figures to Farr, who was "much struck," and Farr ordered every registrar in south London to ask the same question about every cholera death from August 26th onwards.

There was one more problem. Knowing how many deaths happened in each company's houses was not enough. He needed to know how many houses each company had, because if one company had ten times as many houses, it would naturally have ten times as many deaths. A report to Parliament gave the totals: about 40,000 houses for the Southwark and Vauxhall Company and about 26,000 for the Lambeth Company. But it did not say how many were in each district. So the inquiry had to cover every district either company reached. Snow took all the mixed streets himself. Whiting took the districts that had only Battersea water, to find out whether each house used the company's water, a well, or a bucket dipped in the river.

## Fourteen to one

In the first four weeks of the epidemic, from July 8th to August 5th 1854, there were 334 cholera deaths in the districts the two companies supplied. In 286 of the houses the water came from Battersea. In only 14 it came from Thames Ditton. The rest used wells, ditches or the river itself.

Set against the number of houses, that was 71 deaths for every 10,000 Battersea houses and 5 for every 10,000 Ditton houses. Cholera was, Snow wrote, "fourteen times as fatal" in the one group as in the other.

Something else stood out. In those four weeks only 563 people died of cholera in the whole of London, a city of more than two million. More than half of them were customers of one water company. And the Lambeth houses, standing next door to the worst houses in London, had fewer deaths than the rest of the city put together.

By August 26th, after seven weeks, there had been about 1,260 deaths in Battersea houses and 98 in Ditton houses: 315 and 37 for every 10,000 houses. Then Farr's registrars took over. In the next seven weeks they counted about 2,350 deaths in Battersea houses and about 300 in Ditton houses. The gap narrowed as the epidemic went on, because once cholera was in a street it could spread from house to house by dirty hands and food. But it never closed. Over the whole fourteen weeks, out of every 10,000 people with Battersea water, 153 died. Out of every 10,000 with Ditton water, 26 died.

Snow printed the names and addresses of all 334 deaths from the first four weeks at the back of his book, so that anyone who doubted him could go round and check.

## The pump

In the middle of all this, on September 1st 1854, cholera broke out suddenly in the streets around Golden Square, on the north side of the river, far from the Battersea water. Snow left his south London work for two weeks. He found that nearly all the dead had drunk from one street pump in Broad Street. On the evening of September 7th he told the parish officers so, and the next day the pump's handle was taken off. That is the story everyone remembers. Snow himself admitted that the outbreak was already dying down before the handle came off, so the pump could not prove what the pipes proved. The companion piece "[The pump](/deeper/thirty-eight-grains/the-pump/#young)" tells that story.

## What the officials said

Farr printed Snow's results in his weekly report in October 1854, and by December he had added his own. The registrars had not been able to find out the water supply in about one case in five. But in every district and every week, he wrote, "the balance of mortality is heaviest in every district and in every week against the impure water, to an extent that leaves little room for doubt on the mind." He worked out that nearly two thousand people in the Battersea houses had died who would have lived if cholera had been only as deadly there as in the Ditton houses.

Snow's big book came out early in 1855. It cost him more than two hundred pounds to publish and earned him, his friend said, scarcely as many shillings. The leading medical journal, *The Lancet*, was angry with him that summer for a different reason. He had told Parliament that the smell of boiling bones did not cause disease. The journal said he had "fallen down through a gully-hole and has never since been able to get out again." Its joke was that in Snow's view you could smell sewage and breathe it fearlessly, "but don't eat it."

The Board of Health's scientific committee reported in July 1855. It agreed that the Battersea water was foul, full of sewage, and that this "may have exercised great influence" on the cholera. But the committee believed that cholera was something in the air that turned dirt, in air or in water, into poison. Bad water made things worse, in its view. It did not carry the disease. The committee also said about the Broad Street pump: "we see no reason to adopt this belief."

## Simon counts again

In May 1856 John Simon, the Board's medical officer, finished the job that Snow had asked for. The Board had got from the companies a list of every house each one supplied, street by street. Its clerks matched every cholera death in nine south London districts against the lists, for the epidemic of 1849 as well as for 1854.

The numbers came out even more clearly than Snow's. In the Lambeth Company's houses, about 167,000 people, there had been 611 deaths in 1854: 37 for every 10,000 people. In the Southwark and Vauxhall houses, about 268,000 people, there had been 3,476: 130 for every 10,000. Simon picked forty-five streets where the two companies' pipes ran side by side and each supplied exactly half the houses. The Ditton half lost 57 people. The Battersea half lost 164.

Then he looked back to 1849, when both companies had pumped dirty water. In those same houses, the Lambeth Company's customers had died at about the same rate as their neighbours. In 1854, after the move to Thames Ditton, their death rate had fallen to a third of what it was. The other company's had gone up. "And what was the only discoverable difference," Simon asked, "between these two populations?" One had cleaned up its water. The other had not.

Simon called it "an experiment, at which mankind would have shuddered," carried out on half a million people. He worked out that two-thirds of the people who died in the Battersea houses would have lived with the other water. And then he said that this did not settle whether foul water or foul air was the bigger cause, and that both "equally may breed and convey the poison." He had measured the pipe to the last house and still kept the air.

By then the experiment was over anyway. The Southwark and Vauxhall Company had finally moved its pumps, and both companies were sending the same clean water down the same streets.

## After

Snow answered Simon in October 1856, using Simon's own house counts, and got the same answer again: more than five deaths in Battersea houses for every one in Ditton houses. Then he went back to his work on chloroform. On June 10th 1858 he had a stroke at his desk, and he died on June 16th, aged forty-five. A friend wrote to *The Lancet* that no doctor since Jenner, who invented vaccination, had done more for mankind. In the same letter he added that Snow might sometimes have "inferred too much from the facts."

It took another thirty years, and the discovery of the cholera germ under a microscope, before everyone accepted what the pipes had shown in 1854. A professor in Munich named Pettenkofer never did accept it; his story is told in "[One Cubic Centimetre](/experiments/2026/10/19/one-cubic-centimetre.html#young)".

## What it teaches

What does this story teach? A few plain things.

**Sometimes the experiment is already running.** Nobody set up this test. Two water companies had fought for customers and mixed up their pipes, and then one of them moved its pumps. That accident sorted three hundred thousand people into two groups as fairly as a lottery would have. Snow's cleverness was not in building anything. It was in seeing that the test was there and going out to read it.

**A measurement has to be made by somebody.** The deaths were written down at the Register Office. The houses were written down at the water companies. But nobody had put the two lists together, because at most doors nobody knew which company's pipe came in. Snow had to find out, house by house, with a receipt or a bottle of water. When the registrars were told to ask the same question, they could not get an answer one time in five.

**A count is not a rate.** Two hundred and eighty-six deaths against fourteen sounds huge, but it means nothing until you know how many houses each company had. Snow needed the number of houses to turn the deaths into a rate, and he had to stretch his inquiry across all of south London to make that honest.

**Numbers do not decide what they mean.** Farr, Simon and Snow all agreed on the figures. Farr said they left "little room for doubt." Simon called them an experiment mankind would shudder at. And still Simon and the Board of Health said that bad air was just as much to blame, because that was the theory they already held. The numbers were never argued with. They were simply not allowed to change anyone's mind.

So the lesson is simple. Look for the experiment that the world has already run for you, go and read it carefully at the door, and then remember that even a clear answer will not be believed by people who have already decided what the answer should look like.
