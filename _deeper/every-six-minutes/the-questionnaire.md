Roland Radloff opened his section of the Tektite II report with a truism and a complaint. "One of the most widely accepted truisms in psychology is that, 'The best predictor of future behavior is past behavior.'" High school grades predict college grades; past income predicts success at selling life insurance; finishing high school predicts finishing Navy service school. In Sealab II in 1965, he and Robert Helmreich had found that facts about the divers' lives predicted how they performed on the bottom, "especially in contrast with personality and interest inventory data," which predicted almost nothing. And yet, he wrote, nobody had built the instrument. Buros's *Mental Measurements Yearbook* of 1965 listed 196 personality tests and reviewed 125 of them; the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory alone had a bibliography of nearly 1,400 references by 1964. The yearbook listed a few biographical forms, mostly interview sheets for counsellors, reviewed none of them, and gave no reliability or validity figures for any. Biographical data, Radloff wrote, "have been used because they work," one or two items at a time, by investigators who never asked why. Birth order had been studied in hundreds of papers since 1959, nearly always on its own, with no understanding of what it measured.

So for Tektite II he wrote his own. The Life History Questionnaire asked about the first nineteen years of a life: where the person had lived and in what kind of house, how big the hometown was and how often the family moved, the size and shape of the family, the quality of the food and clothing, what the father and mother did, height and weight and health, the kind and size of school and how the person did there, honours in athletics and in schoolwork, church, nights out, dating, fights with other children, clashes with authority, praise and criticism and affection and punishment from parents, work in term time and in the summers, and when the person had first earned his own money. There were thirty-two questions. Twelve of them were answered nineteen times, once for each year of life, and the other twenty for the years that applied, so that a single answer sheet held, in principle, a matrix of thirty-two rows by nineteen columns. The questions asked what had happened, not what the person felt about it: "In what size community did you live?" rather than "In what size city would you prefer to live?"; "How often did your parents punish you?" rather than "How strict do you feel your parents were?" Wherever possible the answers were counts, once a year, once a month, once a week, daily, rather than "seldom" and "often," because "one man's 'frequently' is another's 'seldom'." Most questions had nine factual answers and a tenth, "Don't Remember." Many of the question stems ran to two or three hundred words, because the authors had found in interviews that short questions were misunderstood. "Persons are asked what happened," Radloff wrote, "not what they wished had happened, or hope will happen."

Roger Bakeman at Texas wrote a programme called LIHAN, the Life History Analyzer, to turn the matrix into variables: the mean of an answer over all nineteen years, or the number of times it changed, or the difference between one span of years and another. It was table-driven, so that any new variable an investigator could define could be computed from the cards without touching the data again. The number of variables that could be made this way was, Radloff admitted, "almost infinite," and the authors said they had deliberately resisted the temptation to make them, and had stuck to gross ones.

## What it predicted

The criterion was the one the cards gave best: the percentage of mission time each scientist-aquanaut spent on marine science, direct and in support, punched every six minutes for the length of the mission, between 2,866 and 4,770 records for each person. No aquanaut had been selected by any psychological measure; the scientists had been chosen on their research proposals and the engineers on their skills, and the behaviour programme had wanted it so, "because of our interest in obtaining a wide range of individual differences on criterion measures."

Three sets of variables were formed in advance: seventeen "environmental" ones, such as hometown size, family size, the parents' work and the person's health; twelve "individual" ones, such as school performance, clashes with authority and financial independence; and ten "change" scores, counting how often a thing had changed. Thirteen general facts went in beside them, among them age, years of diving, the six scales of the Allport-Vernon-Lindzey Study of Values, and the intelligence score from the 16PF test.

With forty scientists, a correlation of .30 was significant at the five percent level and .39 at one percent, and the authors said so in a footnote. Of the general facts, two correlated with marine science: intelligence, at .51, which surprised them in a group drawn from the top of the range, and attendance at religious services, at minus .35. Age and years of diving experience correlated with nothing, as they had not in Sealab II, which the authors read as a sign that motivation and not experience set performance in a shallow warm habitat. Of the life history variables, those from a small hometown did more marine science, as in Sealab II; so did those from smaller families, those whose clothes had been poorer in childhood, those who had done better at school, those who had been less active in church, and those who had become financially independent earlier. The school effect was strong for the elementary years, weaker for junior high, and gone by high school, which the authors took as a lesson in favour of year-by-year questions. A count of changes in clothing quality predicted negatively: coming from a poor family helped, and coming from a family that was moving up did not, and the authors offered as "pure speculation" the hypothesis that effective scientists come from lower strata and from families that stay put.

Then they compared the ten scientists who did most marine science with the ten who did least. The two groups differed at the five percent level on a quarter of all the variables. The top ten had been in worse health between the ages of six and twelve, by a wide margin, and in no different health before or after; a hypothesis was offered that a sick child reads, and a healthy adult compensates outdoors, and the finding was christened "the Teddy Roosevelt Effect." The top ten's families had moved house more and changed their composition less. The bottom ten scored higher on the economic scale of the Study of Values, which the authors read as men who "sought more the designation 'Aquanaut' than the opportunity to conduct research in the undersea environment." And the top ten were more often first-born, which was the opposite of Sealab II, where the later-born had done better, and which the authors said they had predicted, since first-borns do better where stress is moderate and the Tektite habitat was warm, shallow and clear.

The regressions came last. Nine environmental variables, chosen from the seventeen by a selection method, gave a multiple correlation of .79 with marine science, or 62 percent of the variance; the authors printed the "shrunken" figure, .71, beside it. Five individual variables gave .69, and five change scores .54. When the criterion was changed to each scientist's deviation from the mean of his own crew, which took out the strong effect of the crew's norm, the figures rose a little, to .80, .74 and .59. "These results provide considerable evidence that the LHQ is a highly sensitive instrument."

## The divers' school

The authors knew what forty subjects and fifty predictors could do, and said that the correlations were "so strong as to raise questions about whether they might be accounted for by peculiarities in the relatively small sample." So Radloff gave the questionnaire to a class of thirty-eight Navy enlisted men at Second Class Diver's School, and took as the criterion whether each man failed, finished scuba training, or finished scuba and hard-hat diving both. The two populations differed at the five percent level on sixteen of the thirty-nine life history variables: the Tektite scientists had come from bigger towns, had more praise and affection from their parents, had done better at school and had moved more; the Navy divers "received more physical punishment, came from more homogeneous communities, went out at night and dated more, and had more clashes with authority." The same variables with the same weights, applied to the divers, predicted their completion of the course at .37, .33 and .46. "Given the great differences in the nature of the two populations," the authors wrote, "the fact that the same variables weighted in the same way predict so well in both groups attests to the overwhelming importance of a limited number of demographic variables." The conclusion of the section is that "an extremely effective predictive test has been developed which needs only refinement and validation to become generally applicable."

## What became of it

Tektite I had done the same thing by hand. Before the four men went down in 1969 each had sat for an hour and a half of structured interview about his education, his work, his family, his money, his hobbies and his sports, and the appendix reported them as a group: all four first-born sons, three of them earning money by the age of eleven on paper rounds and in a shop, all with chores from the age of six or seven, all regular at church through adolescence, all Boy Scouts, all woodworkers, one a gourmet cook. The authors drew a picture of stability and even progress, and admitted that in a group of four "a 'so what' reaction is not unexpected."

Helmreich's answer to that, in 1971, was Tektite II's forty-eight, and the cards. In 1986, writing for NASA with the space station in view, he went back over what the habitats had taught. The strong replicated findings were about environments, not persons: that real work and real rewards made the sea floor a better place to live than a laboratory room, and that crews quarrelled with mission control. On persons he wrote this: "The body of empirical data on personality effects does not provide a coherent or convincing picture. Few significant and replicated findings regarding personality influences in contained environments have emerged." He thought the fault lay in the measures rather than the idea, and had moved on to a different set of them, three components of achievement motivation that he and Janet Spence had found predicted scientists, MBA graduates and airline pilots, and to a "honeymoon effect" by which personality shows in performance only after months on the job, which would mean that a two-week mission could not have found it. The Life History Questionnaire is not mentioned.

What the questionnaire left is the method and the arithmetic. It asked for facts rather than feelings, by the year rather than by the life, in counts rather than adjectives, and that part of its design has outlived it. On forty scientists it found a set of childhood facts that explained most of the difference in how hard they worked under fifty feet of water, and on thirty-eight sailors the same set explained a smaller part of who passed a diving course. The authors called it preliminary and asked for cross-validation, and the programme that could have given it was over before the report was printed.
